How to use this notebook

Placement interviews test Computer Networks in a very predictable way. Almost every question is a variation of six things: what layer does this belong to, TCP versus UDP, what happens when you type a URL, subnetting arithmetic, how does a protocol handle failure, and can you explain this to me simply. Everything in this notebook is arranged to answer those.

A three-pass study plan

Pass 1 (one long sitting): read Parts 01–02 and the "Typing google.com" walkthrough in Part 12. Those three give you the skeleton — after them, every other topic has a place to hang.
Pass 2 (a few days): work down the layers, L1 to L7. Open every question, try to answer out loud before expanding it. Speaking the answer is the skill being tested, not recognising it.
Pass 3 (night before): Part 15 only — rapid-fire and the cheat sheet.

How the colours work

The rail on the left is the OSI stack, and its colour ramp runs from violet at Layer 1 (electrical signals on copper) to crimson at Layer 7 (what a human sees in a browser). Every section header, table accent and question block inherits its layer's colour, so as you scroll you always know how close to the wire you currently are. If a section is grey, it sits outside the stack — it is either background or synthesis.

Interview technique that costs nothing

When you are asked "explain TCP", do not begin listing header fields. Begin with one sentence of purpose ("TCP gives you a reliable, ordered byte stream over an unreliable packet network"), then structure ("it does that with sequence numbers, acknowledgements, retransmission and windowing"), then detail on request. Purpose → structure → detail. Interviewers grade the shape of your answer as much as the content.

01 · Fundamentals

What a network actually is

A computer network is a set of independent devices connected by links so they can exchange data and share resources. "Independent" matters: a CPU talking to its own RAM is not a network, because neither can survive alone. Two laptops connected by Wi-Fi are, because each is a complete computer that has chosen to cooperate.

Everything the subject cares about follows from one hard fact: the link in the middle is unreliable and shared. Wires cut, signals attenuate, packets are dropped when a router runs out of memory, two devices transmit at once and collide, and the receiver may be slower than the sender. Every protocol you will study is a specific answer to one of those failures.

The five goals of networking

Resource sharingPrinters, storage, compute and data reachable from anywhere, regardless of physical location.
ReliabilityAlternate paths and replicated files mean one failure does not stop service.
ScalabilityCapacity grows by adding machines rather than replacing one big one.
CommunicationMail, messaging, voice and video between people and processes.
Cost efficiencyMany cheap clients sharing a few expensive servers beats one mainframe per user.

Total delay has four components, and being able to name them is a common interview checkpoint:

Total delay = Transmission + Propagation + Queuing + Processing Transmission delay = Packet size (bits) / Bandwidth (bps) -> how long to push bits onto the wire Propagation delay = Distance (m) / Propagation speed (m/s) -> how long a bit takes to travel Queuing delay = time waiting in a router's output buffer -> the only variable one; causes jitter Processing delay = header check, lookup, error check at each hop
Bandwidth-delay product

BDP = bandwidth × round-trip time. It is the number of bits "in flight" in the pipe at any instant, and therefore the minimum window size a sender needs to keep the link busy. A 100 Mbps link with 40 ms RTT holds 4 Mb ≈ 500 KB of data in flight — which is exactly why TCP window scaling had to be invented.

Packet switching splits into two flavours. In the datagram approach (used by IP) each packet is routed independently and may arrive out of order. In the virtual circuit approach (ATM, MPLS, Frame Relay) a path is chosen once at setup and every packet carries a small circuit identifier and follows the same path.

Connection-oriented versus connectionless

A connection-oriented service establishes state at both ends before data flows, delivers data in order, and tears the connection down afterwards — like a phone call. TCP is the example. A connectionless service just sends each unit with a full destination address and hopes for the best — like posting a letter. UDP and IP are the examples. Connection-oriented does not automatically mean reliable (ATM is connection-oriented but not reliable), though for TCP it does.

Client–server versus peer-to-peer

In the client–server model one always-on machine with a stable address serves many clients that never talk to each other; it is simple to secure and manage, but the server is a bottleneck and a single point of failure. In peer-to-peer every node is both client and server, so capacity grows as users join (BitTorrent, blockchain networks), at the cost of much harder discovery, security and consistency. Most real systems are hybrids — BitTorrent still uses a tracker, and Skype used supernodes.

Interview questions · Fundamentals
What is a computer network and why do we need one?warm-up

A collection of autonomous computing devices connected by communication links that lets them exchange data and share resources. We need it for resource sharing, reliability through redundancy, scalability, human communication and cost efficiency — one expensive server can serve many cheap clients.

Difference between bandwidth, throughput and latency?very common

Bandwidth is the theoretical maximum rate a link supports; throughput is what you actually achieve after protocol overhead, congestion and errors; latency is the time a single bit takes to get from source to destination. A useful analogy: bandwidth is the number of lanes on a highway, throughput is the cars actually arriving per minute, latency is how long one car takes to drive the route. A high-bandwidth satellite link can still have terrible latency.

List the components of end-to-end delay and say which one causes jitter.

Transmission delay (packet size ÷ bandwidth), propagation delay (distance ÷ signal speed), queuing delay (waiting in router buffers) and processing delay (header parsing and lookup). Queuing delay is the only one that varies with load, so it is the source of jitter — which is why voice and video traffic is given priority queuing.

A 1 KB packet is sent over a 10 Mbps link 2000 km long. Propagation speed is 2×10⁸ m/s. Find transmission and propagation delay.

Transmission delay = (1024 × 8 bits) ÷ (10 × 10⁶ bps) = 8192 ÷ 10⁷ = 0.819 ms.
Propagation delay = 2 × 10⁶ m ÷ 2 × 10⁸ m/s = 10 ms.
Notice propagation dominates. On long links, making the packet bigger costs almost nothing, which is why WAN protocols favour large windows and large frames.

Why is packet switching preferred over circuit switching for data?

Data traffic is bursty — a browser is idle most of the time. A reserved circuit wastes that idle capacity, whereas packet switching lets many bursty flows share the same link (statistical multiplexing), giving far higher utilisation. Packet switching also survives link failure because packets can be rerouted, and it needs no setup delay for short transfers. The price is variable delay and possible loss, which is acceptable for data but was historically bad for voice.

How many cables and how many ports per node does a full mesh of n nodes need?

n(n−1)/2 links, and each node needs n−1 interfaces. For 10 nodes that is 45 cables and 9 ports each — the reason full mesh is only used for small, critical backbones.

Which topology is used in modern Ethernet LANs, and why did bus die?

Physical star (every host cabled to a switch), logically point-to-point full duplex. Bus died because a single cable fault brought down the whole segment, fault isolation was painful, and the shared medium forced half duplex with collisions. A switch gives every host its own collision domain and full-duplex operation.

What is the bandwidth-delay product and why does it matter?

Bandwidth × round-trip time = the amount of data that can be "in flight" on the link at once. A sender's window must be at least this large to keep the pipe full; otherwise it stalls waiting for acknowledgements. High BDP paths ("long fat networks" — satellite, intercontinental fibre) are exactly why TCP needed the window-scaling option beyond the original 64 KB limit.

Half duplex vs full duplex — where does each appear today?

Half duplex survives where the medium is genuinely shared and cannot separate directions: Wi-Fi (a radio cannot cleanly transmit and receive on the same channel simultaneously) and legacy hub-based Ethernet. Full duplex is standard on any switched Ethernet port, because the twisted pair has separate transmit and receive pairs and the switch buffers each direction independently. Full duplex also eliminates collisions, which is why CSMA/CD is disabled on modern links.

02 · Reference models

OSI and TCP/IP

Networking is too big to design as one program, so it is split into layers. Each layer offers a service to the layer above, uses the service of the layer below, and talks to its peer layer on the far machine using a protocol. The rule that makes it work is that a layer may only be replaced, never bypassed: you can swap Wi-Fi for Ethernet underneath IP without touching a single line of a web browser.

OSI (Open Systems Interconnection, ISO 1984) has seven layers. It is a teaching and reference model — almost nothing implements it exactly — but every interview uses its vocabulary.

Encapsulation, decapsulation and headers

Going down the stack, each layer prepends its own header (the data link layer also appends a trailer). Going up, each layer strips its own header and hands the payload upward. The formal name for a layer's unit is PDU (Protocol Data Unit); the name for the data handed down from above is the SDU (Service Data Unit). So a TCP segment's payload is the application's SDU.

The point interviewers look for

MAC addresses change at every hop; IP addresses do not. When your packet crosses five routers, the source and destination IP stay the same all the way (unless NAT rewrites them), but the layer-2 frame is rebuilt with a new source and destination MAC on every single link. That one sentence demonstrates that you understand layering.

Interview questions · Models
Name the seven OSI layers and one protocol for each.guaranteed

Application (HTTP), Presentation (TLS/SSL, JPEG), Session (RPC, NetBIOS), Transport (TCP/UDP), Network (IP, ICMP), Data link (Ethernet, ARP, PPP), Physical (Ethernet 100BASE-TX cabling, DSL, USB electrical spec). Answer top-down and state the data unit at each layer without being asked — it reads as fluency.

Why do we need layering at all? What is the disadvantage?

Layering gives modularity — a layer can be redesigned without touching the others — plus standard interfaces that allow multi-vendor interoperability, and it makes an enormous problem teachable and debuggable one level at a time. The costs are overhead (every layer adds a header, so a 1-byte payload can carry 54 bytes of headers), duplicated functionality (error checking appears at L2, L3 and L4), and lost optimisation opportunity because a layer cannot see information that would help it — for instance TCP treats wireless loss as congestion because L2 hides the reason.

Key differences between the OSI and TCP/IP models?very common

OSI has 7 layers, TCP/IP has 4. OSI cleanly separates service, interface and protocol; TCP/IP does not. OSI's session and presentation layers are folded into TCP/IP's application layer. OSI defined the model before the protocols existed (which is partly why it never got deployed); TCP/IP described protocols that already worked. OSI's network layer supports both connectionless and connection-oriented; TCP/IP's internet layer is connectionless only, with the choice pushed to transport.

Difference between a switch and a router.very common

A switch works at layer 2 using MAC addresses, forwards frames inside a single network, learns addresses automatically by observing source MACs, and floods when it does not know a destination. A router works at layer 3 using IP addresses, connects different networks, makes forwarding decisions from a routing table built by static configuration or routing protocols, decrements TTL, and does not forward broadcasts. In short: a switch decides which port on this LAN, a router decides which network next.

What is a collision domain and a broadcast domain?

A collision domain is the set of interfaces whose transmissions can collide with each other. A broadcast domain is the set of interfaces that receive a broadcast frame sent by any one of them. A hub has one of each. A switch creates one collision domain per port but keeps one broadcast domain (per VLAN). A router breaks both — each interface is a separate broadcast domain. Standard follow-up: an 8-port switch connected to a router port gives 8 collision domains and 1 broadcast domain.

Which layer does a firewall operate at?

It depends on the type. A packet-filtering firewall works at layers 3 and 4 (source/destination IP, port, protocol flags). A stateful firewall also tracks connection state at layer 4. An application-layer or next-generation firewall / proxy inspects layer 7 content such as HTTP URLs and TLS SNI. Saying "layer 3 and 4, or up to 7 if it is an application gateway" is the complete answer.

What are PDU and SDU?

The PDU is the complete unit a layer exchanges with its peer — header plus payload (frame, packet, segment). The SDU is the payload handed down from the layer above, before this layer's header is added. So layer N's PDU = layer N header + layer N SDU, and layer N's SDU is layer N+1's PDU.

A packet crosses three routers. How many times does the MAC address change and how many times does the IP address change?

The layer-2 header is rebuilt on every link, so with three routers there are four links and the source/destination MAC pair changes four times. The IP addresses stay the same end to end — unless NAT is in the path, in which case the NAT device rewrites the source IP and port once on the way out and reverses it on the way back.

Is TLS a layer 6 or layer 4 protocol?

In OSI terms it is presentation layer (6), because it does encryption and syntax translation for the application. In the real TCP/IP stack it sits between TCP and the application — commonly described as "layer 6.5" or simply as part of the application layer. A safe interview answer: "OSI would call it presentation layer; in practice it runs on top of TCP and below HTTP, which is why the combination is called HTTPS."

Layer 1 · Physical

Bits on a wire

The physical layer has exactly one responsibility: take a stream of bits from the data link layer and turn it into a signal the medium can carry — and reverse that at the far end. It defines voltages, pin layouts, cable specifications, frequencies, modulation and bit timing. It has no idea what a frame or an address is.

Nyquist and Shannon

Two formulas bound how fast any channel can go.

Nyquist (noiseless channel): C = 2 * B * log2(L) bits per second B = bandwidth in Hz, L = number of discrete signal levels Shannon (noisy channel): C = B * log2(1 + S/N) bits per second S/N = signal-to-noise power ratio (linear, not dB) SNR(dB) = 10 * log10(S/N) Example: B = 3000 Hz telephone line, SNR = 30 dB -> S/N = 1000 C = 3000 * log2(1001) ~= 3000 * 9.97 ~= 29.9 kbps which is exactly why 56k modems needed a digital leg at the exchange.

Modes of switching at the physical edge

Modem (modulator–demodulator) converts digital data to an analogue carrier for telephone or cable plant. Codec does the reverse — analogue voice to digital, using PCM: sample at twice the highest frequency (8 kHz for 4 kHz voice), quantise to 8 bits, giving the classic 64 kbps voice channel.

Interview questions · Physical layer
Why are the pairs in a twisted-pair cable twisted?

Because the two wires carry equal and opposite signals, twisting makes both wires pick up almost identical external interference. The receiver looks at the difference between them, so the common-mode noise cancels out. Twisting also reduces crosstalk between adjacent pairs, and different pairs use different twist rates so they do not couple with each other.

Single-mode vs multi-mode fibre?

Single-mode has a ~9 µm core allowing only one propagation path, uses a laser, has minimal dispersion and reaches tens to hundreds of kilometres — used for long haul. Multi-mode has a 50–62.5 µm core carrying many light paths, uses cheaper LEDs/VCSELs, but modal dispersion smears pulses so it is limited to roughly 300–550 m — used inside buildings and data centres.

What is Manchester encoding and why was it used in Ethernet?

Every bit period contains a mid-bit transition, so the receiver can recover the clock from the data itself no matter how many identical bits arrive in a row, and the signal is DC balanced. 10 Mbps Ethernet used it for exactly that reason. The cost is that it needs twice the bandwidth (20 Mbaud for 10 Mbps), which is why faster Ethernet moved to 4B/5B and 8B/10B block codes that guarantee transitions with far less overhead.

State Shannon's capacity theorem and compute capacity for a 4 kHz channel with SNR 20 dB.

C = B·log₂(1 + S/N). SNR 20 dB → S/N = 10²⁰ᐟ¹⁰ = 100. C = 4000 × log₂(101) ≈ 4000 × 6.66 ≈ 26.6 kbps. Shannon gives an upper bound that no coding scheme can beat; Nyquist gives the limit for a noiseless channel with a given number of signal levels. In practice you take the smaller of the two.

Difference between FDM and TDM, and what is statistical TDM?

FDM splits the frequency band so all users transmit simultaneously on different frequencies — suited to analogue media. TDM splits time so each user gets the full band in its own slot — suited to digital. Synchronous TDM assigns a fixed slot per user, wasting the slot when that user is idle. Statistical (asynchronous) TDM assigns slots on demand to whoever has data, so it needs an address in each slot but achieves much higher utilisation for bursty traffic.

Why does a hub belong to the physical layer while a switch belongs to the data link layer?

A hub only regenerates and repeats electrical signals — it copies incoming bits to every other port with no idea where a frame begins or what address it carries. A switch buffers the whole frame, reads the destination MAC in the header, consults a learned MAC table and forwards only to the correct port, which requires understanding layer-2 framing.

What limits an Ethernet copper run to 100 metres?

Two things. Signal attenuation and crosstalk degrade the signal beyond recovery over longer copper runs at the specified frequencies. Historically there was also a timing constraint: in half-duplex CSMA/CD the round-trip time of the whole segment had to be short enough that a collision reached the sender before it finished sending the minimum 64-byte frame. Modern full-duplex links only face the signal-quality limit.

Layer 2 · Data link

Getting across one hop, correctly

The data link layer turns a raw, error-prone bit pipe into a link that delivers frames reliably between two directly connected nodes. It has five jobs: framing, physical addressing, error control, flow control and medium access control. It is usually split into two sublayers — LLC (Logical Link Control, IEEE 802.2) which talks to the network layer and handles flow/error control, and MAC (Media Access Control) which decides who gets to transmit.

Framing — where does a frame start?

  • Character count — first field says how many bytes follow. Fragile: one corrupted count destroys all following frames.
  • Byte stuffing — frames delimited by a FLAG byte; any FLAG or ESC inside the payload is preceded by an ESC byte. Used by PPP.
  • Bit stuffing — flag is 01111110; the sender inserts a 0 after any five consecutive 1s in the data, and the receiver removes it. Used by HDLC.
  • Physical layer coding violations — use signal patterns that never occur in valid data as delimiters (possible with Manchester/4B-5B).

MAC addresses

A MAC address is a 48-bit (6-byte) identifier burned into the NIC, written as six hex pairs such as 3C:5A:B4:0F:2E:91. The first three bytes are the OUI (Organisationally Unique Identifier) assigned to the manufacturer; the last three are assigned by the manufacturer. It is flat (no hierarchy), which is why it cannot be routed — a router would need a table with every MAC address on Earth.

  • Unicast — least significant bit of the first byte is 0.
  • Multicast — that bit is 1, e.g. 01:00:5E:xx:xx:xx for IPv4 multicast.
  • Broadcast — all ones, FF:FF:FF:FF:FF:FF.

Error detection

Errors come as single-bit errors (rare, mostly in parallel buses) and burst errors (common on serial links, where several consecutive bits are corrupted).

Parity

Append one bit so the total number of 1s is even (even parity) or odd. Detects any odd number of bit errors, and misses all even-numbered ones. Two-dimensional parity arranges data in a grid with row and column parity, and can detect most burst errors and correct any single-bit error.

Checksum

Split data into k-bit words, add them using one's complement arithmetic, and transmit the complement of the sum. The receiver adds everything including the checksum; the result should be all 1s. Used by IP, TCP and UDP because it is cheap in software — but weak, since reordering words or compensating errors go undetected.

CRC — Cyclic Redundancy Check

The strong one, used by every layer-2 protocol. Treat the bit string as coefficients of a polynomial, append r zero bits where r is the degree of an agreed generator polynomial, divide by the generator using modulo-2 (XOR) division, and replace the appended zeros with the remainder. The receiver divides the whole received frame by the same generator: a zero remainder means no detected error.

Medium access control — who transmits now?

Random access

  • Pure ALOHA — transmit whenever you have data; if no ack, wait a random time and retry. Maximum throughput 18.4% (1/2e).
  • Slotted ALOHA — transmission may only start at slot boundaries, halving the vulnerable period. Maximum throughput 36.8% (1/e).
  • CSMA — listen before transmitting. 1-persistent: if idle, send immediately. Non-persistent: if busy, wait a random time then sense again. p-persistent: if idle, send with probability p.
  • CSMA/CD (Ethernet) — listen while transmitting; on detecting a collision, stop immediately, send a 32-bit jam signal, and back off using binary exponential backoff: after the n-th collision wait a random number of slot times in [0, 2ⁿ−1], capped at n=10, give up after 16 attempts.
  • CSMA/CA (Wi-Fi) — collisions cannot be detected on radio, so avoid them: wait for the channel to be idle for a DIFS interval, pick a random backoff counter, freeze it while the channel is busy, and optionally exchange RTS/CTS before large frames. Every unicast frame is acknowledged.

Controlled and channelised access

Reservation, polling (a primary station invites each secondary to transmit) and token passing (a token circulates; only its holder may send — Token Ring, FDDI) give collision-free, bounded-delay access. FDMA/TDMA/CDMA partition the channel itself and are used in cellular systems.

Switching: how a switch learns

  1. A frame arrives on port 3 with source MAC AA:... → the switch records "AA is on port 3" in its CAM table with a timer (typically 300 s).
  2. It looks up the destination MAC. If found, forward out only that port. If not found, flood to all ports except the incoming one.
  3. If the destination is on the same port the frame came from, filter (drop) it.

Forwarding modes: store-and-forward receives the whole frame and verifies the CRC before forwarding (safest, highest latency); cut-through forwards as soon as it has read the 6-byte destination MAC (lowest latency, propagates corrupt frames); fragment-free waits for the first 64 bytes, catching most collision fragments.

Loops, STP and VLANs

Layer 2 has no TTL, so a loop causes a broadcast storm that multiplies frames until the network collapses. Spanning Tree Protocol (STP, 802.1D) prevents this: switches elect a root bridge (lowest bridge ID = priority + MAC), each other switch picks its lowest-cost root port, each segment picks a designated port, and every remaining port is blocked — producing a loop-free tree that automatically reconverges when a link fails. RSTP (802.1w) does the same in seconds instead of ~50 s.

A VLAN logically splits one physical switch into several broadcast domains. Ports are assigned to VLAN IDs, and traffic between VLANs must pass through a router or L3 switch. Traffic crossing a link that carries several VLANs (a trunk) is tagged with a 4-byte 802.1Q header containing the 12-bit VLAN ID, so VLANs 1–4094 are available.

ARP — bridging layer 3 and layer 2

A host knows the destination IP but a frame needs a destination MAC. ARP fills that gap: the host broadcasts "who has 192.168.1.10, tell 192.168.1.5", the owner replies with its MAC by unicast, and the result is cached in the ARP table for a few minutes.

  • Gratuitous ARP — a host announces its own mapping unprompted, to detect duplicate IPs or update everyone's cache after a failover.
  • Proxy ARP — a router answers on behalf of a host on another segment.
  • RARP / BOOTP / DHCP — the reverse problem, mapping your own MAC to an IP at boot. RARP is obsolete; DHCP replaced it.
Remember this ordering

If the destination IP is on the same subnet, ARP resolves the destination's own MAC. If it is on a different subnet, the host ARPs for the default gateway's MAC instead, and sends the frame there with the original destination IP untouched. Interviewers love this distinction.

Interview questions · Data link layer
What is the difference between a MAC address and an IP address?guaranteed

A MAC address is a 48-bit flat physical address burned into the NIC by the manufacturer; it identifies an interface on a local link, works at layer 2, and cannot be routed because it carries no location information. An IP address is a 32-bit (IPv4) or 128-bit (IPv6) logical address assigned by the network administrator or DHCP; it is hierarchical, so routers can aggregate and forward on the network portion. MAC is "who you are", IP is "where you are". Both are needed: IP gets the packet to the right network, MAC gets the frame to the right device on that network.

Explain ARP with the exact packet flow.very common

Host A wants to send to IP 192.168.1.10 on its own subnet. It checks its ARP cache; on a miss it broadcasts an ARP request to FF:FF:FF:FF:FF:FF containing its own IP and MAC and the target IP. Every host on the segment receives it, but only 192.168.1.10 replies — with a unicast ARP reply carrying its MAC. Both sides now cache the mapping (the target learned A's mapping from the request itself). If the destination were on a different subnet, A would instead ARP for its default gateway.

Why is the minimum Ethernet frame size 64 bytes?

For CSMA/CD to work, a sender must still be transmitting when a collision signal from the farthest point of the network gets back to it — otherwise it would never learn its frame was destroyed. For 10 Mbps Ethernet with a maximum 2500 m span and 4 repeaters, the round-trip slot time is 51.2 µs, which equals 512 bits = 64 bytes. Frames shorter than this are padded; frames received shorter than 64 bytes are treated as collision fragments ("runts") and discarded.

CSMA/CD vs CSMA/CA — why does Wi-Fi not use collision detection?very common

A wireless radio transmits far more strongly than it receives, so its own transmission drowns out any colliding signal — detection is physically impractical on a half-duplex radio. There is also the hidden terminal problem: two stations can both hear the access point but not each other, so neither would ever sense the other's transmission. So 802.11 avoids collisions instead: sense the channel, wait a DIFS, use random backoff with a contention window that doubles after failures, optionally exchange RTS/CTS so all stations set their NAV timer, and acknowledge every unicast frame — an unacknowledged frame is assumed collided.

Compare Go-Back-N and Selective Repeat.common

Go-Back-N has a sender window of N and a receiver window of 1: the receiver discards anything out of order, so one lost frame forces retransmission of that frame and all subsequent ones. Selective Repeat has windows of N on both sides: the receiver buffers out-of-order frames and NAKs only the missing one, so only that frame is retransmitted. Go-Back-N is simpler and needs no receiver buffer but wastes bandwidth on error-prone links; Selective Repeat is efficient but needs buffering, sorting and individual acknowledgement. With an n-bit sequence number the maximum windows are 2ⁿ−1 and 2ⁿ⁻¹ respectively.

Why must the Selective Repeat window be at most half the sequence number space?

Because the receiver cannot distinguish a retransmission from a new frame otherwise. With a 3-bit sequence number (0–7) and window 7: if the receiver accepts 0–6, sends acknowledgements that are all lost, and slides its window to 7,0,1,…, the sender times out and resends frame 0 — which the receiver now accepts as the new frame 0, corrupting the stream. Limiting the window to 4 (half of 8) guarantees the old and new windows never overlap.

How does CRC detect errors, and what can it not detect?

The sender appends r zero bits (r = degree of the generator polynomial), performs modulo-2 division of the data by the generator, and replaces the zeros with the remainder. The receiver divides the whole frame by the same generator; a non-zero remainder means an error. It fails only when the error pattern is itself an exact multiple of the generator polynomial — extremely unlikely for a well-chosen generator. A degree-r generator catches all bursts up to length r, all single and double bit errors, and all odd-numbered errors if (x+1) is a factor.

Compute the number of parity bits needed to Hamming-correct 8 data bits.

Need the smallest r with 2ʳ ≥ m + r + 1 = 8 + r + 1. r = 4 gives 16 ≥ 13 ✓, r = 3 gives 8 ≥ 12 ✗. So 4 parity bits, total codeword 12 bits, placed at positions 1, 2, 4 and 8.

What is a broadcast storm and how does STP prevent it?

Ethernet frames have no TTL. If a physical loop exists, a broadcast frame is flooded around the loop endlessly and duplicated at every switch, saturating links and CPU within seconds; MAC tables also flap as the same source appears on multiple ports. STP builds a loop-free logical tree: switches exchange BPDUs, elect the lowest bridge ID as root, each switch selects one root port with the lowest path cost, each segment elects a designated port, and all other ports are put in blocking state. If an active link fails, a blocked port transitions through listening and learning to forwarding, restoring connectivity.

What is a VLAN and why use one?

A VLAN partitions a physical switch into multiple logical broadcast domains identified by a 12-bit VLAN ID. Benefits: containment of broadcast traffic, security isolation (HR cannot see engineering traffic without passing a router where policy can be applied), and logical grouping independent of physical location. Devices in different VLANs need a router or L3 switch to communicate — "inter-VLAN routing", often done with a router-on-a-stick over an 802.1Q trunk.

Store-and-forward vs cut-through switching?

Store-and-forward buffers the entire frame and validates its FCS before forwarding, so corrupt frames never leave the switch; latency depends on frame size. Cut-through reads only the first 6 bytes (destination MAC) and starts forwarding immediately, giving fixed minimal latency but propagating corrupted and runt frames. Fragment-free is the compromise — wait for 64 bytes, which filters out collision fragments. Data centres with latency-sensitive workloads prefer cut-through; general enterprise switching uses store-and-forward.

What is ARP spoofing and how do you defend against it?

ARP has no authentication, so an attacker can send unsolicited ARP replies claiming the gateway's IP maps to the attacker's MAC. Victims then send all their off-subnet traffic to the attacker, enabling a man-in-the-middle or denial of service. Defences: dynamic ARP inspection on switches validating ARP packets against the DHCP snooping binding table, static ARP entries for critical hosts, port security, and encrypting traffic so interception yields nothing readable.

Layer 3 · Network

Addressing the whole world

The network layer moves packets from a source host to a destination host across any number of intermediate networks. It provides logical addressing (IP addresses), routing (choosing a path), forwarding (moving a packet from an input port to an output port) and fragmentation when a packet is too big for the next link. IP is deliberately a best-effort, connectionless service: it does not guarantee delivery, order or non-duplication. Everything stronger is built above it.

Fragmentation

If a packet is larger than the outgoing link's MTU, a router splits it. Every fragment gets the same Identification, an appropriate Fragment Offset, and MF = 1 except for the last one. Only the destination host reassembles — never an intermediate router — because different fragments may take different paths.

Datagram 4000 bytes total (20 B header + 3980 B data), link MTU = 1500 Frag 1: offset 0 , data 1480 B, MF=1 (offset field = 0) Frag 2: offset 1480 , data 1480 B, MF=1 (offset field = 185) Frag 3: offset 2960 , data 1020 B, MF=0 (offset field = 370) Note: data per fragment must be a multiple of 8 (except the last), because the offset field counts 8-byte units. 1480/8 = 185.

Fragmentation hurts — losing one fragment costs the whole datagram — so modern stacks set DF and use Path MTU Discovery: send with DF set, and if a router replies with ICMP "Fragmentation Needed", reduce the size. IPv6 forbids router fragmentation entirely; only the source may fragment.

NAT lets many private hosts share one public IP. The most common form is PAT / NAT overload, which translates both address and port.

Given 192.168.10.75/26, find the network address, broadcast address, usable range and number of hosts.guaranteed

Why are two addresses subtracted when counting usable hosts?

The all-zeros host portion identifies the network itself and the all-ones host portion is the directed broadcast address for that subnet, so neither can be assigned to an interface. The exception is /31 on point-to-point links (RFC 3021), where both addresses are usable because a broadcast address is meaningless with only two endpoints.

How many subnets and hosts if you subnet a class B network with a /20 mask?

Class B default is /16, so 4 bits are borrowed → 2⁴ = 16 subnets. Host bits = 32 − 20 = 12 → 2¹² − 2 = 4094 usable hosts per subnet. Block size in the third octet = 256 − 240 = 16, so subnets are x.y.0.0, x.y.16.0, x.y.32.0 …

What is the difference between routing and forwarding?

Routing is the control-plane process of building the routing table — running algorithms and exchanging information with other routers to decide the best paths. Forwarding is the data-plane action of taking an arriving packet, looking up its destination in the (already built) forwarding table and sending it out the right interface. Routing happens on a timescale of seconds to minutes; forwarding happens per packet in nanoseconds, usually in hardware.

Explain NAT and one problem it causes.very common

NAT rewrites the source IP (and with PAT, the source port) of outgoing packets so an entire private network appears as one public address, keeping a translation table to reverse it for replies. It conserves scarce IPv4 addresses and hides internal topology. The problem is that it breaks the end-to-end principle: an outside host cannot initiate a connection inward without explicit port forwarding, which is why peer-to-peer applications, VoIP and online games need STUN/TURN/ICE or UPnP hole punching. It also breaks protocols that embed IP addresses inside the payload, such as FTP in active mode and SIP, requiring application-layer gateways.

What is TTL and what happens when it reaches zero?

Time To Live is an 8-bit counter in the IP header, decremented by one at every router. When it hits zero the router discards the packet and sends an ICMP Time Exceeded message back to the source. It exists to stop packets circulating forever when a routing loop forms. Traceroute exploits it deliberately: send packets with TTL 1, 2, 3… and each router along the path identifies itself by returning the ICMP error.

A 4000-byte datagram must cross a link with MTU 1500. Describe the fragments.

Header is 20 bytes, so payload is 3980 bytes and each fragment can carry at most 1480 data bytes (a multiple of 8). Fragment 1: 1480 bytes, offset field 0, MF = 1. Fragment 2: 1480 bytes, offset field 185 (1480/8), MF = 1. Fragment 3: 1020 bytes, offset field 370 (2960/8), MF = 0. All three carry the same Identification value, and only the destination host reassembles them.

Why does IPv6 have no header checksum?

To speed up forwarding. In IPv4 the checksum must be recalculated at every hop because the TTL changes, which is per-packet work at every router. The designers judged it redundant: layer 2 already protects each link with a CRC, and TCP/UDP carry an end-to-end checksum that covers the addresses via the pseudo-header. Removing it also allowed the fixed 40-byte header that hardware can parse in one shot.

What is APIPA and when do you see 169.254.x.x?

Automatic Private IP Addressing. When a host boots and no DHCP server answers, it self-assigns a random address from 169.254.0.0/16 after checking with ARP that nobody else has it. Hosts on the same link can then still talk to each other, but there is no gateway or DNS, so seeing 169.254.x.x in ipconfig is a reliable symptom of "DHCP is not reachable".

Why does DHCP use UDP and not TCP?

The client has no IP address when it starts, so it must broadcast from 0.0.0.0 — and TCP cannot perform a three-way handshake without valid addresses on both sides, nor can it broadcast. UDP's connectionless broadcast fits exactly. Reliability is handled by DHCP itself through retransmission with backoff.

Explain the difference between unicast, broadcast, multicast and anycast.

Unicast is one to one. Broadcast is one to all on a subnet (255.255.255.255 limited, or the subnet's directed broadcast); it does not exist in IPv6. Multicast is one to a subscribed group (224.0.0.0/4 in IPv4, ff00::/8 in IPv6), managed by IGMP — efficient for IPTV and routing protocol updates. Anycast is one to the nearest of many hosts sharing the same address; routing delivers to whichever instance is closest, which is how root DNS servers and CDN edges work.

Aggregate 172.16.0.0/24, 172.16.1.0/24, 172.16.2.0/24 and 172.16.3.0/24.

Third octet values 0, 1, 2, 3 in binary are 00000000, 00000001, 00000010, 00000011 — the first 6 bits are common. So the common prefix length is 16 + 6 = 22, giving 172.16.0.0/22. This is route summarisation, and it is why CIDR replaced classful addressing.

Layer 3 · Routing

Choosing the path

A routing table maps a destination prefix to a next hop and outgoing interface. Forwarding uses longest prefix match: among all entries that match the destination, the one with the most specific (longest) mask wins. 0.0.0.0/0 matches everything with length zero, so it is the last resort — the default route.

Static versus dynamic routing

Static routes are typed in by an administrator: zero protocol overhead, fully predictable, secure — but they do not react to failures and do not scale. Dynamic routing protocols exchange information and reconverge automatically, at the cost of CPU, bandwidth and complexity.

Distance vector routing

Each router knows only the cost to its direct neighbours. It periodically sends its entire distance table to its neighbours, who update their own using the Bellman-Ford relation:

D_x(y) = min over neighbours v of { c(x,v) + D_v(y) } "My cost to y is the cheapest of (cost to reach neighbour v) + (v's claimed cost to y)"

Simple, but suffers count-to-infinity: if a link dies, routers can bounce increasing metrics off each other believing a path still exists. Mitigations: define infinity as a small number (RIP uses 16), split horizon (never advertise a route back out of the interface you learned it on), poison reverse (advertise it back with infinite metric), hold-down timers and triggered updates.

Link state routing

Every router discovers its neighbours, measures link costs, floods a Link State Advertisement to the entire area, and builds an identical map of the whole topology. Each then runs Dijkstra's shortest path first algorithm locally to compute its own tree. Converges fast, no count-to-infinity, but uses more memory and CPU and requires flooding.

Congestion control at the network layer

  • Leaky bucket — traffic leaves at a constant rate regardless of how bursty the input is; overflow is discarded. Smooths absolutely, but destroys legitimate bursts.
  • Token bucket — tokens accumulate at a fixed rate up to a bucket capacity; a packet may only be sent if it consumes a token. Allows bursts up to the bucket size while limiting the long-term average. Preferred in practice.
  • Choke packets / ECN — the router signals the source to slow down, either explicitly or by marking a bit in the IP header that the receiver echoes back.
  • RED (Random Early Detection) — start dropping packets probabilistically before the queue is full, so TCP flows back off at staggered times instead of all at once (avoiding global synchronisation).
Interview questions · Routing
Distance vector vs link state routing.very common

Distance vector: each router knows only its neighbours, shares its whole routing table with them periodically, and uses Bellman-Ford. Low memory, simple, but slow convergence and vulnerable to count-to-infinity. Link state: each router floods information about its own links to everyone, so every router builds a complete topology map and runs Dijkstra independently. Fast convergence, loop free, supports hierarchy through areas — at the cost of more CPU, memory and flooding traffic. RIP is the classic distance vector, OSPF the classic link state.

What is the count-to-infinity problem and how is it solved?

When a network becomes unreachable, a router may hear its own stale route echoed back by a neighbour and believe a path still exists, incrementing the metric one step at a time on every exchange — converging to "infinity" extremely slowly. Fixes: define a small infinity (RIP uses 16 hops), split horizon (never advertise a route back to the neighbour you learned it from), poison reverse (advertise it back with metric infinity), hold-down timers that ignore updates about a downed route for a while, and triggered updates that propagate bad news immediately rather than waiting for the next period.

How does longest prefix match work?

A destination address may match several routing table entries at once. The router selects the entry whose network mask has the most 1-bits — the most specific route. For destination 10.1.1.55, an entry for 10.1.1.0/24 beats 10.1.0.0/16, which beats 0.0.0.0/0. This is what makes CIDR and route aggregation possible: you can advertise a large block and still override a small piece of it with a more specific route.

Why does the Internet use BGP between ISPs instead of OSPF?

Scale and policy. OSPF would require every router to hold the full topology of the entire Internet and rerun Dijkstra on every change — computationally impossible. More importantly, inter-domain routing is not about shortest path but about business relationships: an ISP wants to prefer a customer route over a peer route over a transit route because of money, not hop count. BGP is a path vector protocol carrying the full AS path, which both prevents loops and gives operators the attributes (local preference, AS path prepending, MED, communities) needed to express policy.

Leaky bucket vs token bucket.

Leaky bucket outputs at a strictly constant rate — the bucket leaks at rate r no matter what — so it eliminates burstiness entirely and discards anything that overflows. Token bucket accumulates permission over time: tokens arrive at rate r and pile up to capacity b, so an application that has been quiet can send a burst of up to b immediately and then continues at r. Token bucket is generally preferred because real traffic is bursty and the burst allowance improves responsiveness without violating the long-term rate.

Run Dijkstra: source A, edges A-B 2, A-C 4, B-E 1, B-D 3, C-E 5, E-F 1, D-F 2. Shortest A to F?

A→B = 2. A→B→E = 3. A→B→E→F = 4. Alternative A→B→D→F = 2+3+2 = 7, and A→C→E→F = 4+5+1 = 10. Shortest path is A → B → E → F with cost 4.

What is the difference between a routing table and an ARP table?

The routing table maps destination networks to a next-hop IP and outgoing interface — it answers "which direction does this packet go?" The ARP table maps a next-hop IP to a MAC address on the local link — it answers "what do I write in the frame header to reach that next hop?" Forwarding uses both in sequence: routing table first to pick the next hop, then ARP to address the frame.

Layer 4 · Transport

From host-to-host to process-to-process

IP delivers a packet to a machine. The transport layer delivers it to the right program on that machine, and optionally makes the delivery reliable. It does this with port numbers (16-bit, so 0–65535), and adds segmentation, reassembly, error checking, flow control and congestion control.

  • Well-known ports 0–1023 — assigned by IANA, need privilege to bind.
  • Registered ports 1024–49151 — vendor applications.
  • Ephemeral/dynamic ports 49152–65535 — chosen by the client for the source port.

A connection is identified by a 4-tuple: source IP, source port, destination IP, destination port. That is why a server on port 443 can hold thousands of simultaneous connections — each client contributes a different source IP/port combination. A socket is the OS abstraction that represents one endpoint (IP + port).

The TCP header

0 1 2 3 +-------------------------------+-------------------------------+ | Source Port | Destination Port | +---------------------------------------------------------------+ | Sequence Number | +---------------------------------------------------------------+ | Acknowledgement Number | +------+----------+-------------+-------------------------------+ | Data | Reserved | Flags | Window Size | |Offset| |URG ACK PSH | | | | |RST SYN FIN | | +------+----------+-------------+-------------------------------+ | Checksum | Urgent Pointer | +-------------------------------+-------------------------------+ | Options (0-40 bytes) | +---------------------------------------------------------------+
  • Sequence number — byte offset of the first data byte in this segment, starting from a random Initial Sequence Number (random to defend against sequence prediction attacks).
  • Acknowledgement number — the next byte the receiver expects. TCP acknowledgements are cumulative.
  • Flags — SYN starts a connection, ACK marks the ack field valid, FIN closes gracefully, RST aborts immediately, PSH tells the receiver to hand data to the application without waiting, URG marks urgent data.
  • Window size — how many more bytes the receiver can accept: this is flow control.
  • Options — MSS, window scaling, SACK permitted, timestamps.

Three-way handshake

Client Server | | |--------- SYN, seq = x --------------------------->| client wants to connect | | |<-------- SYN + ACK, seq = y, ack = x+1 -----------| server agrees, states its ISN | | |--------- ACK, seq = x+1, ack = y+1 -------------->| connection ESTABLISHED | |

Why three and not two? Both sides must agree on both initial sequence numbers, and each must confirm that the other's ISN was received. Two messages would only confirm one direction. A third reason: it defends against an old duplicate SYN from a previous connection establishing a phantom connection — the client's final ACK proves the request is live.

Connection termination — four-way

Client Server |------ FIN, seq = u ----------------------------->| |<----- ACK, ack = u+1 ----------------------------| server may still send data |<----- FIN, seq = v ------------------------------| server finishes |------ ACK, ack = v+1 --------------------------->| | [TIME_WAIT: 2 * MSL, then CLOSED] |

It takes four messages because TCP connections are full duplex and each direction is closed independently — this is a half-close. The initiator then waits in TIME_WAIT for twice the maximum segment lifetime (typically 60 s) so that (a) a lost final ACK can be retransmitted, and (b) delayed segments from this connection die before the same 4-tuple is reused.

TCP state machine — the states you should be able to name

CLOSED -> SYN_SENT -> ESTABLISHED (active open, client) CLOSED -> LISTEN -> SYN_RCVD -> ESTABLISHED (passive open, server) Closing (initiator): ESTABLISHED -> FIN_WAIT_1 -> FIN_WAIT_2 -> TIME_WAIT -> CLOSED Closing (responder): ESTABLISHED -> CLOSE_WAIT -> LAST_ACK -> CLOSED

Practical diagnosis: many sockets stuck in CLOSE_WAIT means your application forgot to call close(). Many in TIME_WAIT is normal on a busy client. Many in SYN_RCVD suggests a SYN flood.

Flow control — the sliding window

The receiver advertises a window size in every acknowledgement, meaning "I have this much free buffer". The sender may have at most that many unacknowledged bytes outstanding. As acknowledgements arrive, the window slides forward.

Sender's byte stream [ sent & acked ][ sent, not acked ][ can send now ][ cannot send yet ] ................|=================|===============|.................. ^ ^ send base send base + window Two classic pathologies: Silly window syndrome - receiver advertises tiny windows, sender sends tiny segments. Fix: Clark's solution (receiver waits until half the buffer is free) + Nagle's algorithm (sender coalesces small writes until the previous ACK arrives). Zero window - receiver's buffer is full and advertises 0. Fix: sender starts a persist timer and sends window probes.

Congestion control

Flow control protects the receiver. Congestion control protects the network. TCP maintains a second limit, the congestion window (cwnd), and can send only min(cwnd, receiver window) bytes.

  1. Slow start — cwnd begins at 1 MSS and doubles every RTT (increases by 1 MSS per ACK), so it grows exponentially until it reaches the slow start threshold ssthresh.
  2. Congestion avoidance — beyond ssthresh, cwnd grows by 1 MSS per RTT (additive increase), probing carefully.
  3. Loss detected by three duplicate ACKs — treated as mild congestion. Fast retransmit resends the missing segment immediately without waiting for a timeout; fast recovery halves cwnd and continues in congestion avoidance (TCP Reno).
  4. Loss detected by timeout — treated as severe. ssthresh = cwnd/2, cwnd resets to 1 MSS, and slow start begins again.
cwnd | /\ /\ | / \ / \ <- additive increase | / \ / \ | / | / | <- multiplicative decrease (3 dup ACKs) | / | / | | ___/ |_____ / |___ | / \_/ \ | / <- slow start (exponential) \ <- timeout: back to cwnd = 1 +-------------------------------------------> time This is AIMD: Additive Increase, Multiplicative Decrease. It is what makes TCP flows converge to a fair share of a bottleneck.

Variants worth naming: Tahoe (any loss → cwnd = 1), Reno (adds fast recovery), NewReno, CUBIC (Linux default, cwnd grows as a cubic function of time since the last loss — better on high-BDP links), and BBR (Google; models bottleneck bandwidth and RTT instead of treating loss as the signal).

Retransmission timers

The retransmission timeout must adapt to the path. TCP measures the round-trip time and smooths it (Jacobson's algorithm):

EstimatedRTT = (1 - a) * EstimatedRTT + a * SampleRTT a = 0.125 DevRTT = (1 - b) * DevRTT + b * |SampleRTT - EstimatedRTT| b = 0.25 RTO = EstimatedRTT + 4 * DevRTT Karn's algorithm: never take an RTT sample from a retransmitted segment (you cannot tell which copy was acknowledged). Double the RTO on each retransmission instead - exponential backoff.

UDP header and when to prefer it

+---------------+---------------+ | Source Port | Dest Port | +---------------+---------------+ | Length | Checksum | +---------------+---------------+ Just 8 bytes. Checksum is optional in IPv4, mandatory in IPv6.

Choose UDP when late data is worse than lost data (live voice, video, gaming), when the exchange is a single short request/response so a handshake would double the latency (DNS), when you need broadcast or multicast, or when you want to build your own reliability in userspace (QUIC does exactly this).

QUIC — worth mentioning, it impresses

QUIC runs on UDP and reimplements streams, reliability, congestion control and TLS 1.3 in userspace. It gets a 0-RTT or 1-RTT handshake instead of TCP's handshake plus TLS handshake, eliminates head-of-line blocking between independent streams, and survives an IP address change (Wi-Fi to mobile data) because connections are identified by a connection ID rather than the 4-tuple. HTTP/3 is HTTP over QUIC.

Interview questions · Transport layer
TCP vs UDP — and when would you deliberately choose UDP?guaranteed

TCP is connection-oriented, reliable, ordered, flow- and congestion-controlled, byte-stream based, with a 20-byte minimum header. UDP is connectionless, unreliable, unordered, message-oriented, with an 8-byte header and no congestion control. Choose UDP when timeliness beats completeness — a retransmitted voice packet arrives too late to play, so dropping it is better; when the transaction is one request and one reply, where TCP's handshake would triple the latency (DNS); when you need multicast; or when you want to implement custom reliability, as QUIC and most game engines do.

Explain the three-way handshake and why two messages are not enough.guaranteed

Client sends SYN with its initial sequence number x. Server replies SYN+ACK with its own ISN y and ack = x+1. Client sends ACK with ack = y+1, and the connection is established. Two messages would let the client confirm the server's existence but would never confirm to the server that its own sequence number was received, so the server could not safely start sending. The third message also prevents a delayed duplicate SYN from an old connection creating a half-open connection that consumes server resources forever.

Why is connection termination four-way while establishment is three-way?

Because a TCP connection is two independent simplex streams. During setup, the server can combine its SYN and its ACK into one segment because it is ready to both accept and send immediately. During teardown, when one side sends FIN it means "I have no more data", but the other side may still have data queued — so it acknowledges the FIN right away and sends its own FIN only later, when it is finished. That splits the middle message into two. If both sides close simultaneously, it can compress back to three or four segments exchanged in parallel.

What is the TIME_WAIT state and why does it last 2×MSL?

The side that closes first waits in TIME_WAIT for twice the maximum segment lifetime, about 60 seconds on most systems. Two reasons: if its final ACK is lost, the peer will retransmit its FIN and this socket must still exist to re-acknowledge it; and any straggling segments from this connection must expire before the same 4-tuple can be reused, otherwise old data could be delivered into a new connection. On busy servers this consumes port numbers, which is why SO_REUSEADDR and tuning exist.

Difference between flow control and congestion control.very common

Flow control is end to end and protects the receiver from being overrun — it is driven by the receiver's advertised window, which reflects free buffer space. Congestion control protects the network's intermediate routers from overload — it is driven by the sender's inferred congestion window using loss and delay as signals. TCP sends min(cwnd, rwnd). A fast server talking to a slow phone hits flow control; a fast server on a saturated uplink hits congestion control.

Walk through TCP congestion control phases.very common

Slow start: cwnd = 1 MSS, doubling every RTT until it reaches ssthresh. Congestion avoidance: linear growth of 1 MSS per RTT. On three duplicate ACKs: fast retransmit the missing segment immediately, set ssthresh = cwnd/2, set cwnd = ssthresh (fast recovery) and continue linearly — this is Reno. On timeout, which signals severe congestion: ssthresh = cwnd/2 and cwnd drops back to 1 MSS, restarting slow start. The overall behaviour is AIMD, which mathematically drives competing flows toward an equal share of the bottleneck.

Why is slow start called "slow" if it grows exponentially?

Because it starts from a very small window — one segment — rather than immediately blasting the receiver's full advertised window, which was the behaviour it replaced after the 1986 Internet congestion collapse. The rate of increase is exponential; the starting point is slow.

What is silly window syndrome and how is it fixed?

It is when TCP degenerates into sending many tiny segments, so header overhead dwarfs payload. It happens from the receiver side when an application reads one byte at a time and the receiver keeps advertising a 1-byte window, and from the sender side when an application writes one byte at a time. The receiver-side fix is Clark's solution — advertise zero until either half the buffer or a full MSS is free. The sender-side fix is Nagle's algorithm — buffer small writes and send them as one segment when the previous acknowledgement arrives. Nagle interacts badly with delayed ACK for interactive traffic, which is why TCP_NODELAY exists.

What is a socket, and what makes a TCP connection unique?

A socket is the operating system's endpoint abstraction for network I/O, identified by an IP address and a port number. A TCP connection is uniquely identified by the 4-tuple (source IP, source port, destination IP, destination port) — which is how one server socket listening on port 80 can serve thousands of simultaneous connections: each client contributes a distinct source IP/port pair.

What is a SYN flood and how is it mitigated?

An attacker sends many SYN segments with spoofed source addresses and never completes the handshake. Each one makes the server allocate a Transmission Control Block and sit in SYN_RCVD until timeout, exhausting the backlog queue so legitimate connections are refused. Mitigations: SYN cookies — the server encodes its state into the initial sequence number of its SYN+ACK and allocates nothing until a valid ACK returns; plus shorter SYN timeouts, larger backlogs, rate limiting and upstream filtering.

What does the PSH flag do, and how is it different from URG?

PSH tells the receiving TCP to deliver buffered data to the application immediately instead of waiting for more to accumulate — it affects timing, not ordering. URG marks part of the segment as urgent, and the Urgent Pointer indicates where that data ends; it is intended to be delivered out of band, for example a Ctrl-C in Telnet. URG is essentially deprecated in modern applications.

How does TCP calculate its retransmission timeout?

It measures SampleRTT, then keeps an exponentially weighted moving average: EstimatedRTT = 0.875·EstimatedRTT + 0.125·SampleRTT, plus a deviation estimate DevRTT. RTO = EstimatedRTT + 4·DevRTT, so a jittery path gets a larger margin. Karn's algorithm forbids taking a sample from a retransmitted segment (you cannot tell which copy the ACK refers to) and instead doubles the RTO on each retransmission.

Can UDP be made reliable?

Yes — by implementing sequence numbers, acknowledgements, retransmission and congestion control in the application. That is exactly what QUIC, RTP with retransmission, TFTP and most multiplayer game protocols do. The reason to do this rather than use TCP is to keep control over trade-offs: you might want reliability for some message types and not others, or need to avoid TCP's head-of-line blocking, or need to survive an IP address change mid-connection.

Layers 5 & 6 · Session and presentation

Dialogue and translation

These two layers exist in OSI but were folded into the application layer in TCP/IP, so they are short — yet interviewers ask about them precisely because most candidates skip them.

Session layer (5)

  • Session establishment, maintenance and termination — set up a long-running dialogue between two applications, including authentication and authorisation at the start.
  • Dialogue control — decide whether communication is half or full duplex and manage whose turn it is.
  • Synchronisation and checkpointing — insert markers into a long transfer so that after a failure it resumes from the last checkpoint instead of the beginning. Transferring a 2-hour file with checkpoints every 5 minutes means a crash costs at most 5 minutes.

Examples: RPC, NetBIOS, PPTP, SQL and NFS sessions, and the session tokens in web applications.

Presentation layer (6)

  • Translation — reconcile different internal representations. Character sets (ASCII, EBCDIC, UTF-8) and byte order matter here: big-endian is the "network byte order", so htons() and ntohs() exist for this reason.
  • Encryption and decryption — TLS/SSL is placed here in the OSI mapping.
  • Compression — reduce bytes on the wire; JPEG, MP3, gzip content-encoding.

Data-format standards such as ASN.1 / BER, XDR, JSON and Protocol Buffers are presentation-layer concerns in spirit.

Cookies and sessions in the web sense

HTTP is stateless — each request is independent. State is reconstructed by the server issuing a Set-Cookie header holding a session identifier, which the browser returns on every subsequent request. The real session data lives on the server, keyed by that identifier. Important cookie attributes: HttpOnly (JavaScript cannot read it, blocking theft via XSS), Secure (sent only over HTTPS), SameSite (mitigates CSRF), Domain/Path (scope) and Max-Age/Expires (session cookie versus persistent cookie).

Interview questions · Session & presentation
What does the session layer do that the transport layer does not?

Transport gives you a reliable byte pipe between two ports but has no concept of a longer-lived conversation. The session layer adds dialogue semantics on top: who speaks when, authentication at the start of a dialogue, and synchronisation points so a long transfer can resume after failure rather than restarting. A TCP connection can drop and be re-established while the logical session continues.

What is a session, a cookie and a token — and how do they differ?

A session is server-side state about a client, identified by a session ID. A cookie is a small key-value pair the browser stores and automatically returns to the issuing domain — usually it just carries the session ID. A token (for example a JWT) is a self-contained, signed credential that carries the claims themselves, so the server does not need to store anything — good for stateless horizontal scaling, but harder to revoke before expiry. Cookies are a transport mechanism; sessions and tokens are two different strategies for state.

Why is TLS often described as sitting at layer 6?

Because encryption and data-format translation are presentation-layer functions in the OSI model. In the deployed TCP/IP stack there is no layer 6 — TLS runs directly on top of TCP and beneath the application protocol, so it is often labelled "layer 6.5" or grouped with the application layer. The practical point is that HTTP does not change at all when it becomes HTTPS; TLS wraps the byte stream transparently.

What is network byte order and why does it matter?

Network byte order is big-endian — the most significant byte first. Machines differ: x86 is little-endian, some ARM configurations and older SPARC are big-endian. If a program wrote a 16-bit port number straight from memory, two different architectures would interpret it differently, so socket APIs require conversion with htons()/htonl() before sending and ntohs()/ntohl() after receiving. This is exactly the sort of translation the presentation layer is meant to handle.

DNS — the Internet's phone book

DNS maps human-readable names to IP addresses using a distributed, hierarchical database. No single server holds everything; authority is delegated downward.

. (root) | +-----------+-----------+ com org in <- TLD servers | | google ac.in | | www iiitkottayam | www FQDN: www.google.com. (the trailing dot is the root)

Resolution, step by step

  1. Browser cache → OS cache → /etc/hosts file. If found, done.
  2. The stub resolver asks the configured recursive resolver (your ISP's, or 8.8.8.8).
  3. The resolver asks a root server: "who knows .com?" Root replies with the TLD server addresses (a referral).
  4. The resolver asks the .com TLD server, which refers it to google.com's authoritative nameservers.
  5. The resolver asks the authoritative server, which returns the A record.
  6. The resolver caches the answer for its TTL and returns it to the client.

The client's query is recursive ("give me the final answer"); the resolver's queries to root, TLD and authoritative servers are iterative ("tell me who to ask next").

Alice's client --SMTP--> Alice's mail server --SMTP--> Bob's mail server | Bob's client <--POP3/IMAP-- SMTP = push protocol, sends mail (port 25, 587 submission, 465 SMTPS) POP3 = pull protocol, downloads and usually deletes from server (110, 995 secure) IMAP = pull protocol, keeps mail on the server, syncs state, folders (143, 993 secure)

POP3 suits one device and offline reading; IMAP suits multiple devices because read/unread status, folders and drafts live on the server. Attachments are encoded with MIME, since SMTP was designed for 7-bit ASCII — Base64 encoding costs about 33% overhead.

FTP

FTP is unusual because it uses two TCP connections: a control connection on port 21 that stays open for commands, and a separate data connection for each transfer.

  • Active mode — the client tells the server which port to connect back to, and the server initiates the data connection from port 20. Breaks through client-side NAT and firewalls.
  • Passive mode — the server opens a port and tells the client to connect to it. Works through NAT, and is the default in practice.

FTP is plaintext, including credentials. Use SFTP (file transfer over SSH, port 22) or FTPS (FTP with TLS). TFTP is a stripped-down UDP variant with no authentication, used for PXE network boot and switch firmware.

Remote access and management

  • Telnet (23) — plaintext remote terminal. Obsolete for real use, but still handy for testing whether a TCP port is open.
  • SSH (22) — encrypted shell with public-key authentication, port forwarding and tunnelling. Also carries SCP and SFTP.
  • SNMP (161/162) — managers poll agents for values in a MIB tree using GET/SET; agents send unsolicited traps on events. v3 added authentication and encryption; v1/v2c used cleartext community strings.
  • NTP (123) — hierarchical time sync using strata; stratum 0 is a reference clock, stratum 1 servers attach directly to it.

WebSockets and REST

HTTP is request-response, so the server cannot push. WebSocket upgrades an HTTP connection (Connection: Upgrade, status 101) into a persistent, full-duplex, low-overhead channel — used for chat, live dashboards and multiplayer. Older workarounds were polling, long polling and Server-Sent Events (one-way server → client).

REST is an architectural style, not a protocol: resources identified by URIs, manipulated with standard HTTP methods, stateless requests, and representations in JSON or XML. Contrast with RPC/gRPC, which models remote function calls, uses Protocol Buffers over HTTP/2, and supports streaming.

Interview questions · Application layer
Explain DNS resolution end to end.guaranteed

The browser checks its own cache, then the OS cache and hosts file. On a miss the stub resolver sends a recursive query to the configured resolver. That resolver, if it has nothing cached, queries a root server, which refers it to the .com TLD servers; the TLD servers refer it to the domain's authoritative nameservers; the authoritative server returns the A record. The resolver caches the answer for its TTL and replies to the client. The client's single query is recursive; the resolver's onward queries are iterative.

Why does DNS use UDP, and when does it use TCP?very common

Queries and responses are tiny and the exchange is a single round trip, so UDP avoids the three-way handshake that would double or triple the latency of every name lookup; if a packet is lost, retrying is cheaper than maintaining connection state on a server handling millions of queries. DNS switches to TCP when the response exceeds 512 bytes (the classic UDP limit, or 4096 with EDNS0) — which happens with DNSSEC and large record sets — and for zone transfers between primary and secondary servers, where reliability and ordering are essential.

Difference between HTTP and HTTPS.

HTTPS is HTTP carried inside a TLS session on port 443 instead of plaintext on port 80. TLS provides confidentiality (an eavesdropper sees only ciphertext), integrity (tampering is detected by a MAC), and authentication (the server presents an X.509 certificate signed by a CA, proving it owns the domain). The HTTP semantics — methods, headers, status codes — are unchanged.

GET vs POST.very common

GET requests a resource with parameters in the URL query string; it is safe, idempotent, cacheable, bookmarkable, and limited by practical URL length. POST sends data in the request body; it is neither safe nor idempotent, is not cached by default, has no size limit, and is used to create resources or trigger side effects. Sensitive data should never go in a GET query string because it lands in browser history, server logs and the Referer header.

What is the difference between 401 and 403? And between 301 and 302?

401 Unauthorized means you have not authenticated — send credentials and try again. 403 Forbidden means the server knows who you are and you still may not have it, so retrying with the same credentials will not help. 301 Moved Permanently tells clients and search engines to update their links and is cached aggressively; 302 Found is temporary and the client should keep using the original URL. Note 301/302 historically let clients change POST to GET; 308 and 307 were added to preserve the original method.

What problem does HTTP/2 solve, and what does it not solve?

HTTP/1.1 could only have one outstanding request per connection in practice, so browsers opened six connections per host and still queued resources — application-level head-of-line blocking. HTTP/2 multiplexes many independent streams over one binary-framed connection and compresses headers with HPACK, removing that. What it does not solve is TCP-level head-of-line blocking: a single lost TCP segment stalls delivery of every stream, because TCP must deliver bytes in order. HTTP/3 fixes that by moving to QUIC over UDP, where each stream is independently ordered.

SMTP vs POP3 vs IMAP.

SMTP is a push protocol used to send mail from client to server and between servers, on port 25 (587 for authenticated submission). POP3 and IMAP are pull protocols used to retrieve mail. POP3 downloads messages and typically deletes them from the server, suiting a single device with offline access. IMAP leaves messages on the server and synchronises folders, flags and drafts, so several devices see identical state — which is why it is the default today.

Why does FTP use two connections, and what is passive mode?

FTP separates control from data: a persistent control connection on port 21 carries commands and replies, while a fresh data connection is opened for each file transfer or directory listing. That keeps the command channel responsive during long transfers and lets the connection be aborted independently. In active mode the server initiates the data connection back to a port the client named — which a client-side NAT or firewall usually blocks. In passive mode the server opens a listening port and the client connects to it, so all connections are outbound from the client, which is why passive is the default today.

What is a CDN and how does it work with DNS?

A Content Delivery Network is a geographically distributed set of caching servers that store copies of static content close to users. When you resolve a CDN-backed hostname, the CDN's authoritative DNS returns the address of an edge server chosen by your resolver's location, network conditions and server load — or an anycast address that routing naturally sends to the nearest point of presence. The result is lower latency, less origin load, and absorption of traffic spikes and volumetric attacks.

How does a WebSocket connection get established?

The client sends an ordinary HTTP GET with Upgrade: websocket, Connection: Upgrade and a random Sec-WebSocket-Key. If the server supports it, it replies with 101 Switching Protocols and a Sec-WebSocket-Accept value derived from that key. The same TCP connection then stops speaking HTTP and becomes a persistent full-duplex frame-based channel with very small per-message overhead. It starts as HTTP specifically so it can traverse existing proxies and firewalls on ports 80 and 443.

What is the difference between a proxy and a reverse proxy?

A forward proxy sits in front of clients and makes requests on their behalf — used for content filtering, caching, anonymity and corporate policy; the server sees the proxy's address. A reverse proxy sits in front of servers and receives requests on their behalf — used for load balancing, TLS termination, caching, compression and hiding backend topology; the client sees the reverse proxy's address. Nginx and HAProxy are common reverse proxies.

Explain 304 Not Modified.

When a cached resource's freshness lifetime expires, the browser does not blindly re-download it. It sends a conditional request with If-None-Match: <etag> or If-Modified-Since: <date>. If the resource has not changed, the server replies 304 with headers only and no body, and the browser reuses its cached copy while resetting the freshness timer. This saves the entire payload while still guaranteeing correctness.

Keeping data safe in transit

Security rests on four goals, easily remembered as CIA + A: Confidentiality (only the intended party can read it), Integrity (nobody altered it), Availability (the service stays reachable) and Authentication (you are talking to who you think). Non-repudiation — the sender cannot later deny sending — is a fifth that digital signatures provide.

Hashing, MAC and digital signatures

  • Hash function (SHA-256, SHA-3) — one-way, fixed-length digest. Properties needed: preimage resistance, second-preimage resistance and collision resistance. Provides integrity only — an attacker who changes the data can recompute the hash. MD5 and SHA-1 are broken for collision resistance.
  • MAC / HMAC — a hash keyed with a shared secret, so only someone with the key can produce a valid tag. Gives integrity and authentication, but not non-repudiation, because both parties hold the same key.
  • Digital signature — hash the message and encrypt the hash with your private key. Anyone can verify with your public key. Gives integrity, authentication and non-repudiation.

Digital certificates and PKI

A public key alone proves nothing about identity. An X.509 certificate binds a public key to a domain name and is signed by a Certificate Authority. Your browser ships with a store of trusted root CAs; a server certificate is validated by walking the chain from the leaf up through intermediates to a trusted root, checking each signature, the validity dates, the subject name against the URL, and revocation status via CRL or OCSP.

Interview questions · Security
Symmetric vs asymmetric encryption — and why does TLS use both?very common

Symmetric uses one shared key and is extremely fast, but distributing that key securely to a stranger is the unsolved part. Asymmetric solves distribution — anyone can encrypt to your published public key — but is orders of magnitude slower and unsuitable for bulk data. TLS combines them: asymmetric cryptography during the handshake to authenticate the server and agree on a random session key, then AES symmetric encryption for every byte of application data afterwards.

Explain the TLS handshake.very common

The client sends ClientHello with its supported cipher suites, a random value and the target hostname (SNI). The server replies with ServerHello choosing a cipher suite, its own random, and its X.509 certificate chain. The client validates the chain against its trusted root store, checks the hostname and expiry, then performs the key exchange — with ECDHE both sides contribute ephemeral values and derive a shared secret neither transmitted. Both derive identical session keys, exchange Finished messages that are already encrypted and cover a hash of the whole handshake (so tampering is detected), and then application data flows under symmetric encryption. TLS 1.3 compresses this to a single round trip by having the client guess the key-exchange parameters in its first message.

What is forward secrecy?

The property that compromising a server's long-term private key does not let an attacker decrypt previously recorded sessions. It comes from using ephemeral key exchange (DHE or ECDHE): the session key is derived from temporary values discarded after the session, so the long-term key is used only to sign the exchange, never to encrypt it. With old RSA key transport, an attacker who recorded traffic for years and later stole the private key could decrypt all of it retroactively.

Difference between hashing, encryption and encoding.trap question

Encoding (Base64, URL encoding) transforms data into another representation for safe transport — it is fully reversible by anyone and provides zero security. Encryption is reversible only with the correct key and provides confidentiality. Hashing is one-way and irreversible by design, producing a fixed-length digest used for integrity checking and password storage. If someone says "we encrypt passwords", the correct answer is that passwords should be hashed with a slow salted algorithm such as bcrypt or Argon2, not encrypted.

How does a digital signature work and what does it give you that a MAC does not?

The sender hashes the message and encrypts the digest with their private key; anyone can decrypt it with the public key and compare against their own hash of the message. That proves integrity and authenticates the sender. A MAC also gives integrity and authentication, but because both parties share the same secret key, either could have produced the tag — so a MAC cannot prove to a third party who created it. Digital signatures add non-repudiation.

What is a DDoS attack and how do you defend against one?

A distributed denial of service floods a target from many sources — usually a botnet — to exhaust bandwidth, connection tables or CPU. Types include volumetric (raw traffic, often amplified by reflecting off open DNS or NTP servers), protocol attacks (SYN floods exhausting connection state) and application-layer attacks (expensive HTTP requests). Defences: SYN cookies, connection and request rate limiting, anycast to spread traffic across many points of presence, CDN and scrubbing services that absorb and filter, blackhole routing as a last resort, and capacity headroom. Because the sources are distributed and often spoofed, blocking by IP is largely ineffective.

What is a VPN and how does IPsec tunnel mode differ from transport mode?

A VPN creates an encrypted logical link over a public network so remote hosts behave as if on the same private LAN. IPsec transport mode encrypts only the payload and keeps the original IP header, so it is used host to host where the endpoints themselves are the peers. Tunnel mode encrypts the entire original packet, header included, and prepends a new IP header — so it hides the internal addressing and is what gateways use for site-to-site VPNs.

What is DNS cache poisoning and how does DNSSEC help?

An attacker races the legitimate authoritative server to deliver a forged response to a recursive resolver; if it matches the query ID and port, the resolver caches the false mapping and serves it to every user for its TTL. DNSSEC adds a chain of cryptographic signatures from the root down, so a resolver can verify that a record really came from the zone's owner and was not modified. It provides authenticity and integrity but not confidentiality — that is what DNS over HTTPS and DNS over TLS add.

IDS vs IPS vs firewall.

A firewall enforces an explicit allow/deny policy at the network boundary, mostly on addresses, ports and connection state. An IDS monitors traffic (often from a mirror port, out of band) and raises alerts on signatures or anomalies, but cannot stop anything. An IPS does the same detection inline, so it can drop malicious packets in real time — powerful but risky, since a false positive blocks legitimate users. They complement each other: the firewall controls who may connect, the IDS/IPS inspects what they do.

Interview questions · Wireless & modern
Explain the hidden terminal problem and how RTS/CTS solves it.

Stations A and C are both in range of access point B but out of range of each other. Carrier sensing tells each of them the channel is free, so both transmit and their frames collide at B, while neither can detect it. RTS/CTS fixes this: A sends a short Request To Send, B replies with a Clear To Send that every station in B's range hears, including C. C reads the duration field in the CTS, sets its Network Allocation Vector and stays silent for that period. The cost is extra overhead, so RTS/CTS is usually enabled only for frames above a size threshold.

Why is 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi limited to channels 1, 6 and 11?

Each 802.11 channel occupies about 20–22 MHz, but the channel centres in the 2.4 GHz band are spaced only 5 MHz apart. Adjacent channels therefore overlap and interfere. Channels 1, 6 and 11 are spaced 25 MHz apart, which is just enough that their spectra do not overlap — so they are the only three that can be used simultaneously in the same area without degrading each other.

WEP vs WPA2 vs WPA3.

WEP used RC4 with a static shared key and a 24-bit initialisation vector that repeats within hours, letting an attacker recover the key from captured traffic — it is completely broken. WPA2 uses AES-CCMP with a 4-way handshake deriving fresh per-session keys, and remains secure with a strong passphrase, though the handshake can be captured for offline dictionary attack and was vulnerable to KRACK before patching. WPA3 replaces the pre-shared key handshake with SAE (a password-authenticated key exchange), giving forward secrecy and making offline guessing infeasible because each attempt requires a fresh interaction with the network.

What is SDN and why is separating the control plane useful?

Traditionally every router runs its own routing protocol and makes local decisions — the control plane and data plane live in the same box. SDN moves the control plane to a logically centralised controller that has a global view of the topology and pushes forwarding rules into simple, fast switches. That enables policy that is impossible to express hop by hop (traffic engineering, per-application paths), lets the network be configured through an API instead of device by device, and makes the data plane cheap commodity hardware. The trade-offs are controller scalability and the risk of a central point of failure.

How does a load balancer decide where to send a request?

Common algorithms: round robin (simple rotation), weighted round robin (bigger servers get more), least connections (send to the least busy), least response time, and IP hash or consistent hashing (the same client always reaches the same server, giving session affinity). A layer-4 balancer decides purely on the TCP 4-tuple and is fast; a layer-7 balancer terminates the connection and can route on URL path, headers or cookies, enabling per-service routing and TLS termination at the cost of more processing. Health checks remove failed backends from the pool automatically.

Synthesis

What happens when you type google.com

This single question is asked in almost every interview, because a good answer traverses the entire stack. Learn it as a sequence of 12 steps and you can stretch or compress it to fit the time you are given.

  1. URL parsing. The browser splits the input into scheme, host, port, path and query. If it is not a valid URL it goes to the default search engine instead. It also checks its HSTS list — if google.com is preloaded there, the request is forced to HTTPS before any traffic is sent.
  2. DNS resolution. Browser cache → OS cache → hosts file → recursive resolver. The resolver queries a root server, then the .com TLD servers, then Google's authoritative nameservers, returning an A or AAAA record. Everything is cached along the way for its TTL.
  3. ARP for the next hop. The host compares the destination IP with its own IP and subnet mask. Since 142.250.x.x is not local, the packet must go to the default gateway — so the host ARPs for the gateway's MAC, not Google's.
  4. TCP three-way handshake. SYN → SYN+ACK → ACK with the server on port 443. Sequence numbers are exchanged and the connection enters ESTABLISHED.
  5. TLS handshake. ClientHello with cipher suites and SNI, ServerHello with the chosen suite and certificate chain, certificate validation against the root store, ECDHE key agreement, Finished messages. Session keys are now in place.
  6. HTTP request. GET / HTTP/2 with Host, User-Agent, Accept, Accept-Encoding and any cookies for the domain.
  7. Routing across the Internet. Your home router performs NAT, rewriting the source to its public IP and a chosen port. Each router along the path does a longest-prefix-match lookup, decrements TTL, rebuilds the layer-2 frame for the next link and forwards. Between autonomous systems, BGP determined the path.
  8. The server side. The request likely lands on a CDN edge or load balancer, which may serve from cache or forward to an application server; that may query databases and caches before assembling a response.
  9. HTTP response. Status line, headers (Content-Type, Content-Encoding: gzip/br, Cache-Control, Set-Cookie) and the HTML body, streamed back over the same TCP connection under TLS.
  10. Rendering. The browser parses HTML into the DOM, encounters references to CSS, JavaScript, images and fonts, and fetches each — over the same HTTP/2 connection where possible. CSS builds the CSSOM, the two combine into a render tree, then layout and paint.
  11. JavaScript execution may trigger further XHR/fetch calls, each repeating steps 2 through 9 (usually skipping DNS and handshake thanks to caching and connection reuse).
  12. Connection teardown. When idle, the connection closes with FIN/ACK in both directions, and the initiating side sits in TIME_WAIT for 2×MSL.
How to deliver this answer

Start with the one-line map: "It's DNS, then TCP, then TLS, then HTTP, then render." Then say "shall I go deeper on any of those?" That shows structure and lets the interviewer steer to the part they actually want to test. Candidates who launch straight into DNS record types without the map often run out of time before reaching TLS.

Follow-ups you should expect
Where exactly is the first packet sent, and to whose MAC address?

Assuming the DNS server is outside the local subnet, the very first packet is the DNS query. Its destination IP is the resolver's address, but its destination MAC is the default gateway's MAC, obtained by ARP. This is the crux of the layering question — layer 3 destination is the final target, layer 2 destination is only the next hop.

How many round trips before the first byte of HTML arrives?

Roughly: 1 RTT for DNS if uncached (possibly several if the resolver has to walk the hierarchy), 1 RTT for the TCP handshake, 1–2 RTTs for TLS (1 with TLS 1.3, 2 with 1.2), then 1 RTT for the HTTP request and response. So about 4–5 RTTs cold, dropping to 1 RTT when DNS is cached and the connection is reused. This is exactly why HTTP/3, TLS 1.3 and connection reuse matter so much for perceived speed.

What changes if the site is on the same LAN?

The host computes destination IP AND subnet mask and finds it matches its own network, so no gateway is involved: it ARPs directly for the destination host's MAC and sends the frame straight to it through the switch. No routing, no NAT, no TTL decrement.

You get "server not found". How do you diagnose it, layer by layer?

Work bottom-up. Layer 1/2: is the interface up, is there link, does ipconfig show a real address rather than 169.254.x.x? Layer 3: can you ping your gateway, then ping 8.8.8.8 to test raw IP reachability? If IP works but names do not, it is DNS — test with nslookup google.com and try a different resolver. Layer 4: telnet host 443 to see whether the port is open or filtered. Layer 7: curl -v to see the actual HTTP exchange and any TLS certificate error. Naming that ordering is itself the answer they want.

Interview questions · Tools
How does traceroute work?very common

It exploits the TTL field. It sends a probe with TTL = 1; the first router decrements it to zero, discards the packet and returns an ICMP Time Exceeded, revealing its address. Then TTL = 2 reveals the second router, and so on until the probe reaches the destination, which replies with a Port Unreachable (UDP probes) or Echo Reply (ICMP probes). Three probes per hop give the RTT samples. Asterisks mean no reply — usually a router configured not to send ICMP, not necessarily a failure.

Ping works but the website does not load. What do you check?

Ping proves layer 3 reachability only. Next check DNS (dig or nslookup — maybe the name resolves to the wrong address or not at all), then check whether the specific TCP port is reachable (telnet host 443 or nc -zv) since a firewall may permit ICMP but block 443, then check the application with curl -v for a 5xx status, a redirect loop or a certificate error. It is a layer-by-layer elimination.

What does a socket in CLOSE_WAIT tell you?

The remote end sent a FIN and your TCP acknowledged it, but your application has not called close() on its socket. A pile of CLOSE_WAIT entries is almost always an application bug — a leaked file descriptor — not a network problem. By contrast, many TIME_WAIT entries are on the side that closed first and are normal on a busy client.

4. Stop-and-Wait efficiency: 1 Mbps link, 20 ms one-way propagation, 1000-byte frames. Find efficiency and throughput.

Transmission time Tt = 8000 bits ÷ 10⁶ bps = 8 ms. Propagation Tp = 20 ms, so a = Tp/Tt = 2.5.
Efficiency η = 1/(1 + 2a) = 1/(1 + 5) = 16.67%.
Throughput = η × bandwidth = 0.1667 × 1 Mbps ≈ 166.7 kbps.
Follow-up: what window size gives 100%? Need N ≥ 1 + 2a = 6 frames.

5. Go-Back-N with window 7 on the same link — efficiency?

η = N / (1 + 2a) = 7 / 6 → capped at 100%, because the window is larger than the pipe. Throughput = full 1 Mbps. If the window were 4, η = 4/6 = 66.7%.

6. Sequence-number bits: a Selective Repeat protocol needs a window of 8. Minimum bits?

For Selective Repeat, window ≤ 2ⁿ⁻¹, so 8 ≤ 2ⁿ⁻¹ → 2ⁿ⁻¹ ≥ 8 → n − 1 ≥ 3 → n = 4 bits (sequence numbers 0–15). For Go-Back-N with window 8 the requirement is 8 ≤ 2ⁿ − 1, so n = 4 also works there (window up to 15).

11. How long to send a 20 MB file over a 100 Mbps link with 30 ms RTT, ignoring congestion control?

20 MB = 20 × 8 × 10⁶ = 160 Mb. Transmission = 160/100 = 1.6 s, plus one RTT of 30 ms for handshake and one propagation delay for the last bit ≈ 1.63 s. With TCP slow start the real answer is larger, since the first few RTTs carry far less than 100 Mbps — a common follow-up.

Ten questions that separate good candidates
Why does TCP need sequence numbers if IP delivers packets in order most of the time?

Because IP makes no such promise. Packets can be reordered when routes change mid-flow or when load balancing spreads a flow across multiple paths, they can be duplicated by retransmitting network elements, and they can be lost. Sequence numbers let the receiver reassemble the byte stream correctly, detect duplicates and identify gaps. They also serve as the basis for acknowledgements and for connection-establishment safety against old duplicate segments.

TCP guarantees delivery — so why do my messages still get lost in production?

TCP guarantees delivery into the receiving kernel's socket buffer, not into your application's logic or storage. If the process crashes after the kernel acknowledged the bytes but before it processed them, the data is gone while the sender believes it succeeded. This is why application-level acknowledgements, idempotency keys and durable message queues exist. It is a favourite senior-level follow-up because it tests whether you understand what a guarantee actually covers.

Why is UDP used for video streaming when nobody wants a corrupted video?

For live streaming and calls, a retransmitted frame arrives after its playback deadline, so it is useless — better to conceal the loss and keep the stream moving than to stall the whole pipeline waiting for it, which is what TCP would do. Codecs are also designed to degrade gracefully. Note the nuance: on-demand video (YouTube, Netflix) actually does use TCP or QUIC over HTTP, because buffering ahead removes the deadline problem. So the honest answer is "UDP for real-time, TCP/QUIC for buffered playback".

Two hosts have the same IP on one LAN. What happens?

An IP conflict. ARP resolution becomes non-deterministic — whichever host replies first (or last) populates each peer's cache, so traffic goes to the wrong machine intermittently and both connections behave erratically. Modern stacks perform gratuitous ARP or ARP probe at startup to detect this, log a conflict and may refuse to use the address. DHCP prevents it by leasing addresses and probing before assignment.

Why does adding more bandwidth not always make a website faster?

Because most page loads are latency-bound, not bandwidth-bound. A cold load spends several round trips on DNS, the TCP handshake and the TLS handshake before a single byte of HTML arrives, and those round trips do not shrink when the pipe widens. Beyond roughly 5–10 Mbps the load time curve flattens almost completely, while cutting RTT keeps improving it linearly. That is the whole motivation behind CDNs, TLS 1.3, connection reuse and HTTP/3.

What is head-of-line blocking, and at which layers does it occur?

It is when the first item in a queue blocks everything behind it even though those items are ready. At the application layer, HTTP/1.1 could only process one request at a time per connection. At the transport layer, TCP must deliver bytes in order, so one lost segment stalls every multiplexed HTTP/2 stream sharing that connection. HTTP/2 solved the first, HTTP/3 over QUIC solved the second by giving each stream its own ordering. It also occurs at layer 2 in switches with input queuing.

If IPv6 has been ready since 1998, why is IPv4 still everywhere?

Because NAT removed the urgency. Address exhaustion was the driving pressure, and NAT let an entire organisation live behind a single public address, so the business case for a costly, risky migration evaporated. IPv6 is not backward compatible — an IPv6-only host cannot talk to an IPv4-only server without translation — so the transition requires dual stack everywhere, which means running and debugging two networks. Adoption is real but gradual, driven mainly by mobile carriers and large content providers who genuinely ran out of addresses.

Your application is slow. How do you tell whether it is the network or the server?

Separate the components. Ping gives baseline RTT — if that is already high, it is the path. curl -w breaks a request into DNS lookup, TCP connect, TLS handshake, time to first byte and total transfer: a high TTFB with fast connect means the server is thinking, while a slow transfer after a fast TTFB means bandwidth or loss. Traceroute or MTR shows where latency and loss enter the path. Comparing from a second location, or against a static file on the same server, isolates application logic from the network almost immediately.

Explain layering to a non-technical interviewer in thirty seconds.

"Sending data over a network is like posting a letter. You write the message — that's the application. You put it in an envelope with a name so the right person opens it — that's transport. You write the full postal address so the post office can route it — that's the network layer. The postal service moves it van by van between sorting offices, and each van only cares about the next stop — that's the link layer. And the roads and vehicles themselves are the physical layer. Each stage only needs to understand its own job, which is why we can change the roads without rewriting the letter."

What would you say if you do not know the answer?

Say what you do know, reason toward the boundary of it out loud, and then say plainly that you are not certain beyond that point. "I know DNS uses UDP 53 and falls back to TCP for large responses — I'd guess DNSSEC responses are large enough to trigger that often, but I haven't verified it." That is a far stronger answer than a confident guess, because interviewers are testing calibration as much as recall. Never invent a mechanism.
