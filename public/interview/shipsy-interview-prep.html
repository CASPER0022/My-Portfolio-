Section 6Behavioural questions

Answer all of these with STAR: Situation (10s), Task (5s), Action (30s — the bulk), Result (10s). If you forget everything else, remember that the Action is the part they care about, and that every answer must end with a result.

QTell me about a difficult technical problem you solved.
SAY THIS

Situation. In LegalEase, my RAG system was returning answers that sounded confident but were based on the wrong sections of the law.

Task. I had to figure out whether the problem was the model or the retrieval.

Action. I stopped looking at the final answers and started printing the retrieved chunks for each question. The chunks were the problem — they were being cut in the middle, so a section number was in one chunk and its actual content was in another. I made two changes. I moved to larger semantic chunks of about 1200 characters so a whole article stayed together, and I added a second stage — FlashRank re-ranking — which takes the top results from the vector search and re-scores them against the actual question, so weak matches get pushed out.

Result. The answers became grounded in the right sections, and when I measured it with RAGAS I got around 92% faithfulness and 88% answer relevance.

The hidden message: "I stopped looking at the output and looked at the intermediate step" is exactly the debugging instinct a support engineer needs. Say that sentence clearly — it's the best line in the answer.
Likely follow-ups
  • "What is faithfulness in RAGAS?" → Whether the answer is actually supported by the retrieved documents, rather than invented. Relevance is whether the answer addresses the question asked.
  • "How did you know 92% was good?" → Be honest — it was measured on a test set of questions I wrote against documents where I knew the correct source. It's an internal benchmark, not an industry standard.

Section 7Support & escalation playbook

This is the section that decides your interview. Learn the framework below well enough to use it without naming it. Almost every scenario question they ask can be answered by walking through these five steps.

ARISE — your five steps for any customer issue

THE STEP THAT WINS THE INTERVIEW

Most candidates stop at S. Always add the E. "And then I'd write it up so the next person doesn't have to rediscover it, and if it's the third time we've seen it, I'd look at automating the diagnosis." That single sentence connects Phase 1 to Phase 2 and is the whole point of this role.

"Severity isn't the same as the customer's urgency. A customer will often say everything is P1, and that's fair — from where they sit it is. My job isn't to argue about the label. It's to understand the business impact underneath it, and then be honest about what we're doing first and why."

This is a mature answer that very few candidates give.

What to collect before you escalate

If they ask "what information would you gather," this list is your answer. Learn six of them; you don't need all thirteen.

  • Who — client/tenant name, the specific user, their role and permissions.
  • What — exactly what they did, what they expected, what actually happened.
  • Identifiers — order ID, tracking/AWB number, request or trace ID, ticket ID.
  • When — exact timestamp with timezone. In a global product this is often the whole bug.
  • Where — environment, region, web or mobile, app version, browser.
  • Evidence — screenshot or screen recording, the API request and response, error message verbatim, relevant log lines.
  • Frequency — once, always, or intermittent? What percentage of attempts?
  • Blast radius — one user, one client, or everyone? How many orders affected?
  • Started when — and did anything change around then? A deploy, a config change, a new integration on their side?
  • Business impact — in their words. "3,000 shipments can't be dispatched" is more useful than "high priority."
  • What you already ruled out — so engineering doesn't repeat your work.
  • Workaround status — is the customer currently blocked or limping along?
  • Your best hypothesis — even if uncertain. Say it as a hypothesis.
THE LINE TO REMEMBER

"A good escalation should let an engineer start debugging in two minutes instead of spending twenty asking me questions."

When to escalate

  • It's P1 and there's no workaround — escalate immediately, don't investigate alone first.
  • It needs a code change, a data fix, or access you don't have.
  • Data integrity or security is involved — wrong data shown to the wrong client, anything that looks like a leak. Escalate instantly, never sit on it.
  • You're at risk of breaching an SLA — escalate before the breach, not after.
  • You've genuinely run out of hypotheses. Set yourself a time box.
  • The customer has asked for a manager, or the account is at risk.

The principle to state: "I'd rather escalate slightly early with good information than late with a complete investigation. The cost of escalating early is a bit of someone's time. The cost of escalating late is a customer who stopped trusting us."

How to communicate while you're still investigating

Three rules. Say them in this order and you'll sound experienced:

  1. Update on a schedule, not on progress. If you only write when you have news, silence feels like being ignored. Promise a next update time and hit it — even if the update is "no change yet."
  2. Say what you know, what you don't know, and what's next. Never fill the gap with a guess dressed as a fact.
  3. Never give an ETA you're not confident in. Give a next-update time instead. "I'll come back to you by 4pm with either a fix or a clearer estimate" is a promise you can keep.
SAMPLE UPDATE MESSAGE — USE THIS SHAPE

"Hi — quick update. We've reproduced the issue and confirmed it's affecting bulk uploads with more than 500 rows, not all uploads. We haven't identified the root cause yet; our engineering team is looking at it now.

In the meantime, uploads under 500 rows are processing normally, so splitting the file will let your team keep dispatching today.

I'll update you again by 4pm even if there's no change."

Notice the structure: what we know → what we don't → what unblocks you now → when I'll write next. Four sentences. If they ask you to draft a customer message live, use exactly this shape.

Q1How would you handle an angry customer?
SAY THIS

The first thing I'd remind myself is that the anger is about the situation, not about me. If their operations are stuck, being angry is a reasonable response.

So I'd let them finish without interrupting, and then acknowledge the impact in their own terms — not "sorry for the inconvenience," but something like "so your dispatch team has been blocked since this morning and you've got 400 orders waiting." When someone hears their problem said back accurately, the temperature drops immediately.

Then I'd move to what I can control. What I know so far, what I'm doing next, and when I'll come back to them. Even without a fix, that gives them something solid.

The one thing I wouldn't do is get defensive or start explaining why it isn't our fault. Even if that's true, it's the wrong conversation to have while they're still blocked.

Likely follow-ups
  • "What if they're rude to you personally?" → "I'd stay professional and keep the focus on the problem. If it became abusive I'd loop in my manager, but in my experience most of that heat disappears once someone feels heard."
  • "What if it genuinely is their mistake?" → Fix first, explain later, and explain without blame: "The upload failed because the date column was in a different format — here's the corrected file, and I've also raised that our error message should have said that clearly."
Q2A customer reports a bug. Walk me through what you do.
SAY THIS

I'd acknowledge it first so they know it's picked up. Then I'd get the specifics — what exactly they did, what they expected, what happened instead, and the identifiers: order ID, timestamp with timezone, which user, and a screenshot or the error message.

Then I'd try to reproduce it myself, first with their exact data. If I can reproduce it, the rest is straightforward — I'd narrow down which layer it's failing at by checking whether the request even reached our API, what the API returned, and what the logs say for that request.

Once I know the cause, I'd think about two timelines: what unblocks them today, and what the permanent fix is. Those aren't always the same thing, and the first one matters more to them.

Then I'd close the loop — tell them what actually caused it in plain language, and write it up internally so the next person who sees it doesn't start from zero.

This is just ARISE said naturally. Notice you never named the framework. That's the goal.
Q3You can't reproduce the issue. What now?
SAY THIS

The first thing I'd accept is that it's still real for them — "can't reproduce" is information about my environment, not proof there's no bug.

So I'd look for what's different between us. Usually it's one of a handful of things: their data, their user's role and permissions, their configuration, their app or browser version, the region or timezone, or the load at that moment. I'd try to reproduce with their exact record and their role rather than my test data, because that's where the difference usually lives.

If I still can't, I'd stop guessing and go to the evidence — find the request in the logs by timestamp or trace ID and see what actually happened on our side. If it's intermittent, I'd ask them to send the exact time it next occurs, or get a screen recording with the browser console open.

And I'd be honest with them: "I haven't been able to reproduce it yet, which is why I'm asking for these specific details" is much better than going quiet.

  • "Give me an example of an environment difference." → Timezone is the classic one in logistics: an SLA breach report can look wrong purely because the client's ops are in Dubai and the timestamps are stored in UTC.
Q4How would you troubleshoot an issue that only happens on the customer's side?
SAY THIS

I'd work from the outside in, and try to split the path in half each time rather than checking everything.

The first question is: did the request even reach us? If it's in our logs, the problem is on our side. If it isn't, it's their integration, authentication, network, or something like rate limiting.

If it did reach us, I'd check what we returned. If we returned a correct response and they still see something wrong, then it's on their side or in the display layer — caching, a stale page, a mapping issue in how they consume our fields.

If we returned an error, I'd look at what was in the request. In my experience the most common causes are data-shaped: a field missing or in a different format, a duplicate ID, a value that's valid for them but not for us.

Beyond that I'd specifically check the things that differ per customer — their configuration, their permissions and roles, their custom fields, their app version, and their timezone.

The key phrase: "did the request even reach us?" It's the single most useful first question in integration support and it makes you sound like you've done this before.
Q5You don't know the cause of the issue. What do you do?
SAY THIS

I'd try to reduce the space rather than find the answer straight away.

First I'd establish what changed. Most things that break were working recently, so I'd ask when it started and what happened around then — a deploy on our side, a config change, a new integration or a data upload on theirs.

Then I'd narrow the pattern. Is it all users or one user, all orders or one type, all the time or only at certain hours? Every one of those questions cuts the possibilities roughly in half.

I'd also give myself a time limit — maybe 30 to 45 minutes for something urgent — and if I'm not closer by then, I'd bring in someone who knows that part of the system, with everything I've already ruled out. Sitting alone with a P1 because you want to solve it yourself is ego, not ownership.

That last sentence is a strong line. Say it. It shows self-awareness that a manager will trust.
Q6How would you handle a critical escalation from a large client?
SAY THIS

For a genuine P1 I'd do three things almost at once.

Acknowledge to the customer immediately and confirm we're on it — a fast acknowledgement is worth a lot before you know anything. Pull in the right people straight away rather than investigating alone first. And establish a single owner for the communication, so the client isn't hearing three different versions from three people.

Then, in parallel with the fix, I'd look for anything that reduces the damage now — a workaround, a manual process, disabling one feature so the rest keeps working. For a logistics client, if the goods can keep moving today, the pressure drops enormously even before the bug is fixed.

I'd keep updates on a fixed cadence, maybe every 30 minutes, even when there's nothing new.

And once it's resolved, I'd make sure the root cause analysis actually happens — what broke, why it wasn't caught, and what monitoring would have caught it before the customer called us.

Likely follow-ups

  • "Who do you communicate with during a P1?" → The customer, your manager, the engineering owner, and account management if it's a large account. One voice to the customer.
Q7How would you prioritise five urgent tickets at once?
SAY THIS

I'd rank on two things: how badly the customer is blocked, and whether there's a workaround.

So a whole warehouse unable to dispatch, with no workaround, goes first — that's revenue stopping right now. Something affecting many users but with a manual workaround comes next. A single user blocked completely can outrank a hundred users mildly inconvenienced, if that one user is the only person who can approve dispatches.

Then I'd adjust for two things: contractual SLAs, because some clients have committed response times, and anything touching data integrity or security, which jumps the queue regardless of how few people it affects.

The part I wouldn't skip is telling the other four. A short message saying "I'm on a critical issue, I'll be with you by 3pm" costs a minute and prevents four more escalations.

Q8What if you can't fix the issue immediately? How do you tell the customer?

I've found people accept "this will take two days, here's how to work around it" far better than a vague "we're working on it" that turns into two days of silence.

Q9The customer says it's a bug. You think they're using the product wrong. What do you do?
SAY THIS

This is a maturity test. The wrong answer is "I'd explain that it's user error." The right answer treats confusion as a product signal.

Q11A customer says "your system is showing wrong data." How do you start?
SAY THIS

My first question is always "wrong compared to what?" Usually they're comparing our screen to something else — their own ERP, a report they exported yesterday, or what a driver told them on the phone.

Q12A client's integration suddenly stopped working. Nothing changed on our side. What do you check?
SAY THIS

I'd be a bit sceptical of "nothing changed on either side," because something usually did. So I'd establish exactly when it stopped, and then check what happened around that time.

On our side: any deploy, config change, or certificate or credential that expired. Expired API keys and certificates are a classic cause of "suddenly stopped" with no code change anywhere.

On their side: a new version of their system, a changed field, a firewall or IP allowlist change, or a volume increase that pushed them into rate limiting.

Then I'd look at the actual failure. The HTTP status code tells you a lot immediately — a 401 or 403 points at authentication or permissions, a 429 is rate limiting, a 400 means the request itself is malformed, and a 500 means it's ours. That one number usually decides who needs to fix it.

Know these cold
  • 200 success · 201 created · 400 bad request, malformed data · 401 not authenticated · 403 authenticated but not allowed · 404 not found · 409 conflict, often a duplicate · 429 too many requests · 500 our server errored · 502/503/504 gateway or timeout, service unreachable or too slow.
Q13The same issue has come up five times from different clients. What would you do?
SAY THIS

And if the permanent fix is going to take time, I'd reduce the cost of it in the meantime — a documented resolution so anyone on the team can close it in two minutes, and ideally an automated check or an alert that catches it before the customer notices.

Honestly, that repetition is exactly the input for the automation side of this role. Five identical tickets is a specification for what to build.

Q14How do you make sure you don't miss an SLA?
SAY THIS

Q15What does good customer support mean to you?
SAY THIS

The second one is what separates support that scales from support that just runs faster. If you only close tickets, the queue grows with the customer base. If every ticket also produces a doc, an alert, or an automation, the queue stops growing at the same rate.

Q16A customer wants a feature we don't have and won't take no for an answer.

Q17You made a mistake that affected a customer. What do you do?

Section 9Using AI to find and fix bugs

Your senior specifically flagged this. Shipsy's JD names ChatGPT, Claude, Cursor and GitHub Copilot as tools they use daily. They want someone who uses AI seriously and knows where it will burn them.

The seven ways you'd actually use AI in support work

  1. Reading logs fast. Paste a large log dump and ask for the anomalies, the error clusters, and the sequence of events around a timestamp. This is where AI saves the most time.
  2. Understanding unfamiliar code. In a codebase you didn't write, asking "what does this function do and what calls it" cuts hours off the first weeks.
  3. Generating hypotheses. Give it the symptom, the error, and the context, and ask for five possible causes ranked by likelihood. You still test them — but it stops you tunnelling on your first idea.
  4. Writing throwaway tooling. A regex to slice logs, a SQL query to find affected orders, a script that reproduces the failing request. Small, verifiable, high value.
  5. Drafting customer communication. Rewrite a technical explanation into plain language for a client. You always edit it, but the blank page is the slow part.
  6. Searching past tickets. Semantic search over resolved tickets to find whether this has happened before — keyword search misses it when the customer phrased it differently.
  7. Writing the RCA draft. Give it the timeline and the findings, get a structured first draft, then correct it.
QHow would you use AI to investigate and solve a bug?
SAY THIS

I use AI mainly to speed up the parts that are slow but not hard. The biggest one is logs — if I have hundreds of lines around the time of a failure, I'll give them to a model and ask what stands out and what the sequence of events was. That takes minutes off something that would take much longer to read manually.

The second is unfamiliar code. In a large codebase I didn't write, I'll ask it to explain what a function does and what calls it, so I can find the right place to look.

The third is hypotheses. I'll describe the symptom and the error and ask for the five most likely causes, ranked. That's useful mainly because it stops me getting fixated on my first idea.

But I treat everything it gives me as a hypothesis, not an answer. I verify against the actual code and the actual logs before I act on it, because a model will state a wrong cause just as confidently as a right one. It narrows the search — it doesn't end it.

That last paragraph is the part they're testing for. Someone who says "I'd ask ChatGPT and apply the fix" is a risk to enterprise clients. Say the verification part clearly.
Likely follow-ups
  • "What would you never put into an AI tool?" → Customer data, PII, API keys and credentials, anything under an NDA, unless it's an approved internal tool with the right data agreements. Say this — it's a real concern for a company serving Aramex and Reliance.
  • "Does AI ever slow you down?" → Yes — when the problem is genuinely novel, chasing a plausible-sounding wrong explanation costs more time than reading the code would have. Good honest answer.
QAI suggests a solution but you're not sure it's correct. What do you do?
SAY THIS

I'd treat it as a claim to be checked, and check it in a specific order.

First, does the explanation actually match the code and the logs in front of me? Models will confidently reference a function or a config option that doesn't exist in our system. If I can't find the thing it's describing, the answer is wrong regardless of how reasonable it sounds.

Second, does it explain all the symptoms? If the customer says it fails only for large uploads and the suggested cause would make every upload fail, it's not the cause.

Third, I'd test it — reproduce the failure, apply the change in a safe environment, and confirm the failing case now passes and nothing else broke.

And if it touches production data or anything I can't reverse, I'd have someone senior review it before it goes anywhere near a customer. For an enterprise client I'd much rather be an hour slower and right.

The "does it explain all the symptoms?" test is a genuinely sharp point and very few candidates will make it. It shows you reason about evidence, not just tools.
QWhat are the risks of using AI in customer support?
SAY THIS

Three main ones, I think.

Hallucination is the obvious one — a support bot confidently telling a customer a feature exists when it doesn't, or giving a wrong procedure. That's worse than no answer, because the customer acts on it.

The second is data. Customer data going into external tools, or a bot answering one client's question with another client's data because the retrieval wasn't scoped per tenant. I ran into exactly that class of problem at Klyonix, which is why I moved access control into the LLM layer.

The third is subtler — using automation on problems that should have been fixed properly. If the same issue happens weekly and we build a bot to explain it faster, we've made the symptom cheaper instead of removing the cause.

The way I'd manage all three is to keep a human in the loop for anything customer-facing at first, make the system cite its source so an agent can check it in one click, and let it say "I don't know" rather than guess.

Casper — autonomous research agent

QTell me about Casper.
SAY THIS — 30-SECOND PITCH

Casper is an autonomous agent that answers research questions by searching the web itself, instead of answering from what the model already knows.

The difference from a normal chatbot is that it runs in a loop. It decides what to search for, runs the search, reads the results, and then decides whether it has enough to answer or needs another search. When it's done, it writes a summary with the sources.

I built it with LangGraph on the backend and React on the frontend, and it streams its reasoning steps to the UI live so you can watch what it's doing rather than staring at a loading spinner.

Follow-ups you must be ready for
  • "Why LangGraph and not plain LangChain?" → "A normal LangChain chain runs in a straight line, start to finish. An agent needs to loop — search, check, maybe search again. LangGraph lets you build it as a graph with cycles and a shared state, so the agent can come back to the same node with more information. That looping is the whole point of an agent."
  • "What is the state in your graph?" → "The message history, a counter for how many loops it's done, and the maximum searches allowed. The counter and the budget are what stop it running forever."
  • "What's the hardest problem you hit?" → The infinite loop. Use the story from Section 6.
  • "Why SSE and not WebSockets?" → "The data only flows one way, from the agent to the browser. SSE is simpler for that and reconnects automatically. WebSockets would be the right choice if the user needed to send messages mid-run."
  • "What would you improve?" → See below.
QHow would you improve Casper?
SAY THIS

Three things. First, it trusts whatever the search returns — I'd add a check on source quality, so it weighs an official page higher than a random blog. Second, right now the reasoning is in memory for a run; I'd persist it properly so a long-running task could resume after a crash. Third, and probably most important, I have no automated way to measure whether an answer was actually good. I'd build an evaluation set the way I did for LegalEase, because without measurement you're just tuning prompts on vibes.

"Tuning prompts on vibes" is a nice honest phrase — but only use it if the interviewer's tone is relaxed. If they're formal, say "without a way to measure it, prompt changes are guesswork."
QA user says Casper gave a wrong answer. How would you debug it?
SAY THIS

I'd find where in the chain it went wrong, because "wrong answer" can come from three different places.

First I'd look at what it actually searched for. If the query it generated was off-target, everything downstream is doomed and it's a prompt or planning problem.

If the query was fine, I'd look at what the search returned. If the sources were bad or irrelevant, it's a retrieval problem.

And if the sources were good but the summary contradicts them, then it's the model — it's ignoring the context, which usually means the context was too long, badly ordered, or the prompt didn't insist strongly enough on using only the retrieved material.

The nice thing is the reasoning log is already streamed to the UI, so I can see all three stages for that specific run rather than guessing.

This is a support answer disguised as a project answer. Splitting a system into stages and testing each one is exactly what they want to see. Expect them to like this.

LegalEase — RAG legal assistant

Python · ChromaDB · all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embeddings · FlashRank re-ranker · RAGAS evaluation · FastAPI · React + Tailwind

QExplain LegalEase.
SAY THIS — 30-SECOND PITCH

LegalEase answers legal questions using an actual document base rather than the model's memory, so the answers can be traced back to a source.

When you ask a question, it first searches a vector database for the most relevant sections of the legal documents. Then there's a second stage — a re-ranker that re-scores those results against the question and keeps only the best ones. Those go to the model as context, and it answers from them.

The part I'm most pleased with is the evaluation. I used RAGAS to measure whether the answers were actually grounded in the retrieved documents, and got around 92% faithfulness and 88% relevance. Without that number I'd have had no idea whether my changes were helping.

Follow-ups you must be ready for
  • "Why do you need a re-ranker? Isn't vector search enough?" → "Vector search is fast but approximate — it compares one embedding of the question to one embedding of each chunk, so it retrieves things that are topically close but not actually answering the question. The re-ranker is a cross-encoder: it looks at the question and the chunk together, so it's much more accurate. It's too slow to run on the whole database, which is why you use it as a second stage on the top 20 or so results."
  • "Why 1200-character chunks?" → "Legal text falls apart if you cut it. A section number in one chunk and its content in another is useless. Larger chunks kept whole articles together. The trade-off is you send more irrelevant text to the model, which costs tokens and can dilute the answer — the re-ranker helps offset that."
  • "What is faithfulness?" → Whether the answer is supported by the retrieved context, rather than invented. It's the metric that catches hallucination specifically.
  • "What are the risks of a legal AI?" → A confident wrong answer that someone acts on. That's why grounding and citing sources matters more here than fluency, and why it should be framed as assistance, not advice.
QA customer says LegalEase gives irrelevant answers. How do you troubleshoot?
SAY THIS

I'd check retrieval before I touch anything else, because in RAG the failure is almost always retrieval and almost never the model.

So for their exact question, I'd print the chunks that came back. If the right document isn't in there, the model never had a chance — and then the question is why. Was the document actually ingested? Did the chunking split the relevant part badly? Is the question phrased in words that don't appear anywhere in the corpus?

If the correct chunk was retrieved and the answer still ignored it, then it's a prompting or context problem — too much context, the useful part buried in the middle, or a prompt that doesn't tell the model to answer only from the provided material.

And if the document genuinely isn't in the corpus, the right fix isn't a better prompt — it's for the system to say it doesn't have that information, rather than inventing something.

MEMORISE THIS SENTENCE

"In RAG, the failure is almost always retrieval, not generation." It's true, it's concise, and it's the kind of thing that makes an interviewer nod.

Section 13AI concepts they may test

RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)

THE PLAIN-ENGLISH VERSION — USE THIS FIRST

A language model on its own answers from what it learned during training, and if it doesn't know something it tends to make it up confidently.

RAG fixes that by giving it the material first. When a question comes in, the system searches a set of documents for the relevant pieces, hands those to the model, and asks it to answer using only that. So the answer is based on your actual documents, and you can point to where it came from.

The simplest way to picture it: instead of asking someone to answer from memory, you hand them the right page of the manual and ask them to read it.

Follow-ups — and short answers
  • "Walk me through the pipeline." → Documents are split into chunks → each chunk is converted to an embedding and stored in a vector database → the question is embedded the same way → the closest chunks are retrieved → optionally re-ranked → passed to the model as context → the model answers from that context.
  • "Why not just fine-tune the model instead?" → "Fine-tuning changes the model's behaviour and style; it's a poor way to add facts. With RAG, updating knowledge means updating a document, not retraining. And you get citations, which fine-tuning doesn't give you. For support documentation that changes weekly, RAG is clearly the right choice."
  • "What is an embedding?" → "A list of numbers representing the meaning of a piece of text. Texts with similar meaning end up close together, so you can search by meaning rather than by exact words — someone asking about 'parcel not delivered' can match a document about 'failed delivery attempts'."
  • "What's a vector database?" → "A database built to find the nearest vectors to a given vector, quickly, across millions of items. I've used ChromaDB and FAISS."
  • "How do you choose chunk size?" → "It's a trade-off. Too small and you cut ideas in half; too large and you send a lot of irrelevant text and dilute the answer. I used around 1200 characters for legal text because sections needed to stay whole. Overlapping chunks help so an idea isn't lost at a boundary."
  • "Where does RAG fail?" → "When the answer isn't in the documents at all — then the model may still try to answer. That has to be handled explicitly by allowing it to say it doesn't know. It also struggles with questions that need to combine information from many documents at once, and with counting or aggregation, which is really a database job."

Hallucination

SAY THIS

It's when a model produces something that sounds completely reasonable but isn't true — a function that doesn't exist, a policy we don't have, a confident wrong cause for a bug.

The dangerous part isn't that it's wrong, it's that it's wrong in the same confident tone as when it's right. There's no signal in the output telling you which one you're getting. That's why grounding it in real documents and showing the source matters — you're giving the person a way to check.

AI agents and function calling

SAY THIS

A normal model can only produce text. Function calling means you describe some tools to it — search, a database query, an API call — and instead of answering directly, it can respond with "call this tool with these inputs." Your code runs the tool, gives it the result, and it continues.

An agent is that in a loop, with the model deciding what to do next each time until the task is done. Casper is exactly that: it decides what to search, gets the results, decides whether it has enough, and either searches again or writes the answer.

Follow-ups
  • "What can go wrong with agents?" → "Loops — they repeat the same action forever. Cost, because each step is another model call. And error compounding: if step two is wrong, everything after it is built on that. So you need a step budget, and I'd want a human approving anything that changes data."
  • "Would you let an agent fix production issues automatically?" → "Not straight away, and not for anything destructive. I'd start with read-only actions — gathering logs, checking status, producing a diagnosis for a human. Then maybe safe reversible actions like retrying a failed webhook. Anything that changes customer data needs a person, at least until there's a real track record."
  • "Multi-agent systems?" → Several specialised agents with a coordinator — e.g. one that reads logs, one that searches past tickets, one that writes the summary. Say honestly that you've built single-agent systems, not multi-agent, if that's the case.

Text-to-SQL

SAY THIS

The model is given the database structure — the tables, the columns, what they mean — and the user's question in English, and it writes the SQL query. We run the query and answer from the result.

The advantage is that the numbers are real, computed by the database, rather than the model guessing. The risk is that a query can be valid SQL and still answer the wrong question. So the important work is on the guardrails: only read permissions, restricted tables, a row limit, and showing the user how their question was interpreted.

Prompt engineering — what you'd say if asked

  • Be specific about the role, the task and the output format.
  • Give examples when the format matters — a few examples beat a long description.
  • Explicitly allow "I don't know." Most hallucination happens because the prompt implies an answer is mandatory.
  • Put instructions where they're not lost — models attend less to the middle of a very long context.
  • Test changes against a fixed set of cases. Otherwise you fix one case and quietly break three.

Section 15Speaking clearly in English

You told me this is your weak point. Here is the honest truth: your interviewer is Indian, works with global clients daily, and is used to a range of accents and fluency levels. They are not grading your grammar. They are checking whether you can be understood by a client under pressure. Those are very different bars, and you can clear the second one tonight.

The six rules that fix most of it

  1. Speak slower than feels natural. Nervousness makes you speed up, and speed is what makes accented English hard to follow. Deliberately slow down by about 20%. It also makes you sound more confident.
  2. Short sentences. One idea each. Long sentences are where grammar collapses. "The system searches the documents. Then it picks the best matches. Then the model answers from those." Three short sentences beat one long one, every time.
  3. Pause instead of "umm." Silence sounds thoughtful. "Umm... like... basically..." sounds unsure. When you need a moment, close your mouth and take a breath. Two seconds of silence feels enormous to you and is invisible to them.
  4. Use words you own. Don't reach for an impressive word you're unsure of. "It made the answers more accurate" is better than a misused "it augmented the fidelity." Simple English delivered confidently reads as clear thinking.
  5. Don't translate in your head. If you compose in Malayalam and translate, you'll always lag. Speak directly in simple English, even if it's plainer than what you'd say in Malayalam.
  6. Finish your answers. Don't trail off into "...so, yeah." End with a clear stop: "So that's how I'd approach it." Then be quiet and let them ask the next question.
