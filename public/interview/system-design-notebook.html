Section 01 · Foundations

What System Design Actually Is

Before any diagram: what the round is testing, and why the “right answer” is not a diagram at all.

The one-sentence definition

System design is the process of deciding how a set of machines, services and data stores should be arranged so that a product keeps working correctly when it is used by far more people than one computer can serve.

That definition hides the whole subject inside two words: “keeps working.” A single laptop running Python can serve a hundred users. It cannot serve a hundred million, and it cannot survive its own power supply dying. Everything you will learn — replicas, caches, queues, shards — exists to answer one of exactly two questions:

Question 1 · Scale

“There is more work than one machine can do.” → Split the work. Load balancers, sharding, caching, queues, CDNs.

Question 2 · Failure

“Machines and networks die, always, without warning.” → Keep spare copies. Replication, failover, retries, consensus.

Mental model to keep for the whole notebook

Every technique in system design is either splitting work up or keeping a redundant copy. When you meet a new tool, ask which of the two it is. Kafka? Both. Redis? Splitting (offloads the DB). Raft? Copies. A CDN? Both.

How the round is actually scored

This is the single most important paragraph in the notebook. Freshers assume the interviewer has a correct architecture in mind and is checking whether you produced it. They do not. A 45-minute conversation cannot produce a real design; Instagram took hundreds of engineer-years. What the interviewer is grading is a simulation of working with you.

  • Do you ask before you build? A candidate who starts drawing in minute one has failed the most heavily weighted signal.
  • Do you justify, or just name-drop? “I’ll use Kafka” scores zero. “Writes are bursty and the consumer is slow, so I’ll buffer them in a log-based queue like Kafka; it also lets me replay if the consumer has a bug” scores full marks.
  • Do you know what you traded away? Every choice costs something. Saying the cost out loud, unprompted, is the strongest signal of experience.
  • Can you find your own bottleneck? Interviewers love asking “now it’s 10× traffic.” Beat them to it.
  • Do you communicate? Silent thinking reads as no thinking. Narrate.
The fresher trap

Memorising “the Twitter design” and reciting it is instantly visible and is scored as a fail, because the interviewer will simply change a requirement (“now tweets can be edited”) and the recital collapses. Learn the building blocks in Part II so you can assemble any answer live.

The 4-step framework — your script for every question

Use this every single time. It converts a terrifying open question into a checklist. Time budget assumes a 45-minute round.

  1. Clarify & scope · 5–8 min Pin down functional requirements (what it does), non-functional requirements (how well), and the scale. Say “Let me confirm what we’re building before I design it.” End this step by writing the agreed scope down and asking “does that match what you had in mind?”
  2. Estimate & define the interface · 5–8 min Do the back-of-envelope maths (Section 03): QPS, storage, bandwidth. Then sketch the API endpoints and the data model. Numbers here decide every later choice — a 100 GB dataset and a 100 PB dataset are different problems.
  3. High-level design · 12–15 min Draw 5–8 boxes: client → gateway → services → cache → database, plus any queue. Trace one write and one read end to end through the diagram, out loud. Do not go deep yet.
  4. Deep dive, bottlenecks & failure · 15–20 min The interviewer picks a component, or you offer one. Go three layers down. Then attack your own design: what if this node dies? What if traffic 10×s? What is the hot key? Finish with a one-minute recap of the trade-offs you made.

Interview questions · Section 01
How would you approach a system design question you have never seen before?

“I’d run the same four steps regardless of the problem. First, clarify — I’d list functional requirements, then non-functional ones like latency, availability and consistency, and then get scale numbers, because a system for ten thousand users and one for a hundred million are different designs. Second, I’d do a quick estimate of QPS, storage and bandwidth, and define the API and data model, since those constrain everything downstream. Third, I’d draw a high-level diagram and trace one read and one write through it. Fourth, I’d deep-dive whichever component is the bottleneck and then stress-test my own design against failures. If I genuinely don’t know a domain, the framework still works — I’d just ask more clarifying questions to learn the domain from the interviewer.”

Why this scores: it shows the process is internalised rather than the answers memorised, which is exactly what an unseen question is testing.

What is the difference between HLD and LLD?

HLD is about architecture across machines: which services exist, which databases they use, how requests flow, how the system scales and survives failure. Its output is a box-and-arrow diagram plus trade-off reasoning. LLD is about structure inside one service: classes, interfaces, inheritance, design patterns, and how to keep the code extensible. Its output is a class diagram and often real code.

A quick tell: if the answer changes when traffic goes from 1k to 1M users, it is HLD. If the answer changes when the product adds a new payment type, it is LLD.

What are functional and non-functional requirements? Give an example.

Functional requirements describe what the system does — features, expressed as verbs. For a URL shortener: create a short link for a long URL; redirect a short link to the original; optionally let the user pick a custom alias; expire links.

Non-functional requirements describe how well it must do it — the qualities. For the same shortener: redirects must complete in under 100 ms at p99; the system must be highly available (a dead shortener breaks every link ever shared); short codes must never collide; reads outnumber writes roughly 100:1.

The non-functional set is what actually drives architecture. “Highly available, read-heavy, low latency” is what tells you to add caching and replicas.

The interviewer says “design Twitter” and stays silent. What are your first three questions?
  1. Scope: “Are we designing the whole product, or should I focus on the core — posting a tweet and reading a home timeline? I’d suggest we scope to those two plus follow, and treat DMs, search and ads as out of scope unless you’d like them.”
  2. Scale: “Roughly how many daily active users, and what’s the read:write ratio? I’ll assume 300 million DAU and heavily read-skewed unless you say otherwise.”
  3. Non-functional priorities: “Is it acceptable for a tweet to take a few seconds to appear in followers’ timelines? If eventual consistency is fine there, that unlocks a much cheaper design.”

Notice each question offers a default. Asking a question and proposing an answer keeps momentum and shows judgement; asking twelve open questions with no proposals reads as stalling.

Why do interviewers care so much about trade-offs?

Because in real engineering there is never a free win. Adding a cache buys latency and costs consistency and memory. Sharding buys write throughput and costs the ability to do easy joins and cross-shard transactions. Microservices buy independent deployment and cost operational complexity and network failure modes.

A candidate who names only benefits either hasn’t operated a real system or is reciting. Saying “I’m choosing X; the price is Y, and I’m willing to pay it here because Z” is the single highest-signal sentence pattern in the whole round — use it at least four times.

What is a single point of failure, and how do you remove one?

A SPOF is any component with no redundant peer, so its failure fails the system. Classic examples: one load balancer, one primary database with no replica, one instance of a stateful service, a single availability zone, and — often forgotten — a single DNS provider or a single third-party payment gateway.

You remove it by replicating and automating failover: run at least two instances, spread them across availability zones, put health checks in front, and make sure the promotion of a standby is automatic rather than a human at 3 a.m. For stateless services this is trivial (just run N copies). For stateful ones you need replication plus an election mechanism (Section 13). Also verify there is no hidden SPOF in the failover path itself.

Requirements, Nines & The Metrics That Decide Everything

Latency, throughput, availability, consistency, durability — and the reason p99 matters more than the average.

Latency: why the average lies

Latency is the time for one request. But one number cannot describe a million requests, so we use percentiles. p50 (median) is the experience of a typical request; p99 is the experience of the unluckiest 1%.

Worked intuition

Suppose 99 requests take 10 ms and one takes 3 000 ms. The average is 40 ms — which describes no actual request. p50 is 10 ms, p99 is 3 000 ms. Now consider a page that makes 100 backend calls to render. The chance that at least one of them hits the p99 path is about 1 − 0.99¹⁰⁰ ≈ 63%. So the majority of page loads feel the p99, not the average.

This is tail latency amplification, and it is why big companies optimise p99/p999 and largely ignore the mean. Say this in an interview and you will sound senior.

Latency vs Throughput

They are not the same and improving one can hurt the other. Latency = time per request. Throughput = requests finished per second. Batching 100 writes into one disk flush multiplies throughput but adds waiting time to each individual write. A checkout page optimises latency; a nightly analytics job optimises throughput.

Analogy: a wider motorway raises throughput (more cars/hour) without making any single car faster. A higher speed limit lowers latency.

Combining availability

In sequence (dependency chain) — if A must call B must call C, availabilities multiply:
0.99 × 0.99 × 0.99 = 0.9703 → 97.03%. More dependencies = worse. This is the hidden cost of microservices.

In parallel (redundancy) — if either replica can serve, unavailabilities multiply:
1 − (1 − 0.99)² = 1 − 0.0001 = 99.99%. Two 99% servers give four nines. This is why redundancy is the cheapest availability you can buy.

SLI, SLO, SLA — three words interviewers love

SLI
Service Level Indicator — the measurement itself. “p99 latency of the checkout endpoint”, “percentage of 2xx responses”. It is a number you actually collect.
SLO
Service Level Objective — your internal target for that SLI. “p99 < 300 ms, 99.9% of the time, over 30 days.” Missing it triggers engineering work, not lawyers.
SLA
Service Level Agreement — the contractual promise to a customer, with penalties (usually service credits). Always set looser than your SLO, so you fix problems before you owe money.
Error budget — the sentence that wins the follow-up

An SLO of 99.9% over 30 days permits 43.2 minutes of failure. That remainder is the error budget. It reframes reliability as a currency: if the budget is unspent, ship features faster and take more risk; if it is exhausted, freeze releases and spend the sprint on stability. It stops the pointless argument between “move fast” and “be reliable” by making the trade-off numeric.

The other qualities you should name

Reliability

The system produces correct results, not just any results. A system that returns fast wrong answers is available but unreliable.

Durability

Once written, data survives — disk failure, power loss, datacentre fire. Object stores advertise eleven nines of durability (99.999999999%), achieved by many redundant copies.

Scalability

Capacity grows roughly linearly with hardware added, with no rewrite required.

Maintainability

A new engineer can understand, change and operate it. Often the real reason a design is rejected.

Consistency

Every reader sees the same data. Section 10 explains why this fights availability.

Cost

Newly graded explicitly in 2026 loops. Petabytes in RAM is a wrong answer even if it is fast.

Availability ≠ Reliability ≠ Durability

Interviewers probe this. A cache that serves stale data during an outage is available but not consistent. A DB that returns errors rather than wrong rows is reliable but not available. Data safely on disk in a machine nobody can reach is durable but not available.

Interview questions · Section 02
What does “four nines” mean and how much downtime is that?

99.99% availability — about 52.6 minutes of downtime per year, or 8.6 seconds a day. It’s the common target for a commercial SaaS product. Getting there needs redundancy at every layer, multi-AZ deployment, automated failover, and health-checked load balancing; going from four to five nines usually costs more than the previous three combined, which is why you should ask what the target actually is before over-engineering.

Why do we measure p99 latency instead of the average?

Because the average hides the worst experiences, and the worst experiences are the ones users complain about and the ones that cascade. If 99 requests take 10 ms and one takes 3 seconds, the mean is a comfortable 40 ms while one user waited 3 seconds.

It gets worse at scale because of tail latency amplification: a page that fans out to 100 services has a ~63% chance of at least one call landing on the p99. So the tail, not the mean, is what the typical page load actually feels. Also, averages can’t be meaningfully averaged across servers, whereas percentiles are computed from the real distribution.

A service depends on 5 microservices, each 99.9% available. What is its availability?

If all five must succeed, availabilities multiply: 0.999⁵ ≈ 0.995, so about 99.5% — roughly 43 hours of downtime a year, far worse than any individual component. That is the hidden tax of a deep dependency chain.

Fixes: make non-critical dependencies asynchronous or optional so a failure degrades rather than fails (graceful degradation); add caching so a dead dependency can be served stale; add circuit breakers and sensible timeouts so one slow service doesn’t hold threads; and replicate each dependency so its own availability rises.

Difference between SLA, SLO and SLI?

SLI is the measured number (p99 latency = 240 ms). SLO is the internal target for that number (p99 < 300 ms for 99.9% of the month). SLA is the external contract with penalties (99.9% uptime or you get service credits).

The relationship matters: SLA is deliberately weaker than the SLO, so the team is alerted and fixes the problem before a customer is legally entitled to a refund. And the gap left by the SLO — 0.1% of 30 days ≈ 43 minutes — is the error budget, which teams spend on risky deploys.

Latency vs throughput — can you improve both at once?

Sometimes, but often they trade off. Caching improves both (fewer, faster trips). Adding servers improves throughput and keeps latency flat by avoiding queueing. But batching and buffering raise throughput while raising per-request latency, because early items wait for the batch to fill. Similarly, running a server at 95% utilisation maximises throughput but queueing theory says latency explodes near saturation — which is why production systems are typically run at 50–70% utilisation.

Your system must be “highly available”. What concretely do you do?
  • Remove SPOFs: ≥2 of everything — LBs, app servers, DB replicas.
  • Spread across failure domains: multiple availability zones, and multiple regions if the target is very high.
  • Health checks + automatic failover: the LB removes sick nodes; a standby DB is promoted automatically.
  • Statelessness: keep session state out of app servers so any node can serve any request and nodes are disposable.
  • Isolate failure: timeouts, retries with exponential backoff and jitter, circuit breakers, bulkheads (Section 14).
  • Degrade gracefully: if recommendations are down, show a generic list rather than an error page.
  • Safe deploys: canary and rolling releases, since most outages are caused by changes, not by hardware.
What is an error budget and why is it useful?

It is 100% minus the SLO — the amount of unreliability you are allowed. For a 99.9% monthly SLO that’s ~43 minutes. It is useful because it turns a values argument into an accounting one: while budget remains, the team is permitted to deploy aggressively and run experiments; once it’s spent, feature work pauses in favour of reliability work. It also stops the trap of chasing 100%, which is impossible and infinitely expensive.

The three ratios that actually matter

Memory is ~100 000× faster than a network round trip within a DC, which is ~300× faster than a cross-continent round trip. Everything else follows: cache in RAM before you cache on disk; avoid chatty service calls (10 sequential calls = 5 ms of pure network); put a CDN near users because you cannot beat the speed of light — light in fibre covers roughly 200 km per millisecond, so Mumbai→Virginia is physically ~120 ms minimum, round trip.

The method — five steps, always the same

  1. State assumptions out loud “I’ll assume 200 M DAU, each posting 2 items and reading 100.” Interviewers correct you if it matters; if they don’t, your number is now agreed.
  2. Compute QPS Daily events ÷ 10⁵. Then peak QPS = 2–3× average (traffic is never flat — evenings and time zones).
  3. Compute storage Items/day × bytes/item × retention. Add ~30% for indexes, metadata and replication overhead, then multiply by the replication factor (usually 3).
  4. Compute bandwidth QPS × payload size, separately for ingress and egress. Egress is usually the surprise (video, images).
  5. Convert to machines QPS ÷ per-machine QPS = servers. Storage ÷ per-machine disk = shards. This is the number that impresses.

Worked example — Twitter-scale, done properly

Assumptions

Peak read QPS ≈ 3× = 180 000 QPS
Read:write ratio ≈ 40:1 → this is a read-heavy system. That one conclusion dictates caching, read replicas and fan-out-on-write.

Storage

Text: 150 M × 500 B = 75 GB/day → × 365 × 5 ≈ 137 TB over 5 years
Images: 15 M/day × 200 KB = 3 TB/day → × 365 × 5 ≈ 5.5 PB
With 3× replication: text ≈ 0.4 PB, media ≈ 16 PB
Conclusion: media cannot live in the database. It goes to object storage (S3-like) + CDN; the DB stores only URLs.

Bandwidth & machines

Read egress ≈ 60 000 QPS × 5 KB timeline page ≈ 300 MB/s ≈ 2.4 Gbps (before media; media served by CDN)
App servers ≈ 180 000 peak QPS ÷ 5 000 QPS per box ≈ 36 servers, so run ~50–60 for headroom and failure
Cache: hold hot 20% of timelines in RAM → tens of TB across a Redis cluster of ~100+ nodes

Estimation mistakes that cost marks
  • Forgetting peak ≠ average. Always apply a 2–3× peak factor.
  • Forgetting replication when sizing storage (usually ×3).
  • Sizing the DB for media blobs instead of moving them to object storage.
  • Doing arithmetic silently. The reasoning is the answer; the digits are not.
  • Using precise numbers — 86 400 instead of 10⁵ — and then getting lost in long division.
  • Never converting to machine counts, which is the only figure that leads anywhere.
Interview questions · Section 03
Estimate the storage needed for a URL shortener over 5 years.

“Assume 100 million new URLs a day. Per record I need the short code (7 bytes), the long URL (~100 bytes average), a user id (8 bytes), created-at and expiry (16 bytes), plus click counters — call it 500 bytes with indexing overhead, rounding generously.

100 M × 500 B = 50 GB/day. Over 5 years: 50 GB × 365 × 5 ≈ 91 TB. With 3× replication, ~275 TB.

Write QPS = 100 M ÷ 10⁵ = 1 000/s, peak ~3 000/s. Reads are redirects and typically 10:1, so 10 000/s average, 30 000/s peak — heavily read-skewed, so the design needs an aggressive cache in front.

91 TB doesn’t fit one machine, so I’d shard by short code, or use a distributed KV store. And since it’s a pure key→value lookup with no joins, a KV store fits better than a relational DB.”

How many servers do you need for 1 million requests per second?

“It depends entirely on what one request costs, so let me state that. If requests are cheap and cache-served, a well-tuned server might handle 10 000 QPS → 100 servers. If each request hits a database and does real work, 1 000 QPS per box is realistic → 1 000 servers.

Then I’d add: I never run at 100% — target ~60% utilisation because latency degrades sharply near saturation, so multiply by ~1.7. And I need to survive losing an availability zone, so with 3 AZs I need each to carry the full load at 1.5× the per-AZ share. Realistically that’s 250–300 servers for the cheap case.

The real answer, though, is to reduce the 1 M QPS: a CDN and a cache layer might absorb 90% of it, leaving 100 000 QPS to the origin — which is a much cheaper system.”

How much bandwidth does a video streaming service need?

“Say 10 million concurrent viewers at 1080p, which is roughly 5 Mbps per stream. Total = 10⁷ × 5 × 10⁶ bits/s = 5 × 10¹³ bps = 50 Tbps.

A single 10 Gbps NIC does 10⁻² Tbps, so this is 5 000 fully saturated servers of pure network — which tells me immediately that origin servers cannot do this. It has to be a CDN, with content pushed to edge PoPs close to users, and adaptive bitrate so mobile users on poor connections pull 500 kbps instead of 5 Mbps. Typically a CDN offloads 95%+ of egress, leaving the origin serving only cache misses.”

Why do you multiply average QPS by 2–3 for peak?

Because traffic is never uniform across 24 hours. Users concentrate in waking hours and especially evenings, and a global product still peaks when its largest market is awake. A rough model: most of the day’s traffic arrives in an 8-hour window, which alone gives ~3×. Then add event spikes — a cricket final, a sale, a celebrity post — which can be 10× or more for minutes.

You size steady-state capacity for peak, and handle spikes above that with autoscaling, queues to absorb bursts, and rate limiting/load shedding so an unexpected spike degrades instead of collapsing.

How would you estimate the QPS of Instagram’s photo uploads?

“Assume 500 M DAU, and that 10% of them upload one photo a day. That’s 50 M uploads/day. 50 × 10⁶ ÷ 10⁵ = 500 QPS average, ~1 500 QPS peak. That’s a surprisingly small number and is a useful sanity check — the writes are not the hard part.

The hard part is size: at ~2 MB per original, that’s 100 TB/day of ingest before generating thumbnails and multiple resolutions, which typically doubles it. And reads are enormously larger — if each user views 50 photos a day, that’s 25 billion reads/day = 250 000 QPS, almost all of which must be served by a CDN rather than the origin.”

Roughly how much data can one machine hold and serve?

Useful working assumptions to state aloud: a commodity server has 64–256 GB RAM, 1–10 TB SSD, a 10 Gbps NIC, and dozens of cores. In terms of service: an app server does roughly 1 000–10 000 QPS depending on work per request; a relational database does ~1 000–5 000 QPS of mixed load on a single primary; Redis does 100 000+ QPS because it’s in-memory and single-purpose.

The point of memorising these isn’t precision — it’s to know instantly whether a workload fits on one box. 100 GB of data and 2 000 QPS? One machine, don’t shard. 100 TB and 200 000 QPS? You are distributed whether you like it or not.

Section 04 · Building Blocks

Networking & Communication Protocols

What actually happens between typing a URL and seeing a page — plus how services talk to each other, and how a server pushes to a client.

What happens when you type a URL — the canonical warm-up

Asked constantly, because it lets the interviewer probe any layer. Learn the chain:

  1. DNS resolution The browser checks its cache, then the OS cache, then asks the configured resolver. If unknown, the resolver walks root → TLD (.com) → authoritative nameserver, and gets back an IP. Results are cached per the record’s TTL.
  2. TCP connection A three-way handshake (SYN → SYN-ACK → ACK) — one round trip before any data moves.
  3. TLS handshake For HTTPS, certificate exchange and key agreement. TLS 1.3 needs one round trip; 1.2 needed two.
  4. HTTP request Method, path, headers, cookies travel to the server — usually first hitting a CDN edge, then a load balancer.
  5. Server processing LB → app server → cache lookup → database if missed → response assembled.
  6. Response & render HTML returns; the browser parses it and fires more requests for CSS, JS and images, mostly served by the CDN.
The senior-sounding addition

“Note that steps 1–3 cost 3 round trips before a single byte of content. Over a 100 ms link that is 300 ms of pure setup, which is why we use connection keep-alive, HTTP/2 multiplexing over one connection, TLS session resumption, and a CDN to make those round trips short.”

Why a video call uses UDP

If a frame of audio is lost, retransmitting it is pointless — by the time it arrives, the conversation has moved on. TCP would stall the whole stream waiting for it (head-of-line blocking) and you would hear a freeze instead of a tiny glitch. UDP drops it and continues. REST essentials you should not fumble

  • Idempotent methods: GET, PUT, DELETE, HEAD — repeating them has the same effect as doing them once. POST is not idempotent, which is why payment APIs use an idempotency key (Section 12).
  • Safe methods: GET/HEAD change nothing, so they can be cached and retried freely.
  • Status codes: 2xx success · 3xx redirect (301 permanent, 302 temporary) · 4xx client error (400 bad request, 401 unauthenticated, 403 unauthorised, 404 missing, 409 conflict, 429 rate limited) · 5xx server error (500, 502 bad gateway, 503 unavailable, 504 timeout).
  • Statelessness: each request carries everything needed. This is what lets any server handle any request, which is what lets you scale horizontally.
  • Pagination: prefer cursor-based (?after=tweet_9812) over offset-based (?page=500). Offset gets slower as it grows and skips/duplicates items when the list changes underneath you.

DNS in one paragraph

DNS maps names to addresses, and is also a load-balancing and failover tool. Record types worth naming: A (name → IPv4), AAAA (IPv6), CNAME (alias to another name), MX (mail), NS (delegation), TXT (verification). Routing policies let you do geo-routing (send Indian users to the Mumbai region), latency-based routing, weighted routing (send 5% to a canary) and failover routing. The catch is TTL: clients cache records, so a DNS-level failover takes as long as the TTL to propagate — which is why DNS is a coarse tool and real failover happens at the load balancer.

Interview questions · Section 04
Walk me through what happens when you type google.com into a browser.

“The browser resolves the name via DNS — checking its own cache, the OS cache, then the resolver, which if needed walks root → .com TLD → Google’s authoritative nameservers, returning an IP that gets cached for the record’s TTL. Then TCP connects with a three-way handshake, and TLS negotiates encryption — one round trip in TLS 1.3.

The HTTPS GET usually lands on a nearby CDN edge or an anycast IP, then a load balancer, then an application server. That server checks a cache, hits a database on a miss, and returns HTML. The browser parses it and issues follow-up requests for CSS, JS, fonts and images, most of which the CDN serves.

Worth noting: DNS + TCP + TLS is three round trips before any content, so real systems use keep-alive, HTTP/2 multiplexing and edge termination to cut that down.”

TCP or UDP for a live video call — and why?

UDP, because in real-time media timeliness beats completeness. TCP guarantees delivery by retransmitting lost packets and delivering in order, but a retransmitted audio packet arriving 200 ms late is useless — and worse, TCP’s in-order guarantee causes head-of-line blocking, so one lost packet freezes everything behind it. With UDP a lost packet is simply a small glitch and the stream continues.

The application layer then adds only what it needs: sequence numbers to detect loss, jitter buffers, forward error correction, and codec-level concealment — which is essentially what WebRTC does. For the signalling and chat parts of the same app, TCP (or WebSocket) is correct, because there losing a message is unacceptable.

WebSocket vs long polling vs server-sent events — when do you pick each?

I pick the weakest mechanism that meets the requirement, because each step up costs statefulness.

  • SSE when updates only flow server → client: live scores, a notification bell, streaming LLM tokens. It’s plain HTTP, works through proxies, and reconnects automatically.
  • WebSocket when the client must also send frequently and latency matters: chat, collaborative editing, multiplayer games, trading. It’s full-duplex with tiny per-message overhead.
  • Long polling as a fallback when infrastructure doesn’t support the above.
  • Short polling only when updates are rare and staleness of a few seconds is fine.

The scaling caveat for WebSockets: connections are long-lived and stateful, so the load balancer must support sticky/upgraded connections, and you need a pub/sub layer (like Redis) so a message arriving on server A reaches a user connected to server B. Also budget memory — a million concurrent connections is a real capacity line item.

What makes an API idempotent, and why does it matter?

An operation is idempotent if performing it N times has the same effect as performing it once. GET, PUT and DELETE are idempotent by definition; POST is not — two POSTs create two resources.

It matters because networks lose responses, not just requests. If a client sends “charge ₹500” and the connection drops, the client cannot tell whether the charge happened. Its only safe options are to retry (risking a double charge) or give up (risking a lost payment). Idempotency removes the dilemma: the client generates an idempotency key, the server stores the result against that key, and any retry with the same key returns the original result instead of charging again. Every payment API works this way.

REST vs gRPC for internal microservices?

gRPC is usually the better internal choice: Protobuf is a compact binary format so payloads are far smaller than JSON and parsing is cheaper; it runs over HTTP/2 so many calls multiplex on one connection with no head-of-line blocking; the .proto file is a machine-checked contract that generates clients in every language; and it supports streaming in both directions natively.

The costs: it isn’t natively callable from a browser (needs a proxy), payloads aren’t human-readable so debugging needs tooling, and HTTP-level caching by URL doesn’t apply. So the standard shape is gRPC inside the fence, REST at the edge.

How does DNS help with load balancing and failover, and what are its limits?

DNS can return different answers to different clients: round-robin across several A records, latency- or geo-based routing to the nearest region, weighted routing to send a small percentage to a canary, and failover routing that swaps in a standby IP when a health check fails.

Its limit is caching. Resolvers and clients honour the TTL — and some ignore it and cache longer — so a change can take minutes to propagate. That makes DNS unusable for fast failover or fine-grained balancing. In practice DNS does coarse, geographic routing to a region, and a real load balancer inside that region does the per-request work with health checks reacting in seconds. Lowering TTL helps but increases DNS query load and latency.

What is head-of-line blocking?

It’s when one stuck item blocks everything queued behind it, even though those items are independent. It shows up at two layers:

  • HTTP/1.1: one request at a time per connection, so a slow response blocks the rest — which is why browsers opened 6 connections per host. HTTP/2 fixed this by multiplexing many streams over one connection.
  • TCP: because TCP must deliver bytes in order, a single lost packet stalls delivery of every stream sharing that connection — so HTTP/2 still suffers at the transport layer. HTTP/3/QUIC fixes it by running over UDP with per-stream ordering, so one lost packet only stalls its own stream.

It also appears in message queues: one poison message at the head of a partition can block the consumer, which is why you need dead-letter queues.

Scaling Fundamentals

One server to a million users, in seven moves — and why “stateless” is the word that unlocks all of them.

The answer that shows judgement

“I’d scale vertically first, because it’s free in engineering time and modern servers are enormous — hundreds of GB of RAM handles a very large product. I switch to horizontal when I hit the machine ceiling, when I need redundancy for availability, or when cost per unit of capacity starts rising. In practice most real systems are vertical for the database and horizontal for the stateless application tier.”

Statelessness — the precondition for everything

A stateless server keeps no client-specific data between requests. Any request can go to any server, so servers become interchangeable, disposable and infinitely addable. A stateful server stores something (say, a login session in local memory) that means only that server can serve that user.

The evolution: 1 user → 10 million users

Learn this progression. It doubles as an answer template — when asked to scale anything, you walk down this list and stop where the requirements stop.

  1. Single server Web app + database on one box. Fine to a few thousand users. SPOF, and the two workloads fight for resources.
  2. Split the database out Web tier and data tier on separate machines. Now each scales independently, and the DB gets a machine tuned for I/O.
  3. Load balancer + multiple stateless app servers Removes the app-tier SPOF and gives horizontal scale. Requires kicking sessions out to Redis or using signed tokens.
  4. Cache layer Put Redis/Memcached between app and DB. Typically absorbs 80–95% of reads and is the single highest-leverage change in the whole list.
  5. CDN Serve static assets, images and video from edge locations near the user. Cuts latency and removes most bytes from your origin.
  6. Database read replicas Writes go to the primary; reads fan out to replicas. Solves read scale, and introduces replication lag — reads may be slightly stale.
  7. Asynchronous processing Move slow work (email, thumbnails, feed fan-out, analytics) onto a message queue with worker pools. The user request returns immediately.
  8. Sharding / partitioning Split the data itself across machines when one primary can no longer hold or write it. This is the expensive one — do it last.
  9. Multi-region Full stacks in several geographies for latency and disaster recovery. Brings the hardest consistency problems in the notebook.
Order matters, and interviewers test it

If a candidate reaches for sharding before caching and read replicas, that is a red flag. The correct instinct is: reduce the load before you distribute it. Caching is cheap and reversible; sharding is expensive, permanent and breaks joins and transactions.

Scaling the read path vs the write path

Read-heavy (most systems)

Cache aggressively · add read replicas · CDN for static and media · denormalise so a read touches one row · precompute (materialised views, fan-out-on-write).

Write-heavy (logging, IoT, chat)

Shard by key so writes spread · buffer through a queue to absorb bursts · batch writes · pick LSM-tree storage (Cassandra) over B-tree · relax durability where acceptable · aggregate before storing.

Amdahl’s Law — the theory answer

Speedup is limited by the fraction of work that cannot be parallelised. If 10% of a job is inherently serial, then even with infinite machines the maximum speedup is 1 ÷ 0.10 = 10×. In system design the serial fraction usually is a shared lock, a single primary database, or a global counter — which is exactly why removing shared mutable state is the recurring theme of this whole part.

Interview questions · Section 05
How would you scale a system from 1 000 to 10 million users?

“I’d go in order of cost and reversibility, and stop when the numbers say to stop. Start by splitting the web and database tiers so they scale independently. Then put a load balancer in front of several stateless app servers, which means moving sessions to Redis or signed tokens. Next, add a cache in front of the database — that’s usually the biggest single win, absorbing 80–95% of reads. Then a CDN for static assets and media, which removes most bytes from my origin entirely.

When reads still exceed one database, add read replicas and route reads to them, accepting some replication lag. Move anything slow — emails, image processing, feed fan-out — onto a message queue with workers so the user request returns fast. Only when a single primary can no longer absorb the writes or hold the data do I shard, because sharding costs me joins and cross-shard transactions permanently.

Throughout, I’d be watching metrics to know which layer is actually the bottleneck rather than guessing.”

Vertical or horizontal scaling — which would you choose?

Vertical first, horizontal when forced. Vertical is instant and needs no code change, and modern hardware is big enough that many products never outgrow one database machine. But it has a hard ceiling, the cost per unit of capacity rises sharply at the top end, scaling usually means a restart, and crucially it does nothing for availability — one machine is still one machine.

Horizontal is unbounded and gives redundancy for free, but demands a load balancer, stateless services, and distributed data with all the consistency problems that brings. Real systems mix them: scale the stateless app tier out, and scale the database up as far as possible before sharding.

Why is statelessness important for scaling?

Because it makes servers interchangeable. If a server holds a user’s session in local memory, then that user is bound to that server: the load balancer needs sticky sessions, a server crash logs users out, you can’t rebalance traffic, autoscaling down destroys sessions, and rolling deploys are disruptive.

Move the state to a shared Redis store or into a signed token the client carries, and every server becomes identical. Now you can add nodes during a spike, remove them after, replace unhealthy ones automatically, and lose a machine with zero user impact. Statelessness is the precondition for horizontal scaling, autoscaling and zero-downtime deploys — which is why it’s the first thing to fix.

Your database is the bottleneck. What do you do, in order?
  1. Measure first — is it CPU, I/O, locks, or connections? Find the slow queries.
  2. Add indexes and fix N+1 query patterns. Often a 100× win for zero infrastructure.
  3. Cache the hot reads in Redis. Removes most load without touching the schema.
  4. Connection pooling — thousands of app connections crush a database; pool them.
  5. Read replicas for read scale; route analytics to a replica so it never touches the primary.
  6. Scale up the primary — often cheaper than any of the below.
  7. Denormalise so hot reads hit one row instead of a five-table join.
  8. Move cold data out — archive old rows to cheap storage; keep the working set small.
  9. Shard — last, because it is irreversible in practice and costs joins and transactions.
What does “sticky session” mean and why avoid it?

Session affinity: the load balancer always sends a given user to the same backend, usually via a cookie or a hash of the client IP. It exists so stateful servers keep working.

Avoid it because it undoes the benefits of load balancing. Traffic becomes uneven since users aren’t equal; a dead server loses every session pinned to it; you can’t drain a node cleanly for deployment; and autoscaling is blunted because new nodes only get new users. The fix is to make the app stateless — external session store or a signed JWT — after which any server can serve anyone. The legitimate exception is long-lived connections like WebSockets, where affinity is inherent; there you pair it with a pub/sub layer so messages can reach a user on any node.

The traffic cop. Layer 4 vs Layer 7, six algorithms, health checks — and the difference between a load balancer, a reverse proxy and an API gateway.

A load balancer sits in front of a pool of servers and distributes incoming requests among them. It does four jobs, and you should name all four: distribute load, detect and remove unhealthy nodes, enable zero-downtime deploys (drain a node, update, return it), and provide a stable single entry point so clients need not know about individual servers.

Health checks

This is a real and commonly-cited outage pattern.

Interview questions · Section 06
L4 vs L7 load balancing — which and why?

An L4 balancer only sees IPs and ports, so it forwards connections without inspecting content. That makes it extremely fast, low-latency and able to handle any TCP/UDP protocol, and it can pass encrypted traffic straight through. An L7 balancer terminates the connection and reads the HTTP request, so it can route by path, host, header or cookie, terminate TLS, cache, compress, rate limit and do A/B or canary routing — at the cost of more CPU and slightly more latency.

I’d use L7 for a typical web application because path-based routing to microservices and TLS termination are things I actually need. I’d use L4 when raw throughput matters, for non-HTTP protocols, or when the payload must stay encrypted end to end for compliance. Large systems layer them: L4 at the edge, L7 behind it.

How does a load balancer know a server is down?

Mainly via active health checks: it calls something like GET /healthz every few seconds and expects a 200 within a timeout. After N consecutive failures the node is removed from the pool; after M consecutive successes it returns. Thresholds prevent a single blip from flapping the pool. It also uses passive signals from real traffic — connection refusals, timeouts, a spike in 5xx.

The subtlety worth raising: health checks should be shallow enough not to correlate. If /healthz queries the database and the database gets slow, every server fails its check simultaneously and the balancer removes the entire fleet — turning a degradation into a total outage. Good practice is a shallow liveness check for the LB, a deeper readiness check used at deploy time, and a floor that refuses to remove more than a set fraction of the pool.

What if the load balancer itself fails?

Then it was a single point of failure and the design was wrong. Standard fixes:

  • Active–passive pair sharing a virtual IP: the standby monitors a heartbeat and takes over the VIP within seconds.
  • Active–active: several balancers all serving, with DNS round-robin or anycast spreading clients across them, so losing one loses only its share.
  • Multi-AZ / multi-region balancers with DNS failover above them for site-level loss.
  • Managed cloud balancers (ALB/NLB) do this internally and are effectively a redundant fleet behind one endpoint.

The general principle: at every layer, ask “what if this box dies?” — including the box whose job is to handle other boxes dying.

Which load balancing algorithm would you choose and why?

For a typical stateless web tier with identical machines, round robin is fine and is what most people start with. But my preferred default is least connections or least response time, because request cost is rarely uniform — round robin will happily keep feeding a server that is already stuck behind slow requests, whereas least-connections naturally routes around it.

I’d use weighted round robin when hardware is mixed, or to send 5% of traffic to a canary release. I’d use consistent hashing for a cache tier, because there I want the same key to land on the same node so the cache stays warm — and consistent hashing means adding a node only remaps 1/N of keys rather than all of them.

What is an API gateway and do I need one?

An API gateway is the single entry point in front of a set of microservices. It handles everything you don’t want duplicated in every service: authentication and token validation, rate limiting and quotas, request routing and versioning, protocol translation (REST outside, gRPC inside), request/response transformation, aggregating several service calls into one client response, plus logging and metering.

You need one once you have several services and external clients, because otherwise every service reimplements auth and rate limiting — inconsistently. You don’t need one for a single monolith; a reverse proxy is enough. The trade-offs to mention: it’s an extra network hop, it can become a deployment bottleneck if every team must change it, and it must itself be replicated so it isn’t a SPOF.

How do you load balance WebSocket connections?

WebSockets are long-lived and stateful, so a few things change. The balancer must support the HTTP upgrade handshake and then act as an L4 pass-through for the life of the connection, with generous idle timeouts (otherwise it kills quiet connections). Balancing by connection count matters more than by request rate, because connections persist for hours.

The real design problem is that user A is connected to server 1 while the message for them is produced on server 2. The standard solution is a pub/sub layer — Redis pub/sub or a message broker — where each server subscribes to the channels for the users it holds, so any server can publish and the right server delivers. You also need a presence/registry store mapping user → connected server, sticky routing or connection IDs, and a client that reconnects with backoff plus a “what did I miss since message N” resync, because connections will drop.

The highest-leverage move in system design, and the one with the most ways to go wrong.

A cache stores the result of expensive work in fast storage so the work isn’t repeated. It works because real workloads obey the Pareto principle — roughly 20% of the data serves 80% of requests — and because RAM is ~100 000× faster than a network+disk round trip.

Eviction policies

  • LRU (Least Recently Used) — evict what hasn’t been touched longest. The sensible default; matches temporal locality. Implemented with a hash map + doubly linked list for O(1) get and put.
  • LFU (Least Frequently Used) — evict the least-requested. Better for stable popularity, but a formerly-hot item can squat forever unless you age counts.
  • FIFO — evict the oldest inserted. Simple, ignores usage, rarely optimal.
  • TTL — not really eviction but expiry; every entry should have one so stale data self-heals.
  • Random — surprisingly decent and very cheap; Redis approximates LRU/LFU by sampling rather than maintaining exact order.

The four cache failures interviewers ask about

1 · Thundering herd / cache stampede

A popular key expires. Ten thousand concurrent requests all miss, and all hit the database simultaneously, which falls over. Fixes: a per-key lock or single-flight so only one request rebuilds while the others wait; probabilistic early expiry (refresh slightly before the TTL, randomly); serving stale data while asynchronously revalidating; and adding jitter to TTLs so keys written together don’t expire together.

2 · Cache penetration

Requests for keys that don’t exist anywhere — often malicious — always miss and always reach the database. Fixes: cache the negative result (“not found”) with a short TTL, and/or put a Bloom filter in front that can say “definitely not present” in O(1) with no false negatives.

3 · Cache avalanche

A large fraction of the cache expires at once — or the cache cluster restarts — and the entire load lands on the database at once. Fixes: randomised TTLs, warming the cache before taking traffic, a circuit breaker to shed load, and multi-level caching so a local in-process cache absorbs some of it.

4 · Hot key

One key (a celebrity’s profile, a flash-sale item) receives so much traffic it saturates the single cache node that owns it. Fixes: replicate the hot key across several nodes and read a random one; add a local in-process cache in front of the distributed cache; or shard the key artificially (key#1 … key#10).

“There are only two hard things in Computer Science”

…cache invalidation and naming things. Invalidation is genuinely hard because the truth lives in the database and the copy lives elsewhere with no automatic link. Your three practical strategies: TTL (simple, self-healing, accepts bounded staleness), explicit invalidation on write (fresher, but easy to miss a code path), and versioned keys — include a version or timestamp in the key so a new version simply misses and old entries age out, avoiding the delete race entirely.

CDN — the cache you don’t run

A CDN is a globally distributed network of edge servers (PoPs) that cache your content near users. Benefits: latency drops from ~200 ms to ~20 ms; your origin’s bandwidth bill collapses because the CDN serves 90%+ of bytes; and it absorbs DDoS traffic across its whole network.

  • Push CDN — you upload content ahead of time. Good for small, rarely-changing sets; wasteful if most content is never requested.
  • Pull CDN — the edge fetches from your origin on the first request and caches it. The common default; the first user in each region pays the slow path.
  • Cache-Control headers — max-age, s-maxage, no-store, stale-while-revalidate. Learn ETag + If-None-Match: the client sends the version it holds and the server can reply 304 Not Modified with an empty body.
  • Invalidating a CDN is slow and often rate-limited, so the standard trick is cache busting: fingerprint filenames (app.9f2c1a.js) and set an effectively infinite TTL. A new deploy produces a new name, so nothing needs purging.
  • Dynamic content can still benefit — CDNs terminate TLS at the edge and keep warm connections to the origin, saving round trips even on uncacheable responses.
When NOT to cache

Data that changes on nearly every read (a live counter), data where staleness is unacceptable (an account balance at the moment of transfer), data with almost no reuse (a unique per-user report), and anything where the hit rate would be low — a cache with a 10% hit rate adds latency and complexity while saving nothing. Also remember caching personalised responses at a shared layer is a security risk: cache one user’s data on a CDN with the wrong key and you serve it to everyone.

Interview questions · Section 07
Explain cache-aside vs write-through, and which you would pick.

Cache-aside: on a read, the app checks the cache; on a miss it reads the database and writes the result into the cache. On a write, it updates the database and then invalidates the cache entry. Write-through: every write goes through the cache, which synchronously writes to the database, so the cache is always current.

I’d default to cache-aside. It only caches data that is actually read, so memory isn’t wasted on write-once data; and if the cache goes down the system still works, just slower. The costs are that every miss pays an extra round trip, the first request for any key is slow, and there’s a window where the cache can be stale.

I’d choose write-through when reads must never be stale and writes are relatively rare — and I’d combine write-through with read-through so the app talks to one interface. I’d avoid write-back unless I can tolerate losing recent writes when the cache node dies.

What is a cache stampede and how do you prevent it?

Also called a thundering herd: a very popular key expires, and every concurrent request for it misses simultaneously and stampedes the database. A key that was serving 50 000 requests/second from RAM suddenly sends 50 000 queries/second at a database that can do 5 000 — and it collapses, which then keeps the cache empty, which prolongs the outage.

Prevention, in order of usefulness:

  • Single-flight / mutex: the first request to miss takes a lock and rebuilds; the rest wait briefly for that result, so the database sees one query.
  • Serve stale while revalidating: return the expired value immediately and refresh in the background — users never see the miss.
  • Probabilistic early expiration: as the TTL approaches, each request has a small growing chance of refreshing, so exactly one tends to do it before expiry.
  • TTL jitter: add a random ±10% so keys populated together don’t expire together — this also prevents the wider cache avalanche.
How do you handle a hot key that overwhelms one cache node?

The problem is that consistent hashing sends one key to exactly one node, so a viral item concentrates all its traffic on a single machine's CPU and NIC no matter how large the cluster is.

  • Local caching in front: each app server keeps the hot value in process memory with a short TTL of a few seconds. This is the most effective fix — traffic to the shared cache drops by the number of app servers.
  • Key replication: store the value under key#0…key#N on different nodes and have readers pick one at random, spreading the read load N ways.
  • Detect and promote: track per-key request rates and automatically promote keys crossing a threshold into the replicated scheme.
  • For write-hot keys like a global counter, shard the counter into N sub-counters and sum on read.
How do you keep the cache consistent with the database?

You accept that you cannot perfectly, and you choose a bounded staleness you can live with. The three practical approaches:

  • TTL: everything expires. Simple, self-healing, and works even when a code path forgets to invalidate. Staleness is bounded by the TTL.
  • Invalidate on write: after committing to the database, delete the cache key. Prefer delete over update, because two concurrent writers updating the cache can commit in one order and update the cache in the reverse order, leaving it permanently wrong.
  • Change data capture: tail the database’s replication log and invalidate from there. This catches every write including ones that bypass your application, and is what large systems actually do.

Order matters: write DB, then invalidate cache. The reverse leaves a window where a concurrent read repopulates the cache with the old value just before the DB is updated.

What is a CDN and how does it work?

A content delivery network is a set of geographically distributed edge servers that cache your content close to users. A request for cdn.example.com/logo.png is resolved — by anycast or geo-DNS — to the nearest point of presence. If that edge has the file cached, it serves it in ~10–30 ms; if not, it fetches from your origin once, caches it per the Cache-Control header, and serves everyone else locally.

The three benefits to state: latency, because physics limits how fast a packet crosses an ocean; origin offload, because 90%+ of bytes never reach your servers, which is often the largest single cost saving in a media-heavy product; and resilience, since the CDN absorbs traffic spikes and DDoS across its network.

For invalidation, the practical answer is not to invalidate: fingerprint asset filenames so a new build produces a new URL and old entries simply age out.

Design an LRU cache.

The requirement is O(1) get and O(1) put with eviction of the least recently used item, so I need constant-time lookup and constant-time reordering. That’s a hash map plus a doubly linked list.

  • The hash map maps key → pointer to the list node, giving O(1) lookup.
  • The doubly linked list keeps nodes in recency order — most recent at the head, least recent at the tail. Doubly linked matters because removing a node from the middle in O(1) requires knowing its neighbours.
  • get(k): look up the node, unlink it, move it to the head, return the value.
  • put(k,v): if present, update and move to head. If new and at capacity, remove the tail node and delete its key from the map, then insert at the head.

Use sentinel head and tail nodes to avoid null checks. For a real distributed cache, note that exact LRU is expensive at scale — Redis approximates it by sampling a few random keys and evicting the oldest among them, which is nearly as good and far cheaper.

When would you NOT use a cache?

When the hit rate would be low, because a cache that misses adds a network hop and complexity while saving nothing — for example unique per-user reports or a long tail of rarely-repeated queries. When staleness is unacceptable, such as a balance check at the instant of a transfer or an inventory count during a flash sale, though even there you can often cache with a very short TTL. When data changes on nearly every read, so the cache is invalidated as fast as it is filled. When the dataset is already small enough to sit in the database’s own buffer pool. And when the operational cost of an extra system — another thing to monitor, secure, size and fail over — outweighs a modest latency gain.

Also flag the security case: never cache personalised responses at a shared layer without including the user in the cache key.

Databases & Storage

SQL vs NoSQL without the religion, ACID, indexes, isolation levels, and how to pick storage in one sentence.

ACID — memorise the four

Atomicity
A transaction is all-or-nothing. If the debit succeeds and the credit fails, both are rolled back. There is no half-transfer.
Consistency
A transaction moves the database from one valid state to another, respecting constraints, foreign keys and triggers. (Note: this “C” is not the “C” in CAP — a classic trick question.)
Isolation
Concurrent transactions don’t corrupt each other; the result is as if they ran in some serial order. The degree of this is tunable — see isolation levels below.
Durability
Once committed, the data survives a crash — guaranteed by writing to a write-ahead log on durable storage before acknowledging.
BASE — the NoSQL counterpart

Basically Available, Soft state, Eventually consistent. Instead of guaranteeing correctness at every instant, the system stays available and converges to a correct state over time. It is not a weaker attempt at ACID; it is a deliberate choice to trade immediate consistency for availability and partition tolerance (Section 10).

Indexing — the highest-value database topic

An index is a separate sorted data structure that maps column values to row locations, turning an O(n) table scan into an O(log n) lookup. The cost is real: every index must be updated on every write, and it consumes disk.

  • B+ tree — the default in relational databases. Sorted, balanced, shallow (3–4 levels covers billions of rows), and supports range queries and ORDER BY because the leaves are linked in order.
  • Hash index — O(1) exact-match lookup, but useless for ranges or sorting.
  • LSM tree — used by Cassandra, RocksDB and LevelDB. Writes go to an in-memory table and an append-only log, then are flushed and compacted into sorted files. Optimised for write throughput at the cost of read amplification. This is the structural reason Cassandra is chosen for write-heavy workloads.
  • Composite index — an index on (a, b, c) obeys the leftmost prefix rule: it serves queries on a, (a,b) and (a,b,c), but not on b alone. Column order is a design decision.
  • Covering index — includes every column the query needs, so the database answers from the index without touching the table at all.
  • Clustered index — the table itself is physically stored in this order (the primary key in InnoDB). There can be only one, and it makes range scans on that key very fast.
  • Inverted index — word → list of documents containing it. The basis of all full-text search.
Index anti-patterns

Indexing every column (writes crawl and disk balloons) · indexing a low-cardinality column like a boolean (the database will scan anyway) · wrapping the indexed column in a function, e.g. WHERE YEAR(created_at)=2026, which prevents index use — write a range instead · forgetting that an index on (a,b) does not help a query on b.

Normalisation vs denormalisation

Normalise (3NF)

Each fact stored once. No update anomalies, smaller storage, integrity enforced by the schema. Cost: reads need joins, which get expensive at scale and impossible across shards.

Say it as a rule: normalise for correctness, denormalise for read performance, and only after you have measured. Also mention materialised views — precomputed query results refreshed periodically, which is denormalisation the database manages for you.

Interview questions · Section 08
SQL or NoSQL for this system — how do you decide?

I decide from the access pattern and the invariants, not from fashion. I ask four questions:

  • Are there invariants that must never break? Money, inventory, seat booking — I want multi-row ACID transactions, so relational.
  • Are queries known and key-based, or ad hoc? If every read is “get by user id”, a key–value store fits and scales trivially. If analysts will write arbitrary joins, I need SQL.
  • What’s the write volume? If writes exceed what one primary can absorb and the data partitions cleanly, a wide-column store like Cassandra is built for that.
  • Is the shape uniform? Heterogeneous, nested, rapidly-evolving documents suit a document store.

My default is PostgreSQL, because it handles far more scale than people assume and gives me transactions, joins and JSON columns in one system. I’d also happily use both — the relational database for orders and payments, Redis for sessions, Cassandra for the message history, S3 for media. Polyglot persistence is normal.

Explain ACID with a real example.

Take transferring ₹1 000 from A to B — a debit and a credit.

  • Atomicity: both statements succeed or neither does. If the credit fails, the debit is rolled back — money never vanishes.
  • Consistency: constraints hold before and after. If there’s a rule that balances can’t go negative, the transaction is rejected rather than committed into an invalid state.
  • Isolation: if A simultaneously transfers to C, the two transactions don’t interleave into a wrong balance. The outcome equals some serial order of the two.
  • Durability: once the user sees “transfer complete”, a power cut cannot undo it — the write-ahead log was flushed to durable storage before acknowledging.

Worth adding: the C in ACID means “satisfies your declared constraints”, which is a different thing from the C in CAP, which means “all replicas return the same value”.

How does a database index work, and what does it cost?

An index is an auxiliary sorted structure — usually a B+ tree — mapping column values to row locations. Without it, finding a row means scanning every row, O(n). With it, the database walks a tree of depth 3 or 4 even for billions of rows, so lookup is O(log n). B+ trees are used rather than hash tables because they keep the data sorted, which means they also serve range queries, ORDER BY and prefix matches, and their leaves are linked for efficient scans.

The costs: every INSERT, UPDATE and DELETE must update every affected index, so write throughput drops roughly linearly with index count; indexes consume significant disk and memory; and the optimiser can pick a bad plan when there are too many. So index the columns in your WHERE, JOIN and ORDER BY clauses, prefer composite indexes matching real query patterns, and drop indexes nothing uses.

What are isolation levels? What problem does each solve?

They control how much concurrent transactions can see of each other, trading correctness against throughput.

  • Read uncommitted allows dirty reads — seeing uncommitted data that may be rolled back. Almost never acceptable.
  • Read committed prevents dirty reads; you only see committed data. But reading the same row twice can give different answers. This is PostgreSQL’s default and is fine for most applications.
  • Repeatable read additionally guarantees a row read twice returns the same value, usually via a snapshot. MySQL’s default. Phantoms — new rows matching your range query — are still permitted by the standard.
  • Serializable guarantees the outcome equals some serial execution. Fully correct, and slowest, because it needs predicate locking or aborts on conflict.

In practice, use read committed and add explicit locking (SELECT … FOR UPDATE) or optimistic concurrency with a version column for the few operations that genuinely need it, rather than making everything serializable.

Where would you store user-uploaded images?

In object storage like S3, never in the database. Storing multi-megabyte blobs in a relational database bloats rows, destroys the buffer pool’s hit rate, makes backups and replication enormously slow, and gives you nothing a filesystem doesn’t.

The pattern: the client asks my API for a pre-signed upload URL, uploads the bytes directly to S3 without touching my servers, and then tells my API the object key. I store only the key, content type, size, owner and dimensions in the database. Serving goes through a CDN in front of the bucket. Asynchronously, a worker picks up an upload event and generates thumbnails and alternate resolutions. Private files are served via short-lived pre-signed download URLs so authorisation stays with my application.

Normalisation vs denormalisation — when do you denormalise?

I start normalised, because each fact living in exactly one place eliminates update anomalies and lets the schema enforce integrity. I denormalise when a specific, measured read path is too slow or too expensive — typically a hot query doing a multi-table join at high QPS, or a query that would need a join across shards, which is effectively impossible.

Concretely: storing the author’s display name on each post so rendering a feed doesn’t join to users; keeping a like_count column rather than counting a billion-row table; precomputing each user’s timeline. The price is that a write now updates several places, so I need either transactions, an async job, or acceptance of brief inconsistency — and a way to repair drift, usually a periodic reconciliation job. Denormalisation is a deliberate trade of write complexity for read speed, and I’d state it that way.

What is a write-ahead log?

A WAL is an append-only file where the database records every change before applying it to the data pages. On commit, only the log needs to be durably flushed — a sequential write, which is far faster than random writes scattered across data files. If the process crashes, recovery replays the log to restore committed transactions and roll back incomplete ones.

It’s the mechanism behind the D in ACID, and it does double duty: replication streams the same log to replicas, point-in-time recovery replays it to any moment, and change-data-capture tools tail it to feed caches, search indexes and data warehouses.

Replication, Partitioning & Consistent Hashing

Copies for availability, splits for scale — and the hashing trick that stops a resize from invalidating everything.

Two words candidates confuse

Replication = the same data on multiple machines. Buys availability, read scale and durability. Partitioning (sharding) = different data on different machines. Buys write scale and storage capacity. Real systems do both: shard the data, then replicate each shard.

Synchronous vs asynchronous replication

  • Synchronous — the leader waits for the replica to confirm before acknowledging the client. No data loss on failover, but every write pays the replica’s latency, and a slow replica stalls all writes.
  • Asynchronous — the leader acknowledges immediately and ships the log later. Fast, but if the leader dies before shipping, those writes are lost.
  • Semi-synchronous — wait for at least one replica, not all. The common production compromise.
Quorum arithmetic — learn this formula

With N replicas, W nodes acknowledging a write, and R nodes consulted on a read:

W + R > N guarantees the read set overlaps the write set, so at least one node returns the latest value.

N=3, W=2, R=2 → balanced (the common default) · W=1, R=3 → fast writes, slow reads · W=3, R=1 → fast reads, writes fail if any node is down · W=1, R=1 → fastest, no consistency guarantee.

Replication lag — the bug you must anticipate

With async replication, a replica is always slightly behind. That creates three concrete user-visible bugs, and naming them is a strong signal:

  • Read-your-own-writes violation: a user posts a comment, the read goes to a lagging replica, and their own comment is missing. Fix: route reads to the leader for a short window after a user writes, or track the write position in the session and require a replica at least that current.
  • Monotonic read violation: two consecutive reads hit different replicas and the data appears to go backwards in time. Fix: pin a user to one replica by hashing their id.
  • Causal violation: a reply appears before the message it replies to, because they replicated at different speeds. Fix: causal consistency tracking, or keep causally related data in one partition.

Consistent hashing, explained properly

The problem: with hash(key) mod N, going from 4 to 5 cache nodes changes the modulus, so almost every key maps somewhere new. Every cache misses at once and the database dies. Consistent hashing fixes exactly this.

  1. Map the hash space onto a ring Take the output range of a hash function (say 0 to 2³²−1) and treat it as a circle whose ends join.
  2. Place nodes on the ring Hash each server’s identifier to get its position.
  3. Place keys on the same ring Hash the key, then walk clockwise to the first node you meet. That node owns the key.
  4. Add a node It takes over only the keys sitting between it and its predecessor — on average K/N keys, not all K.
  5. Remove a node Its keys pass to the next node clockwise. Everything else is untouched.
  6. Add virtual nodes With few servers the ring is lumpy, so each physical node is hashed to ~100–200 positions. This evens the distribution and, when a node dies, spreads its load across many peers rather than dumping it all on one neighbour.

Used by: DynamoDB, Cassandra, Riak, Memcached client libraries, and CDN request routing. For replication, the key is stored on the next R distinct physical nodes clockwise, not just the first.

Sharding’s permanent costs — say these out loud
  • No cross-shard joins. The app must fetch from each shard and join in memory.
  • No cross-shard transactions without a saga or two-phase commit (Section 12).
  • Celebrity / hotspot problem: one key can be so hot that its shard saturates while others idle.
  • Rebalancing is an operational project, not a config change.
  • Choosing the shard key is irreversible in practice — pick one with high cardinality, even distribution, and which appears in most queries so you can route to a single shard.

Federation and other lighter alternatives

Before sharding, mention the cheaper options: federation (split by function — a users database, an orders database, a forum database — which spreads write load with none of the hash complexity), read replicas, archiving cold data, and vertical partitioning (move rarely-read wide columns to a side table).

Interview questions · Section 09
What is consistent hashing and why do we need it?

The problem it solves: if you place keys with hash(key) mod N and N changes — you add a cache node or one dies — then the modulus changes and nearly every key maps to a different node. For a cache tier that means a near-total miss storm hitting the database at once, which is how a routine scale-up becomes an outage.

Consistent hashing maps both keys and nodes onto a circular hash space. A key belongs to the first node found walking clockwise from its position. Now adding a node only steals the keys in the arc between it and its predecessor — on average K/N keys — and removing a node passes only its own arc to its successor. Everything else stays put.

One refinement matters: with few nodes the arcs are very unequal, so each physical node is hashed to ~150 virtual nodes scattered around the ring. That evens out load and means a failed node’s keys spread across many survivors rather than doubling one neighbour’s load. It’s used by DynamoDB, Cassandra and CDN routing.

Replication vs sharding — what does each solve?

Replication keeps identical copies on multiple machines. It solves availability (a replica takes over when the primary dies), read scalability (spread reads across replicas), durability (data survives a disk loss), and latency (place a replica near users). It does not help write throughput, because every write still goes to every copy.

Sharding splits different data across machines. It solves write throughput and storage capacity — each shard handles only its slice. It does not help availability; in fact it hurts it, because now any one shard being down makes part of your data unavailable.

Which is why production systems do both: shard the dataset for capacity, and replicate each shard three ways for safety.

How do you choose a shard key?

Four criteria, and getting it wrong is effectively unfixable:

  • High cardinality — enough distinct values to spread across many shards. Sharding by country gives you at most ~200 shards, unevenly.
  • Even distribution — no value should be dramatically more common. Sharding a chat app by chat id is better than by user id if some users are in enormous groups.
  • Present in most queries — otherwise every read becomes a scatter-gather across all shards, and you’ve made reads worse than a single database.
  • Keeps related data together — data you need to read or transact over atomically should land on one shard.

Concretely: for a chat app, shard by chat_id so a conversation’s messages live together. For a multi-tenant SaaS, shard by tenant_id. Avoid monotonically increasing keys like timestamps or auto-increment ids, because with range sharding all new writes hit the last shard. If a natural key is skewed, use a composite like hash(user_id) + bucket.

What is replication lag and what user-visible bugs does it cause?

Lag is the delay between a write committing on the leader and appearing on an asynchronous replica — usually milliseconds, but seconds or minutes under load or during a long transaction. Three named anomalies follow:

  • Reading your own writes fails: a user updates their profile, the next read hits a lagging replica, and the old value comes back — looking like the save didn’t work. Fix by routing that user’s reads to the leader for a short window, or by tracking the log position they wrote and requiring a replica at least that far along.
  • Monotonic reads violated: consecutive reads hit different replicas and time appears to run backwards. Fix by pinning a user to one replica via a hash of their id.
  • Causality violated: a reply is visible before the message it answers. Fix with causal consistency tracking or by co-locating causally related data.

Monitoring lag and failing reads over to the leader when it exceeds a threshold is standard practice.

Explain W + R > N.

In a leaderless quorum system with N replicas per key, a write is acknowledged once W replicas confirm it and a read consults R replicas and takes the newest version. If W + R > N, the write set and read set must overlap in at least one node by the pigeonhole principle — so any read is guaranteed to see at least one copy of the latest write, giving strong consistency.

The tuning is a latency/consistency dial. N=3, W=2, R=2 is the balanced default and tolerates one node down for both reads and writes. W=1, R=3 makes writes fast and reads slow — good for write-heavy logging. W=3, R=1 makes reads fast but writes fail if any replica is unavailable. W=1, R=1 is fastest and gives no guarantee, which is fine for a cache.

Systems like Cassandra and DynamoDB expose this per query, and use read repair plus anti-entropy (Merkle trees) to converge replicas that fell behind.

What happens if one shard becomes a hotspot?

That’s the celebrity problem: one key or range attracts disproportionate traffic, so its shard saturates while others sit idle, and adding shards doesn’t help because the load isn’t distributed.

  • Split the hot key: append a random bucket suffix (celebrity_id#0…#9) so writes spread across ten partitions, and fan out on read to recombine.
  • Cache it separately: hot items are by definition read-heavy, so an in-process cache on each app server absorbs most of it.
  • Special-case it: Twitter famously handles celebrity accounts with a hybrid fan-out — normal users get push-based timelines, celebrities are pulled at read time.
  • Dedicated shard: give the hot tenant its own hardware.
  • Better key up front: if hotspots are structural — like sharding by timestamp, where all writes hit the newest shard — change the key to hash-based or composite.
What is federation, and when is it better than sharding?

Federation, or functional partitioning, splits databases by feature rather than by key: a users database, an orders database, a forum database, each on its own machine. It spreads read and write load and shrinks each dataset, so caches and indexes fit in memory better — and it needs no hashing, no routing layer and no resharding.

It’s better than sharding when your load is spread across several distinct domains and no single table is the bottleneck, which describes most mid-sized systems. It’s also a natural stepping stone toward microservices. It stops helping when one single feature outgrows a machine on its own — at that point you must shard that feature’s data. The cost is losing joins and transactions across the federated databases, which is the same cost sharding imposes.

CAP, PACELC & Consistency Models

The most misquoted theorem in the industry. Get it exactly right and you separate yourself from most candidates.

The CAP theorem, stated correctly

In a distributed data store you can guarantee at most two of:

C · Consistency
Every read receives the most recent write, or an error. (This is linearizability, not the C of ACID.)
A · Availability
Every request receives a non-error response — though possibly not the most recent data.
P · Partition
tolerance
The system continues operating despite messages between nodes being dropped or delayed.
The correction that impresses interviewers

“Pick two” is misleading. Network partitions are not a design option — cables are cut, switches fail, packets are lost. Any real distributed system must tolerate partitions. So P is mandatory, and the theorem actually says: when a partition occurs, you must choose between C and A. CA systems only exist on a single machine.

PACELC — the more useful version

CAP only describes behaviour during a partition, which is rare. PACELC extends it to normal operation:

If there is a Partition → choose between Availability and Consistency;
Else (normal operation) → choose between Latency and Consistency.

This matters because even with a healthy network, guaranteeing consistency means waiting for other replicas to confirm, which costs latency. Classifications: Cassandra and DynamoDB are PA/EL (available under partition, low latency otherwise). MongoDB and HBase are PC/EC. Google Spanner is PC/EC — it buys consistency with atomic clocks and pays for it in write latency.

Conflict resolution when you chose availability

If two partitions both accepted writes, you must reconcile. The standard mechanisms:

  • Last Write Wins (LWW) — keep the write with the highest timestamp. Trivially simple and silently loses data; also unreliable because clocks drift between machines.
  • Version vectors / vector clocks — track per-node counters so you can tell whether two versions are causally ordered or genuinely concurrent. Concurrent versions are returned to the application as siblings to resolve (Dynamo does this).
  • CRDTs — Conflict-free Replicated Data Types are structured so that merges are automatic and order-independent: a grow-only counter, an add-wins set, a sequence type for collaborative text. This is how multiplayer document editors avoid conflicts entirely.
  • Application-level merge — the classic example is Amazon’s shopping cart, which unions items from both sides. Adding back a removed item annoys the user far less than losing an added one.
Read repair & anti-entropy

Eventually-consistent stores actively converge. On a quorum read, if one replica returns an old version, the coordinator writes the newer value back to it — that’s read repair. In the background, replicas compare Merkle trees (hash trees over key ranges) to find divergent ranges cheaply and sync only those — that’s anti-entropy. Mentioning these shows you know “eventually” isn’t magic.

Interview questions · Section 10
Explain the CAP theorem.

CAP says a distributed data store cannot simultaneously guarantee consistency, availability and partition tolerance. Consistency here means every read returns the most recent write — linearizability, which is a different thing from the C in ACID. Availability means every request gets a non-error response. Partition tolerance means the system keeps working when the network drops messages between nodes.

The important correction is that “pick two” is misleading. Partitions are a fact of networks, not a design choice — you cannot opt out of cables failing. So P is mandatory for any distributed system, and CAP really says: when a partition happens, you must choose between refusing requests to protect consistency, or serving possibly-stale data to stay available. A CA system is just a single machine.

And in practice the choice is per-operation. In a payments system I’d take CP for the ledger and AP for the transaction-history feed.

Is MongoDB CP or AP? Is Cassandra?

MongoDB is CP by default. It has a single primary per replica set; if a partition isolates the primary, the majority side elects a new one and the isolated primary steps down and stops accepting writes — so writes on the minority side fail rather than diverge. You can loosen this by reading from secondaries, which trades consistency for availability.

Cassandra is AP by default. It’s leaderless, so every replica accepts writes even during a partition, and divergence is reconciled afterwards through last-write-wins timestamps, read repair and anti-entropy. But it’s tunable: setting the consistency level to QUORUM for both reads and writes gives you W + R > N and therefore strong consistency, at the cost of failing when a quorum is unreachable.

The general point worth making: most modern databases are not fixed points on the CAP triangle — they expose the trade-off as a per-query setting.

What is eventual consistency? Give an example where it is fine and one where it is not.

Eventual consistency guarantees only that if writes stop, all replicas will converge to the same value. Reads in the meantime may return stale data, and different readers may see different values.

Fine: the like count on a post. If it shows 1 042 instead of 1 043 for two seconds, nobody is harmed, and the relaxed guarantee buys enormous write throughput and availability. Same for DNS, view counts, search index freshness, and “last seen” presence.

Not fine: decrementing inventory in a flash sale, where two nodes each seeing 1 item left will both sell it and you’ve oversold. Also bank balances at the moment of a withdrawal, seat and ticket booking, and anything involving uniqueness — like reserving a username. Those need strong consistency or an explicit reservation/compensation mechanism.

What is PACELC and why is it better than CAP?

PACELC extends CAP: if there is a Partition, choose between Availability and Consistency; Else — in normal operation — choose between Latency and Consistency.

It's more useful because partitions are rare, so CAP describes an exceptional case, while the latency-versus-consistency trade-off is what you actually pay for every single request, every day. If a write must be acknowledged by a quorum across three availability zones before returning, that's real milliseconds on every write regardless of network health.

So Cassandra is PA/EL — available during partitions, and fast rather than strict normally. Spanner is PC/EC — it chooses consistency in both cases, and pays with commit-wait latency from its TrueTime clock bounds. Naming the "else" branch shows you understand consistency has an everyday cost, not just a disaster-time one.

Two replicas accepted conflicting writes during a partition. How do you resolve it?

Options, from crudest to best:

  • Last write wins by timestamp — simple, and silently discards one user’s work. It also depends on synchronised clocks, which don’t exist reliably, so it can discard the newer write.
  • Version vectors: each replica keeps a counter, so the system can distinguish “B is a descendant of A” (safe — keep B) from “A and B are concurrent” (a genuine conflict). Concurrent siblings are handed to the application.
  • Application merge: domain knowledge decides. Amazon’s cart takes the union of both versions — reviving a deleted item is a smaller sin than losing an added one.
  • CRDTs: pick data types whose merge is mathematically guaranteed to converge regardless of order — counters, add-wins sets, sequence types for text. Then conflicts are impossible by construction, which is how collaborative editors work.

And the alternative worth stating: if conflicts are unacceptable for that data, don’t choose AP for it — route those writes through a single leader or a consensus group.

What is linearizability?

The strongest single-object consistency guarantee: the system behaves as though there is exactly one copy of the data, and every operation takes effect atomically at some instant between when it was invoked and when it returned. The practical consequence is that once a write completes, every subsequent read from any client anywhere sees it — there is no window in which different clients disagree.

It’s what a single-node database gives you for free, and what distributed systems like etcd, ZooKeeper and Spanner spend enormous effort to provide, using consensus protocols and — in Spanner’s case — atomic clocks. The cost is latency and reduced availability: a linearizable system must refuse requests during a partition rather than serve stale data. Use it for leader election, distributed locks, unique constraints and financial ledgers; don’t pay for it on a news feed.

Queues, Streams & Event-Driven Architecture

How to stop making users wait for work that doesn’t need them — and the delivery guarantees nobody gets right first time.

Why asynchronous processing exists

Consider uploading a video. Synchronously, the request must: store the file, transcode it into five resolutions, generate thumbnails, run moderation, notify subscribers, update search — then respond. The user waits four minutes, any single failure fails everything, and a traffic spike kills the server.

Asynchronously: store the file, publish an event, respond in 200 ms with “processing”. Workers consume the event and do the rest at their own pace.

The four things a queue buys you
  1. Decoupling — the producer doesn’t know or care who consumes; add a new consumer without touching the producer.
  2. Load levelling / buffering — a 10× traffic spike fills the queue rather than crashing the workers. The queue absorbs burst; workers drain at a steady rate.
  3. Resilience — if a consumer is down, messages wait instead of being lost. The user-facing path stays up even when a downstream service is broken.
  4. Retryability — a failed message goes back on the queue or into a dead-letter queue for inspection.

“Exactly once” is mostly a lie — say this and explain why

True exactly-once delivery is impossible over an unreliable network: the sender can never be certain whether a lost acknowledgement means “not processed” or “processed, ack lost”. What systems actually provide is exactly-once processing, built from at-least-once delivery plus one of: an idempotent consumer (a dedupe table keyed on message id), or an atomic commit of the side effect and the offset in one transaction (Kafka transactions). Design consumers to be idempotent and the whole problem dissolves.

Operating a queue: the four controls

  • Dead-letter queue (DLQ) — after N failed attempts a message is moved aside so it stops blocking the partition and can be inspected. Without one, a single malformed “poison” message can halt a consumer forever.
  • Backpressure — when consumers can’t keep up, the queue grows without bound and eventually fails. Responses: autoscale consumers on queue depth, shed or sample low-value messages, rate-limit producers, or apply priority queues so critical work drains first. Consumer lag is the number-one metric to alert on.
  • Ordering — global ordering costs throughput because it forces a single consumer. The standard compromise is ordering per key: hash the entity id to a partition, so all events for one order or one chat stay ordered while different entities process in parallel.
  • Visibility timeout / redelivery — a claimed message is hidden for a period; if the worker doesn’t ack in time it reappears for someone else. Set it longer than the worst-case processing time or you get accidental duplicates.

Event-driven architecture

Instead of services calling each other directly, services emit facts (“OrderPlaced”) and other services react. The order service doesn’t know that email, inventory, analytics and loyalty all care.

Gains

Loose coupling · add consumers with zero changes upstream · natural buffering · a replayable audit log of everything that happened.

Costs

Eventual consistency everywhere · debugging spans many services · no single place shows “what happens when an order is placed” · event schema changes are hard to coordinate.

Two patterns worth naming

Event sourcing — store the sequence of events as the source of truth rather than the current state; current state is a fold over the events. Gives perfect audit history and time travel; costs query complexity, so it’s usually paired with CQRS (Command Query Responsibility Segregation), where writes go to the event store and reads come from separately-maintained, denormalised read models.

Change data capture (CDC) — tail the database’s write-ahead log and publish every row change as an event (Debezium). This is how you keep a search index, a cache and a data warehouse in sync without asking every application to remember to publish.

Interview questions · Section 11
Why use a message queue at all?

Four reasons. Decoupling: the producer publishes a fact and doesn’t know or care who consumes it, so I can add a new consumer — say a fraud checker — without redeploying the order service. Load levelling: a queue absorbs bursts. If traffic spikes 10×, the queue grows and workers drain it at a steady rate, instead of the spike crashing everything. Resilience: if the email service is down, messages wait in the queue rather than failing the user’s checkout. Responsiveness: the user gets a 200 in 200 ms instead of waiting for transcoding, emails and index updates.

The costs I’d name unprompted: everything downstream becomes eventually consistent, so the UI must show “processing” states; debugging is harder because the flow is no longer a stack trace; and I now have to operate and monitor the broker, watching consumer lag as the key signal.

Kafka vs RabbitMQ — when do you pick each?

Kafka is a distributed, partitioned, retained log. Consumers track their own offset, so messages aren't destroyed on read and multiple independent consumer groups read the same stream at their own pace — and a new service can replay from the beginning. It does millions of messages/second and guarantees ordering within a partition. Pick it for event streaming, analytics pipelines, CDC, activity feeds, and anywhere you might want to reprocess history.

RabbitMQ is a traditional broker with rich routing — topic and fanout exchanges, per-message TTLs, priority queues, per-message acks and easy DLQs. Messages are deleted once acknowledged. Pick it for task/job distribution, request-reply, complex routing rules, and when you need per-message control rather than a stream.

Short version: Kafka for streams of events you may want to replay; RabbitMQ for jobs you want done once.

How do you guarantee exactly-once processing?

I'd start by pushing back gently: exactly-once delivery is impossible over an unreliable network, because a lost acknowledgement is indistinguishable from lost work, so the sender must either retry (risking a duplicate) or not (risking a loss). What's achievable is exactly-once effect.

The reliable way is at-least-once delivery plus an idempotent consumer. Every message carries a unique id; before processing, the consumer inserts that id into a dedupe table with a unique constraint — if the insert fails, it's a duplicate and is skipped. Crucially, the dedupe insert and the business side effect must be in the same transaction, otherwise a crash between them reopens the hole.

Alternatives: design the operation to be naturally idempotent (SET balance = 500 rather than balance += 100), or use a broker's transactional support such as Kafka's atomic write-and-commit-offset. And if the downstream is an external API, pass an idempotency key so it deduplicates.

What is a dead-letter queue and why do you need one?

A DLQ is a separate queue where messages go after failing processing N times. You need it because of poison messages: a malformed or unprocessable message that fails, gets redelivered, fails again, forever. In an ordered partition it blocks every message behind it — head-of-line blocking — so one bad record halts the pipeline. Without a DLQ your options are lose the message or stop the consumer.

With one, the message is set aside after its retry budget, the consumer moves on, and an engineer can inspect, fix and replay it. In practice: retry a few times with exponential backoff (transient failures usually resolve), then DLQ, and alert on DLQ depth — a sudden rise usually means a bad deploy or a schema change upstream.

Your consumers can’t keep up and the queue is growing. What do you do?

First, confirm it's real by looking at consumer lag and whether it's growing linearly or exponentially. Then:

  • Scale consumers out — but note the ceiling: in Kafka, parallelism is capped by partition count, so you may need to add partitions first.
  • Make consumers faster — batch database writes, remove synchronous calls from the hot path, profile the actual bottleneck.
  • Shed or prioritise load — use a priority queue so payments drain before analytics; sample or drop low-value messages like debug telemetry.
  • Apply backpressure to producers — rate-limit or reject at the API edge, so the queue doesn't grow unboundedly and eventually fail.
  • Autoscale on queue depth rather than CPU, so this handles itself next time.

The thing to avoid is silently letting the queue grow past its retention, because then you're losing messages without knowing it.

How do you preserve message ordering?

You almost never need global ordering, and it's very expensive — it forces a single partition and a single consumer, capping throughput at one machine. What you usually need is ordering per entity.

The standard technique is to partition by key: hash the entity id (order id, chat id, user id) to choose the partition. All events for that entity then land on the same partition, which is an ordered append-only log consumed by exactly one consumer in the group — so they're processed in order — while different entities process in parallel across partitions.

Two caveats. Adding partitions later changes the key→partition mapping and can break ordering during the transition. And retries can reorder: if message 1 fails and is retried while message 2 succeeds, order is lost — so either block the partition on failure, or make consumers order-independent by including a sequence number and ignoring stale updates.

What is event sourcing and CQRS?

Event sourcing stores the full sequence of state-changing events as the source of truth, instead of only the current state. A bank account isn't a balance column; it's every deposit and withdrawal ever, and the balance is derived by replaying them (with periodic snapshots so you don't replay from the beginning). Benefits: a complete audit trail for free, the ability to reconstruct state at any past moment, and the ability to build a brand-new read model by replaying history. Costs: querying is awkward, event schemas must be versioned forever, and it's genuinely harder for the team to reason about.

CQRS separates the write model from the read model. Commands go through the domain model and emit events; queries are served from separate, denormalised read stores shaped exactly for each screen. It pairs naturally with event sourcing because it solves its query problem, and it lets reads and writes scale independently. The cost is eventual consistency between the two sides — the UI must handle a just-written item not yet appearing.

I'd apply both selectively, to a domain that genuinely needs auditability like payments or orders, not across a whole system.

Distributed Transactions & Idempotency

What replaces BEGIN … COMMIT once your data lives in five services.

Inside one database, a transaction is easy. Across services — “charge the card, reserve the seat, send the ticket” — there is no shared transaction manager, and any step can fail after earlier steps succeeded. This section is the standard set of answers.

Two-phase commit (2PC)

A coordinator runs a vote. Phase 1 (prepare): ask every participant “can you commit?” Each does the work, locks the rows, writes to its log and answers yes/no. Phase 2 (commit/abort): if all said yes, tell everyone to commit; otherwise tell everyone to abort.

Why 2PC is rarely used across services
  • Blocking: if the coordinator dies after phase 1, participants sit holding locks indefinitely, unsure what to do.
  • Locks held across the network for the whole protocol — disastrous for throughput.
  • The coordinator is a SPOF and availability multiplies down: all N participants must be up.
  • Requires every participant to support the protocol — most modern services and NoSQL stores don’t.

It is still used within a trusted boundary — across shards of one database, or in Kafka’s transactional producer. For cross-service work, use a saga.

The Saga pattern

A saga replaces one distributed transaction with a sequence of local transactions, each with a defined compensating action that semantically undoes it. If step 4 fails, run the compensations for steps 3, 2, 1 in reverse.

Saga styleHowProsCons
ChoreographyEach service listens for events and emits the next one. No central brain.Fully decoupled; no SPOF; simple for 2–3 stepsThe flow exists nowhere as a whole; hard to debug; cyclic dependencies creep in

Rule of thumb: choreography for short flows, orchestration once you have four or more steps or non-trivial branching. Production systems often use a workflow engine (Temporal, AWS Step Functions, Camunda) as the orchestrator so state and retries are durable.

The transactional outbox

The dual-write problem

Your service must write to its database and publish an event. There is no transaction across both. If you commit then publish, a crash in between loses the event. If you publish then commit, a rollback leaves an event about something that never happened.

Solution — outbox pattern: in the same local transaction, write the business row and insert the event into an outbox table. A separate relay process (or CDC tailing the WAL) reads unpublished outbox rows and publishes them, marking them sent. Now both writes are atomic because they’re in one database, and the event is guaranteed to be published at least once. This is the standard answer and it is worth memorising.

Idempotency in practice

Since almost every reliable system delivers at least once, consumers and APIs must tolerate repeats. The concrete mechanism:

1  Client generates a UUID → sends it as the Idempotency-Key header
2  Server opens a transaction
3    INSERT INTO idempotency (key, status) VALUES (?, 'IN_PROGRESS')
         -- UNIQUE constraint on key
4    if duplicate-key error → this is a retry:
             return the stored response (or 409 if still in progress)
5    perform the business operation (charge the card)
6    UPDATE idempotency SET status='DONE', response=? WHERE key=?
7  COMMIT   -- side effect and dedupe record commit together
  • The unique constraint is what makes this safe under concurrency — two simultaneous retries can’t both pass.
  • Keys need a TTL (24 hours is typical) so the table doesn’t grow forever.
  • Prefer naturally idempotent operations where possible: SET status = 'PAID' is safe to repeat; balance = balance - 100 is not.

Optimistic vs pessimistic concurrency

Pessimistic

Lock the row before reading (SELECT … FOR UPDATE). Nobody else can touch it. Correct and simple, but locks hurt throughput, can deadlock, and don’t work well across services or long think-time.

Optimistic

Read a version number, then write with WHERE version = old_version. If zero rows update, someone else won — re-read and retry. No locks; excellent when conflicts are rare, wasteful when they’re common.

Use pessimistic for genuinely contended resources (the last seat), optimistic for the common case of rare conflicts (editing a profile).

Interview questions · Section 12
How do you do a transaction across microservices?

You don't, in the ACID sense — there's no shared transaction manager, and two-phase commit is a poor fit across services because it holds locks over the network, blocks indefinitely if the coordinator dies, and requires every participant to support it.

Instead I'd use a saga: break the operation into a sequence of local transactions, each of which commits independently and each of which has a compensating transaction that semantically undoes it. Order flow: create order (pending) → reserve inventory → charge payment → book shipping → mark confirmed. If shipping fails, run compensations backwards: refund the payment, release the inventory, cancel the order.

Two things I'd flag. First, a compensation is not a rollback — the charge genuinely happened and the customer may see it, so compensations must be designed as real business operations. Second, sagas give atomicity of outcome but no isolation: other requests can observe intermediate states, so you may need semantic locks or a pending status. For anything beyond three steps I'd use an orchestrator, ideally a durable workflow engine, so the state machine and retries survive restarts.

The best answer, though, is often to avoid the problem: if two pieces of data must change atomically, that's strong evidence they belong in the same service and the same database.

What is two-phase commit and why is it rarely used?

2PC is a consensus protocol for atomic commit across participants. In the prepare phase, a coordinator asks every participant whether it can commit; each does the work, takes locks, durably logs its readiness and votes. In the commit phase, if all voted yes the coordinator tells everyone to commit, otherwise to abort. It genuinely gives atomicity.

It's avoided across services for four reasons. It's blocking: if the coordinator crashes after prepare, participants hold locks and cannot decide alone — they're stuck. It holds locks across network round trips, which destroys throughput. It reduces availability, since every participant must be reachable, so availabilities multiply. And it requires protocol support that most services and NoSQL stores simply don't have.

It is still used inside trusted boundaries — across shards of a single distributed database, or in Kafka's transactional writes — where the coordinator is highly available and the participants are homogeneous. Three-phase commit adds a step to reduce blocking but is rarely used in practice.

Explain the outbox pattern.

It solves the dual-write problem: a service needs to update its database and publish an event, and there's no transaction spanning both. Commit-then-publish loses the event if the process crashes in between; publish-then-commit can announce something that then rolls back.

The outbox pattern makes both writes local. In one database transaction, the service writes the business change and inserts a row into an outbox table describing the event. Since it's one transaction in one database, it's atomic. A separate relay — either a poller or, better, change-data-capture tailing the write-ahead log — reads unsent outbox rows and publishes them to the broker, marking them sent.

The guarantee is at-least-once publication, because the relay can crash after publishing but before marking. That's fine: consumers should be idempotent anyway. It's the standard, boring, correct answer and it works for cache invalidation and search-index updates too.

How do you make a payment API idempotent?

The client generates a unique idempotency key — a UUID — for each logical payment attempt and sends it as a header. Retries of the same logical payment reuse the same key.

On the server, inside a single transaction: insert the key into an idempotency table with a unique constraint. If the insert succeeds, this is the first attempt — perform the charge, store the response against the key, and commit, so the charge and the dedupe record become durable together. If the insert violates the unique constraint, this is a retry: return the stored response if the original finished, or a 409 if it's still in flight so the client backs off rather than racing.

Details that matter: the unique constraint, not an application-level check, is what makes it safe under concurrency; the charge and the record must share one transaction; keys need a TTL so the table doesn't grow forever; and the key should be bound to the request payload so reusing a key with different amounts is rejected rather than silently returning the wrong response.

Optimistic or pessimistic locking for a ticket booking system?

It depends on contention, and a booking system has both regimes.

For a normal event where thousands of seats are available and two people rarely want the same one, optimistic is better: read the seat with its version number, then UPDATE … WHERE seat_id = ? AND version = ?. If zero rows change, someone beat you — return "seat taken" and let the user pick another. No locks, no deadlocks, high throughput.

For a high-demand sale where ten thousand people fight for the same front-row seat, optimistic locking degenerates: almost every attempt fails and retries, wasting work. There I'd use pessimistic locking (SELECT … FOR UPDATE) with a short timeout, or better, sidestep contention with a reservation model — a queue or an atomic Redis operation grants a short-lived hold on a seat, and the user completes payment within that window while a background job expires abandoned holds.

The general rule: optimistic when conflicts are rare, pessimistic when they're common, and redesign to avoid the shared hot row when they're extreme.

Choreography or orchestration for a saga?

Choreography has each service react to events and emit the next one, with no central controller. It's maximally decoupled and has no single point of failure, and it's fine for two or three steps. Its weakness is that the overall workflow exists nowhere — to answer "what happens when an order is placed?" you must read five codebases, and debugging a stuck order means correlating logs across all of them. Cyclic dependencies also creep in over time.

Orchestration puts the flow in one service that calls each step and decides what to do on failure. The workflow is explicit, testable, monitorable, and easy to change. The cost is another service to build, deploy and keep available, and a mild tendency toward centralising business logic.

My default beyond three steps is orchestration, implemented on a durable workflow engine like Temporal or Step Functions, so the saga state, timeouts and retries survive a crash of the orchestrator itself — otherwise you've just moved the reliability problem.

Coordination, Consensus & Unique IDs

Leader election, distributed locks, Raft in plain English — and how to generate a billion unique IDs a second.

Why coordination is hard

In a distributed system you cannot tell the difference between “the node is dead”, “the node is slow”, and “the network to the node is broken”. Every coordination problem descends from that single ambiguity. Two consequences worth stating:

  • There is no reliable global clock. Machine clocks drift and NTP corrections can jump backwards, so timestamps cannot be trusted for ordering across nodes. This is why last-write-wins is fragile and why Google built Spanner’s TrueTime with GPS and atomic clocks.
  • FLP impossibility: in a fully asynchronous network with even one faulty node, no algorithm can guarantee consensus in bounded time. Real systems dodge this with timeouts and randomisation — they give up perfect guarantees for practical ones.

Consensus: Raft in plain English

Consensus means getting a group of nodes to agree on a value (or a sequence of values) despite failures. Paxos is the famous one and is famously hard to understand; Raft was designed to be teachable and is what etcd, Consul and CockroachDB use. It has three parts:

  1. Leader election Every node is a follower with a randomised election timeout. If a follower hears no heartbeat before its timeout, it becomes a candidate, increments the term number and requests votes. A node that wins votes from a majority becomes leader. Randomised timeouts make split votes rare, and a fresh election resolves them.
  2. Log replication All client writes go to the leader, which appends to its log and sends the entry to followers. Once a majority have persisted it, the entry is committed and applied to the state machine, and the leader responds to the client.
  3. Safety A candidate can only win if its log is at least as up to date as the voters’, which guarantees committed entries are never lost. Terms act as logical clocks so stale leaders are detected and step down.
Why majority quorum, and why odd numbers

A cluster of N nodes tolerates ⌊(N−1)/2⌋ failures, because any two majorities must overlap in at least one node — which prevents two leaders from being elected.

3 nodes → tolerate 1 failure · 5 nodes → tolerate 2 · 4 nodes → still only 1 (majority of 4 is 3). That’s why consensus clusters are always sized 3, 5 or 7 — an even number adds cost without adding fault tolerance.

Split brain

Two nodes both believing they are leader, accepting conflicting writes. Majority quorum prevents it: a partitioned minority cannot reach a majority and so cannot elect a leader, and any old leader that finds itself in the minority steps down. Additional protection comes from fencing tokens — a monotonically increasing number issued with the lock, which storage rejects if it’s older than the last one seen, so a resurrected zombie leader can’t do damage.

Distributed locks

Sometimes exactly one node must do something — run a cron job, process a queue, be the writer for a shard.

  • Redis-based: SET lock_key node_id NX PX 30000 — set only if absent, with a 30-second expiry so a crashed holder’s lock is released. Release must check ownership (a Lua script comparing the value before deleting), or you might delete someone else’s lock.
  • The correctness problem: the lock can expire while the holder is still working (a long GC pause), so two nodes believe they hold it. The fix is fencing tokens, as above — the resource itself rejects writes carrying a stale token.
  • ZooKeeper/etcd-based: stronger, because they’re built on consensus with sessions and ephemeral nodes that vanish when a client disconnects. Slower, but correct — use these when correctness actually matters.
  • Best answer: avoid distributed locks. Partition the work so each node owns a disjoint key range, or use a single-writer-per-partition model, or use atomic database operations and optimistic concurrency instead.

Interview questions · Section 13
How does leader election work?

I'd describe Raft, since it's the one used in practice. Every node starts as a follower with a randomised election timeout. The leader sends periodic heartbeats; if a follower doesn't receive one before its timeout, it assumes the leader is gone, increments the term number, becomes a candidate and requests votes from everyone.

Each node votes for at most one candidate per term, and only for a candidate whose log is at least as up to date as its own — which is what guarantees committed entries are never lost. A candidate that receives votes from a majority becomes leader and starts heartbeating. The randomised timeouts make simultaneous candidacies rare, and if a split vote does happen, no one gets a majority and a new election starts after another random delay.

Majority is the key idea: it makes split brain impossible, because two majorities of the same cluster must overlap in at least one node, and that node won't vote twice in a term. It also means an N-node cluster tolerates ⌊(N−1)/2⌋ failures, which is why clusters are sized 3, 5 or 7 — a 4-node cluster tolerates the same single failure as a 3-node one.

Design a distributed unique ID generator.

I'd clarify the requirements first: uniqueness across all nodes, high throughput, ideally roughly sortable by time (so it works well as a database primary key and for pagination), and no single point of failure.

My answer is Snowflake: a 64-bit integer split into 1 unused sign bit, 41 bits of millisecond timestamp relative to a custom epoch (about 69 years of range), 10 bits of machine id (1 024 nodes), and 12 bits of a per-millisecond sequence counter (4 096 per node per millisecond). That's roughly 4 billion IDs a second cluster-wide, generated with zero coordination per ID, and sortable by creation time because the timestamp is in the high bits.

Trade-offs I'd raise unprompted: machine ids must be uniquely assigned, typically by ZooKeeper or the container orchestrator at startup. Clock skew is the real risk — if the clock jumps backwards, the node must refuse to issue IDs until it catches up, or you get duplicates. And the ID leaks creation time, which is sometimes a privacy concern.

Alternatives: UUID v4 needs no coordination at all but is 128 bits and unsortable, and its randomness fragments B-tree indexes, badly hurting insert performance. UUID v7 fixes the ordering. A ticket server handing out ID blocks is simpler but adds a central dependency.

How do you implement a distributed lock, and what can go wrong?

The common implementation is Redis: SET lock_key unique_owner_id NX PX 30000 — set only if the key doesn't exist, with a 30-second expiry. The NX makes acquisition atomic, and the expiry means a crashed holder's lock is eventually released rather than deadlocking forever. Releasing must verify ownership before deleting, via a Lua script, so you never delete a lock someone else now holds.

What goes wrong is the expiry race: the holder pauses — a long garbage collection, a disk stall, a network partition — the lock expires, another node acquires it, and now two nodes believe they hold the lock and both write. No timeout value fixes this, because you cannot bound a pause.

The correct mitigation is a fencing token: the lock service issues a monotonically increasing number with each grant, the holder includes it in every write, and the storage system rejects any write with a token lower than the highest it has seen. The zombie's stale write is refused.

For real correctness I'd use etcd or ZooKeeper, which are consensus-backed with sessions and ephemeral nodes. But the best answer is usually to avoid the lock — partition work so each node owns a disjoint key range, or use atomic database operations and optimistic concurrency instead.

What is split brain and how do you prevent it?

Split brain is when a network partition leaves two halves of a cluster each believing it is in charge, so both accept writes. The data diverges, and when the partition heals you have conflicting histories with no clean way to merge them — in a financial system that's genuinely dangerous.

The primary prevention is majority quorum: require more than half the nodes to agree before electing a leader or committing a write. Two disjoint majorities of the same cluster cannot exist, so at most one side can operate; the minority side becomes read-only or unavailable. That's a deliberate CAP choice — consistency over availability.

Supporting mechanisms: fencing tokens so a resurrected old leader's writes are rejected by storage; lease-based leadership, where a leader must renew its lease and stops serving if it can't; and, in hardware clusters, STONITH — literally powering off the other node so it can't come back and cause trouble.

Why are consensus clusters always 3, 5 or 7 nodes?

Because fault tolerance is determined by the majority requirement, and adding an even-numbered node doesn't raise it. A cluster of N tolerates ⌊(N−1)/2⌋ failures: 3 nodes need a majority of 2, so they tolerate 1 failure; 4 nodes need a majority of 3, so they still tolerate only 1 — you've paid for an extra machine and gained nothing, while making every write wait for one more acknowledgement. 5 nodes need 3 and tolerate 2.

Beyond 7 it rarely makes sense either, because every write must reach a majority, so latency rises with cluster size while the marginal fault tolerance is small. The usual pattern is a small consensus group (3 or 5) managing metadata, with the actual data replicated more widely by cheaper mechanisms.

Why can’t you rely on timestamps to order events across servers?

Because there is no global clock. Machine clocks drift at meaningfully different rates, NTP corrections can move a clock backwards, virtual machines can have their clocks frozen and jumped, and network delay means you can never observe two clocks simultaneously. So an event with an earlier timestamp may genuinely have happened after one with a later timestamp — which makes last-write-wins conflict resolution capable of discarding the newer write.

The alternatives: logical clocks (Lamport timestamps) give a consistent total order that respects causality without needing real time; vector clocks go further and can tell whether two events are causally ordered or genuinely concurrent, so you can detect conflicts rather than silently losing one. And Google Spanner takes the expensive route — TrueTime, backed by GPS and atomic clocks, gives a bounded uncertainty interval, and transactions simply wait out that interval before committing so external consistency is guaranteed.

Reliability, Resilience & Rate Limiting

Designing for the assumption that everything fails — plus the four rate-limiting algorithms you must be able to draw.

Retries, and how they cause outages

Retrying a failed request is the most obvious resilience measure and the most dangerous one. If a service is struggling and every client retries three times, you have just tripled the load on a system that is already failing — a retry storm. Do it properly:

  • Only retry retryable errors. A 500 or a timeout, yes. A 400 or 403 will fail identically forever.
  • Exponential backoff: wait 1 s, 2 s, 4 s, 8 s… so pressure decreases as failure persists.
  • Jitter: add randomness to the wait. Without it, every client that failed at the same moment retries at the same moment, producing synchronised waves. Jitter is the difference between a working retry policy and a broken one.
  • Cap attempts and total time — usually 3 attempts, and never retry past the caller’s own deadline.
  • Only retry idempotent operations, or use an idempotency key (Section 12).
  • Retry budgets: allow retries to be at most ~10% of total requests; beyond that, stop.

Circuit breaker

If a dependency is down, calling it repeatedly wastes threads, adds latency to every request and can exhaust your connection pool — the failure spreads upward. A circuit breaker wraps the call and fails fast instead.

The rest of the resilience toolkit

Timeouts

Every network call needs one. Without it, a hung dependency holds your threads until you run out and the whole service dies. Set them from the p99 of the dependency, and make them shorter as you go deeper so an inner call can’t outlive its caller.

Bulkhead

Isolate resources per dependency — separate thread pools or connection pools — so a flood to one downstream can’t consume every thread and take down features that don’t use it. Named after a ship’s watertight compartments.

Graceful degradation

Ship a reduced experience rather than an error. Recommendations down → show bestsellers. Personalisation down → show the generic page. Decide the fallback per feature before the incident.

Load shedding

When overloaded, reject a fraction of requests immediately — prioritising by user tier or request type. Serving 80% well beats serving 100% so slowly they all time out.

Backpressure

Signal upstream to slow down rather than silently queueing forever. Bounded queues that reject when full are the simplest correct implementation.

Chaos engineering

Deliberately kill instances and inject latency in production (Netflix’s Chaos Monkey) so failure modes are discovered on a Tuesday afternoon, not during a sale.

Cascading failure — the mechanism to be able to describe

One service slows down → its callers’ threads block waiting → their thread pools exhaust → they become unresponsive → their callers block → the whole system is down, though only one component actually broke. Timeouts, circuit breakers and bulkheads exist specifically to break this chain. The recovery trap is the thundering herd on restart: everything reconnects at once and knocks the recovered service straight back over — so restarts need staggering and caches need warming.

Interview questions · Section 14
Design a rate limiter.

I'd clarify first: rate limit per user, per IP or per API key? What limits — say 100 requests per minute? Is it a single service or a distributed fleet? Must it be exact, or is approximate acceptable? And should excess requests be rejected or queued?

Assuming a distributed API with per-user limits, I'd use a token bucket or sliding window counter, implemented in Redis and enforced at the API gateway so rejected requests never reach business logic.

For token bucket, I store two fields per user — token count and last-refill timestamp. On each request, lazily refill based on elapsed time (no background job needed), then atomically decrement if tokens remain, else return 429. The whole thing runs as a Lua script so the read-modify-write is atomic and race-free across gateway nodes.

I'd pick token bucket because it permits short bursts while enforcing the average rate, which matches how real clients behave, and it's only two numbers per user. Fixed window is simpler but has the boundary problem — a client can send double the limit across a window edge. Sliding window log is exact but stores a timestamp per request, which doesn't scale.

Scaling notes: the Redis call adds a round trip to every request, so for very high throughput I'd shard Redis by user id and consider local per-node counters with a fraction of the budget, accepting slight over-admission for a large latency win. If Redis is unavailable I'd fail open — better to allow traffic than to take down the API — and I'd return 429 with Retry-After and the standard rate-limit headers.

Token bucket vs leaky bucket?

Token bucket accumulates tokens at a fixed rate up to a capacity; each request consumes one. If a client has been idle, tokens have built up, so it can send a burst — then it's throttled to the refill rate. It enforces an average rate while tolerating bursts, which is what you want for user-facing APIs, since real usage is bursty and rejecting a legitimate burst is annoying.

Leaky bucket puts requests into a fixed-size FIFO queue drained at a constant rate. Output is perfectly smooth regardless of input shape; overflow is dropped. It's what you want when the thing downstream is fragile and cannot absorb bursts at all — a legacy system, a third-party API with hard limits, or a network shaper.

So: token bucket to protect against abuse while staying friendly to real clients; leaky bucket to protect a downstream that needs an even rate. Token bucket is the more common default.

What is a circuit breaker and when does it help?

It's a wrapper around calls to a dependency that tracks the failure rate. It has three states. Closed: calls pass through normally while failures are counted. If the failure rate crosses a threshold — say 50% over the last 20 calls — it trips open, and every subsequent call fails immediately without touching the network. After a cool-down it moves to half-open, letting a single trial request through: success closes it, failure reopens it.

It helps because calling a dead dependency is worse than useless. Each call blocks a thread for the full timeout, so under load your thread pool exhausts and your service becomes unresponsive too — that's how one failing component cascades into a total outage. Failing fast keeps threads free, so you can serve a fallback and stay up. It also gives the struggling dependency room to recover instead of being hammered.

Pair it with a sensible fallback — cached data, a default, or a clear degraded response — and with bulkheads so each dependency has its own resource pool.

Why do retries need jitter?

Because without it, retries synchronise. If a service blips and ten thousand clients fail at the same instant, then all ten thousand back off for exactly 1 second and retry at exactly the same moment — a wave that's as big as the original load. The service fails again, they all wait 2 seconds, and retry together again. The system oscillates and never recovers, even though capacity would be sufficient if the load were spread out.

Jitter randomises each client's wait, spreading retries across the interval so the recovering service sees a smooth ramp instead of a spike. "Full jitter" — sleeping a random amount between 0 and the exponential backoff value — works well in practice.

I'd add the other retry rules while I'm there: only retry idempotent operations and retryable errors, cap the attempts, respect the caller's overall deadline, and enforce a retry budget so retries never exceed ~10% of traffic. Otherwise the retry policy itself becomes the outage.

How would you prevent a cascading failure?

Cascading failure is when one slow component consumes its callers' threads, making them unresponsive, which propagates upward until everything is down. The defences layer:

  • Timeouts everywhere — no unbounded waits. Make them tighter the deeper you go, so an inner call can never outlive its caller's deadline.
  • Circuit breakers so a dead dependency fails fast instead of consuming resources.
  • Bulkheads — separate thread and connection pools per dependency, so a flood to one can't starve the rest.
  • Load shedding — reject excess requests immediately rather than queueing them into timeout.
  • Backpressure and bounded queues so overload propagates as a fast rejection, not an infinite queue.
  • Graceful degradation — a defined fallback per feature so a broken dependency costs a feature, not the page.
  • Retry budgets and jitter so the recovery attempt isn't itself the second outage.

And the recovery detail people forget: stagger restarts and warm caches, because everything reconnecting simultaneously will knock the recovered service straight back down.

How do you rate limit across many servers?

The naive approach — a counter in each server's memory — fails, because with 10 gateway nodes a user gets 10× the intended limit, and it gets worse as you autoscale.

The standard fix is shared state in Redis: all nodes read and write the same per-user counter using atomic operations. Use a Lua script so the check-and-decrement is a single atomic step, otherwise concurrent requests race past the limit. Shard Redis by user id so no single node is a bottleneck.

The cost is a network round trip on every request, which at very high QPS matters. The common optimisation is a hybrid: each node gets a local share of the budget and enforces locally, syncing with Redis periodically. That's approximate — a user might slightly exceed the limit briefly — but it removes the per-request round trip entirely, and for abuse prevention approximate is fine.

Finally, decide the failure mode deliberately: if Redis is down, I'd fail open and allow traffic, because taking down the whole API to enforce a rate limit is a worse outcome than briefly not enforcing it.

The answer that shows maturity

“I’d start with a well-structured modular monolith and extract services when there’s a concrete reason — a module needing to scale differently, a team needing to deploy independently, or a genuine technology mismatch. Microservices solve an organisational problem more than a technical one; adopting them before you have the organisation or the platform maturity buys you distributed-systems difficulty with none of the benefit.” This is what senior engineers actually believe, and it lands far better than reflexively drawing twelve services.

How to draw service boundaries

  • By business capability / bounded context — Orders, Payments, Inventory, Identity. Not by technical layer (never a “database service”).
  • High cohesion, low coupling — things that change together belong together. If two services are always deployed in lockstep, they are one service.
  • Data ownership — each service owns its data exclusively. Others ask via its API; they never read its tables. Shared databases are the number-one microservices anti-pattern, because they recreate coupling invisibly.
  • Transaction boundaries — if two things must change atomically, that’s strong evidence they belong in one service. Otherwise you’ve signed up for a saga.
  • Team topology — Conway’s Law says your architecture will mirror your communication structure, so design services around teams that can own them end to end.

Supporting infrastructure microservices demand

API gateway

Single entry point: auth, rate limiting, routing, aggregation, protocol translation.

Service discovery

A registry so services find healthy instances as they come and go.

Service mesh

Sidecar proxies (Envoy/Istio/Linkerd) that handle mTLS, retries, timeouts, circuit breaking and traffic-splitting outside application code.

Distributed tracing

Correlation ids propagated across every hop, so one user request is one visible trace. Non-negotiable.

Centralised logging

Aggregated, structured, searchable — SSHing into 40 machines is not a debugging strategy.

BFF

Backend-for-Frontend: a thin per-client layer (web, iOS, Android) that aggregates and shapes responses so clients aren’t chatty.

The distributed monolith — the worst outcome

Services split apart but still tightly coupled: they share a database, must be deployed together, and every feature touches five repos. You now pay the full cost of distribution — network failures, eventual consistency, tracing, ops — while keeping every constraint of a monolith. Signs: “we can’t deploy A without B”, “this change needs four PRs”, synchronous call chains five deep.

Serverless

Functions triggered by events, with the provider handling all infrastructure; you pay per invocation and scale to zero. Strong for spiky, event-driven, or genuinely intermittent workloads — image processing on upload, scheduled jobs, webhooks, glue between managed services.

Limits to name: cold starts add latency to the first request after idle; execution time is capped (typically 15 minutes); connecting to a relational database is awkward because thousands of concurrent functions exhaust connection limits, so you need a proxy/pooler; local development and debugging are harder; and cost becomes worse than containers at sustained high traffic. It is excellent glue and a poor default for a high-throughput core API.

Interview questions · Section 15
Monolith or microservices for a new startup?

Monolith — specifically a well-modularised one with clear internal boundaries. A startup's constraints are speed of iteration and a tiny team, and microservices tax both: every cross-service change becomes a coordinated deploy, every call can fail partially, and you need tracing, discovery, per-service CI/CD and a service mesh before you've validated the product. You'd spend your first six months building a platform instead of a business.

What I would do is keep the monolith structured so extraction is cheap later: organise by business domain rather than technical layer, keep modules talking through explicit interfaces rather than reaching into each other's tables, and keep an eye on which module would be first to need independent scaling.

The triggers to extract are concrete: a module with radically different scaling needs (video transcoding versus the API), a team large enough that deploy coordination is genuinely painful — roughly beyond 15–20 engineers — or a genuine technology mismatch like needing Python for ML inside a Java codebase. Extract that one service; don't rewrite everything.

How do you decide service boundaries?

By business capability, not technical layer. Orders, Payments, Inventory, Identity, Notifications — each owning a coherent piece of the domain. A "database service" or "validation service" is an anti-pattern because it splits along the wrong axis and guarantees every feature touches every service.

My concrete tests:

  • Do they change together? If two modules are always modified and deployed in lockstep, they're one service.
  • Can it own its data exclusively? If a service can't own its tables — if others need to read them directly — the boundary is wrong. Shared databases recreate coupling invisibly and are the top microservices failure mode.
  • Does it need atomic transactions with its neighbour? If yes, keep them together rather than signing up for a saga.
  • Can one team own it end to end? Conway's Law means the architecture will mirror the org chart regardless, so design with that in mind.

And I'd size boundaries generously at first. Merging two services is easy; splitting a wrongly-drawn one after a year of coupling is not.

Each microservice has its own database — how do you query across services?

You can't join across them, and that constraint is deliberate — it's what keeps services independently deployable. The options, in order of preference:

  • API composition: the caller (usually an API gateway or BFF) fetches from each service and combines in memory. Fine for small result sets, poor for anything needing filtering or sorting across services.
  • CQRS read model: maintain a separate denormalised store built by consuming events from each service, purpose-shaped for the query. This is the standard answer for a dashboard or a search page that spans domains. Cost: eventual consistency.
  • Data replication: a service keeps a local read-only copy of the small slice it needs from another service, kept current via events — for example Orders caching a customer's name and tier. Avoids a synchronous call on every request.
  • A data warehouse fed by CDC, for analytics and reporting. Never run analytical queries against production service databases.

And I'd say plainly: if you constantly need to join two services' data, that's evidence the boundary is drawn in the wrong place.

What is a service mesh and do you need one?

A service mesh moves cross-cutting network concerns out of application code and into a sidecar proxy deployed alongside every service instance — Envoy, managed by Istio or Linkerd. The proxies handle mutual TLS between services, retries with backoff, timeouts, circuit breaking, load balancing, traffic splitting for canaries, and they emit consistent metrics and traces for every call.

The appeal is that you get all of that identically across services written in different languages, without asking every team to correctly implement retry logic in their own framework.

Do you need one? Not below roughly a dozen services — libraries and a good gateway are simpler and the mesh's operational complexity is real: an extra proxy per pod, more latency, a control plane to run, and a genuinely difficult debugging surface when it misbehaves. Adopt it when you have many services in multiple languages and mTLS or fine-grained traffic control has become a requirement.

What are the downsides of microservices?
  • Every function call becomes a network call — it can be slow, fail, or time out. You now need retries, timeouts, circuit breakers everywhere.
  • Availability multiplies down: five dependencies at 99.9% each gives 99.5% overall.
  • No distributed transactions — you're writing sagas and compensations, and living with eventual consistency in the UI.
  • Debugging is much harder — a stack trace becomes a distributed trace across services, requiring correlation ids and real tracing infrastructure.
  • Operational cost multiplies — N pipelines, N deployments, N on-call surfaces, service discovery, mesh, centralised logging.
  • Data duplication and consistency drift between services' local copies.
  • Versioning and contract management — a breaking API change must be rolled out compatibly across independently-deployed consumers.
  • Testing — integration testing now requires standing up many services or maintaining contract tests.

Which is why the right question is never "monolith or microservices" but "what specific problem am I buying this complexity to solve?"

Search, Analytics & Big Data

Inverted indexes, batch vs stream, and the probabilistic data structures that answer “too big to count” questions.

Full-text search & the inverted index

WHERE description LIKE '%wireless%' cannot use an index and scans the whole table — hopeless at scale, and it can’t rank, stem or handle typos. Search engines invert the problem.

A forward index maps document → words. An inverted index maps word → list of documents (a posting list, often with positions). A query for “wireless headphones” fetches both posting lists and intersects them — O(size of the shorter list) rather than O(number of documents).

The indexing pipeline

  1. Tokenise Split text into terms.
  2. Normalise Lowercase, strip punctuation and accents.
  3. Remove stop words “the”, “a”, “is” — huge posting lists, near-zero signal.
  4. Stem / lemmatise “running”, “ran”, “runs” → “run”, so a query matches all forms.
  5. Build posting lists term → sorted document ids, compressed by delta-encoding.
  6. Rank at query time TF-IDF or BM25 scores documents by how often the term appears in the document versus how rare it is across the corpus. Add signals like recency, popularity and personalisation.

Elasticsearch (built on Lucene) is the standard answer: it shards the index across nodes, replicates each shard, and scatter-gathers a query to all shards, merging the top-K results. Note it is near real-time — new documents become searchable after a refresh interval, typically a second — and it should not be your source of truth; keep the authoritative data in a database and feed the index via CDC or events.

Autocomplete / typeahead — a common follow-up

Use a trie (prefix tree) where each node stores the top-K completions for its prefix, precomputed. A lookup then walks the prefix and returns the cached list in O(length of prefix), with no ranking work at query time. Build it offline from query logs, rebuild periodically, cache aggressively at the edge, and debounce on the client so a five-letter word doesn’t fire five requests. For fuzzy matching, add edit-distance tolerance or n-gram indexing.

Batch vs stream processing

A mobile user goes into a tunnel and their events arrive twenty minutes late. If you bucket by processing time they land in the wrong window and your metrics are wrong. Interview questions · Section 16
How does full-text search work? Why not use SQL LIKE?

LIKE '%term%' can't use a B-tree index because the wildcard is at the start, so it scans every row — O(n) on a table that might have a hundred million rows. It also can't rank results by relevance, doesn't understand that "running" should match "run", and can't handle typos or synonyms.

A search engine builds an inverted index: instead of document → words, it stores word → the sorted list of documents containing it. A two-word query fetches both posting lists and intersects them, which costs the length of the shorter list rather than the size of the corpus. Building it involves tokenising, lowercasing, removing stop words, and stemming so word forms collapse together.

Ranking then uses BM25 or TF-IDF — a term that appears often in this document but rarely across the corpus is a strong signal — blended with business signals like recency and popularity.

In practice I'd use Elasticsearch, keep the database as the source of truth, and feed the index asynchronously via events or change-data-capture, accepting that search is near-real-time rather than instantly consistent.

What is a Bloom filter and where would you use one?

A Bloom filter is a compact bit array with k hash functions that answers set membership with a one-sided error: it can say "definitely not present" or "possibly present". To add an element, hash it k ways and set those bits. To query, hash and check — if any bit is 0 the element was definitely never added; if all are 1 it's probably present, but could be a false positive from other elements' bits overlapping. It stores no elements, so it's tiny: about 10 bits per element for a 1% false-positive rate.

Uses I'd cite: cache penetration — before querying the database for a key, check the filter; if it says no, skip the query entirely, which defends against attackers requesting nonexistent keys. In LSM-tree databases like Cassandra, each SSTable has a Bloom filter so a read skips files that definitely don't contain the key, avoiding disk I/O. In a web crawler, to check whether a URL has already been seen without keeping a billion URLs in memory.

Limitations: you can't delete (use a counting Bloom filter), you can't enumerate contents, and you must size it up front from the expected element count and tolerable error rate.

Design autocomplete for a search box.

Requirements: return the top 5–10 suggestions for a prefix in under ~100 ms, updated as the user types, ranked by popularity, at very high QPS — every keystroke is a request, so it's typically an order of magnitude more traffic than search itself.

The core structure is a trie. Each node represents a prefix, and crucially I'd precompute and store the top-K completions at every node. Then a lookup is just walking the prefix — O(length of prefix) — and returning a cached list, with no ranking at query time.

The data comes from query logs, aggregated offline: count query frequencies over a rolling window, filter out rare and inappropriate terms, and rebuild the trie periodically — hourly or daily is fine, since suggestions don't need to be real-time. Serve it from memory, sharded by first character or by hash of the prefix, with replicas for read scale.

Optimisations I'd mention: cache popular prefixes at the CDN, since a small number of prefixes dominate traffic; debounce on the client by ~50 ms so a five-letter word doesn't fire five requests; cancel in-flight requests when the user keeps typing; and for typo tolerance, either index n-grams or allow a small edit distance. For trending terms that must appear quickly, layer a real-time count-min sketch on top of the batch-built trie.

Batch vs stream processing — which for what?

Batch processes a bounded dataset on a schedule, with latency measured in minutes to hours. It's the right choice when the computation needs the complete dataset, when correctness matters more than freshness, and when reprocessing must be simple — nightly billing runs, ML model training, monthly reports, and any backfill after a bug. It's easier to test, easier to rerun, and cheaper per record.

Stream processes an unbounded flow with latency in milliseconds to seconds. Use it when the value of the answer decays fast — fraud detection during a transaction, live operational dashboards, alerting, real-time personalisation, trending topics.

The hard part of streaming that I'd raise: event time versus processing time. Events arrive late and out of order — a phone reconnecting after a tunnel — so windowing must be based on when the event happened, with watermarks deciding when a window can be closed and a policy for stragglers. Batch avoids this problem by construction.

Architecturally, Kappa — a single stream layer, with replay from the log when you need to recompute — is now preferred over Lambda's dual batch-and-speed layers, because maintaining the same logic in two codebases is a persistent source of bugs.

How would you count unique visitors to a site with a billion users?

Exactly counting distinct items requires storing every id you've seen — a billion 8-byte ids is 8 GB per counter, and you probably want it per day, per page and per region, so exact counting doesn't fit.

I'd use HyperLogLog. It hashes each id and tracks the maximum number of leading zeros seen across a set of buckets — a value that grows predictably with cardinality — and estimates the distinct count from that. It uses about 12 KB for roughly 2% error at any cardinality, which is a six-order-of-magnitude saving.

Two properties make it especially good here: it's mergeable, so each server keeps its own sketch and you union them to get a global count, and the union of daily sketches gives weekly and monthly uniques without recounting. Redis implements it directly with PFADD and PFCOUNT.

I'd flag the trade explicitly: 2% error is fine for analytics dashboards and capacity planning, and not fine for billing or compliance, where I'd fall back to exact counting on a much smaller filtered set.

Security & Authentication

Authentication vs authorisation, sessions vs tokens, OAuth in plain English, and the attacks you must name.

The distinction to open with

Authentication = who are you? (verifying identity — password, OTP, biometric). Authorisation = what are you allowed to do? (checking permissions). Authentication happens once per session; authorisation happens on every request. Also: authentication ≠ encryption ≠ hashing.

A JWT has three base64 parts: header (algorithm), payload (claims — user id, roles, exp), and signature. The signature proves the payload wasn’t tampered with; it does not encrypt it, so never put secrets in a JWT — anyone can decode it.

The revocation problem and its standard fix

Because a JWT is validated by signature alone, a stolen token works until it expires, and “log out everywhere” doesn’t work. The standard mitigation is a short-lived access token (5–15 minutes) plus a long-lived refresh token that is stored server-side and can be revoked. Access tokens stay stateless and fast; the blast radius of a theft is minutes; and revocation is possible at the refresh step. Add a deny-list for emergency revocation of specific tokens.

OAuth 2.0 & OpenID Connect in plain English

OAuth 2.0 is an authorisation delegation protocol: it lets an app access a resource on your behalf without you giving it your password. “Sign in with Google” is really OpenID Connect, a thin authentication layer on top of OAuth that adds an ID token describing who you are.

  1. Redirect The app sends you to Google with its client id and the scopes it wants.
  2. Consent You authenticate with Google and approve the requested scopes. The app never sees your password.
  3. Authorisation code Google redirects back to the app with a short-lived one-time code.
  4. Token exchange The app’s backend swaps that code — plus its client secret — for an access token, on a back channel the browser never sees.
  5. Access The app calls Google’s API with the access token, limited to the granted scopes, and can be revoked independently.

Use the authorisation code flow with PKCE for web and mobile; the implicit flow is deprecated because tokens in URLs leak through history and referrers.

Passwords, encryption, hashing

  • Never store plaintext passwords. Hash with a deliberately slow, memory-hard algorithm — bcrypt, scrypt or Argon2. Never MD5 or SHA-256 alone: they’re designed to be fast, so a GPU tries billions per second.
  • Salt — a unique random value per password, stored alongside it, so identical passwords hash differently and precomputed rainbow tables are useless.
  • Encryption is reversible; hashing is not. Encrypt data you need to read back (card numbers, PII); hash data you only need to verify (passwords).
  • In transit: TLS everywhere, including between internal services (mTLS in a mesh). At rest: disk/database encryption plus field-level encryption for the most sensitive columns.
  • Secrets management: never in source control or environment files in the repo — use a vault (HashiCorp Vault, AWS Secrets Manager) with rotation.

Interview questions · Section 17
How would you design authentication for a web application?

On registration, I'd hash the password with bcrypt or Argon2 and a per-user salt — deliberately slow and memory-hard, so offline cracking is expensive. Never MD5 or plain SHA-256, which are fast by design.

On login, verify the hash, then issue a short-lived access token (15 minutes) and a long-lived refresh token (days to weeks) stored server-side so it can be revoked. The access token is a signed JWT carrying the user id and roles, so every service can validate it locally without a database lookup; the refresh token is what makes logout and revocation actually work.

Transport and storage: TLS everywhere, tokens in HttpOnly, Secure, SameSite cookies so JavaScript can't read them and CSRF is mitigated — storing tokens in localStorage exposes them to any XSS. Rate limit login attempts and add exponential lockout to blunt credential stuffing, offer MFA, and check submitted passwords against known-breached lists.

And I'd say what I'd not build: for most products, delegating to an identity provider via OpenID Connect is safer than rolling your own.

Session-based auth vs JWT — which and why?

Sessions keep state on the server and give the client an opaque id, so every request costs a lookup — but revocation is instant, the cookie is tiny, and nothing sensitive lives on the client. JWTs carry the claims themselves and are validated by signature, so any service can authorise without a lookup, which is genuinely valuable in a microservices or mobile setting.

The decisive weakness of JWT is revocation: since validation is signature-only, a stolen or stale token remains valid until it expires. You can't log a user out, and you can't immediately strip a permission.

So my answer is the hybrid that most production systems use: short-lived JWT access tokens for the stateless fast path, plus a server-stored refresh token for revocation. That gives cheap validation with a blast radius of minutes. For a traditional single-server web app where immediate logout matters and there's no microservice fan-out, plain server-side sessions in Redis are simpler and I'd just use those.

Explain OAuth 2.0.

OAuth 2.0 is a protocol for delegated authorisation — letting one application access your data on another service without you handing over your password. The classic case is an app wanting to read your Google Calendar.

The authorisation code flow: the app redirects you to Google with its client id and the scopes it wants. You authenticate with Google directly — the app never sees your credentials — and approve the scopes. Google redirects back with a short-lived, single-use authorisation code. The app's backend then exchanges that code plus its client secret for an access token over a back channel the browser never sees, which is why the code step exists at all: it keeps the token out of the URL and browser history. The app then calls Google's API with the token, restricted to the granted scopes and revocable independently of your password.

Two clarifications worth making: OAuth is authorisation, not authentication — "Sign in with Google" is actually OpenID Connect, a layer on top that adds an ID token describing who you are. And for browser and mobile clients that can't hold a secret, use the authorisation code flow with PKCE; the implicit flow is deprecated because it exposes tokens in URLs.

How do you prevent SQL injection and XSS?

SQL injection happens when user input is concatenated into a query so it changes the query's structure. The fix is parameterised queries — the driver sends the query template and the values separately, so input is only ever data and can never become syntax. Escaping by hand is not a substitute; it fails on edge cases. Layer on least-privilege database accounts so even a successful injection can't drop tables, plus input validation and an ORM that parameterises by default.

XSS happens when attacker-supplied content is rendered as executable script in another user's browser. The fix is context-aware output encoding — escaping differs for HTML body, attributes, JavaScript and URLs — which modern frameworks do automatically unless you use an escape hatch like dangerouslySetInnerHTML. Add a Content-Security-Policy to block inline and third-party scripts, sanitise any rich text with a proven library rather than a regex, and set HttpOnly on session cookies so a successful XSS still can't steal the session.

The shared principle: never trust input, and never let data cross into a code context.

How do you handle secrets like API keys and database passwords?

Never in source control — including config files, notebooks and commit history, since a secret committed once is compromised even after deletion and must be rotated. Never baked into container images either.

Use a dedicated secrets manager — HashiCorp Vault, AWS Secrets Manager, or the cloud equivalent — that stores secrets encrypted, controls access by identity, audits every read, and supports rotation, ideally issuing short-lived dynamic credentials rather than static ones. Applications fetch secrets at startup or on demand using their workload identity, so there's no bootstrap secret to protect.

Supporting practices: least privilege so each service can read only its own secrets, encryption in transit and at rest, automated scanning in CI to block accidental commits, and a documented rotation procedure — because the real test of secret management is how fast you can rotate everything after a suspected leak.

How would you protect an API from abuse?
  • Authenticate everything — API keys or OAuth tokens, so requests are attributable.
  • Rate limit per key, per user and per IP, with different budgets per endpoint since an expensive search shouldn't share a quota with a health check (Section 14).
  • Quotas on top of rate limits — daily or monthly caps tied to the plan.
  • Validate input strictly — schema validation, maximum payload size, maximum page size, and a cap on query depth or complexity for GraphQL, otherwise one query can scan your whole database.
  • WAF and DDoS protection at the edge — a CDN absorbs volumetric attacks across its network before they reach you.
  • Authorise per resource, not just authenticate, to prevent IDOR — check that this user owns that order.
  • Timeouts and pagination limits so no single request can consume unbounded resources.
  • Monitor and alert on anomalous patterns, and return 429 with Retry-After so honest clients back off correctly.

How they work together in a real incident

An alert fires on a metric (p99 latency crossed the SLO). You open a dashboard of metrics to see which service and which endpoint. You pull a trace for a slow request to find which span consumed the time. You read the logs for that trace id to see the exact error. Metrics tell you that something is wrong; traces tell you where; logs tell you why. Say this and you have answered the whole topic.

Distributed tracing — the mechanism

A request entering the system is assigned a trace id. Every service passes it along in headers and creates a span — a timed operation with a parent span id. The collector reassembles the spans into a tree showing exactly how time was spent and where an error originated. Without this, debugging a microservices latency problem means correlating timestamps across a dozen log files by hand. OpenTelemetry is the vendor-neutral standard for instrumenting it.

Because tracing every request is expensive, systems sample — either head-based (decide at the edge, e.g. keep 1%) or tail-based (buffer, then keep every trace that was slow or errored, which is far more useful and more expensive).

What to monitor

The Four Golden Signals (Google SRE)

Latency (and separate successful from failed requests — fast errors flatter your average) · Traffic (QPS) · Errors (rate and type) · Saturation (how full the most constrained resource is).

RED & USE

RED for services: Rate, Errors, Duration. USE for resources: Utilisation, Saturation, Errors. Use RED for anything request-driven, USE for CPU, memory, disk and queues.

Alert on symptoms, not causes

Do not alert on “CPU is 90%” — that may be perfectly fine. Alert on “error rate exceeded the SLO” or “p99 latency is above target” or “queue lag is growing” — things a user would notice. Cause metrics belong on dashboards for diagnosis, not in a pager. Every alert must be actionable, must say what to do, and must be rare. Alert fatigue is a genuine production risk: an on-call engineer who receives forty pages a night stops reading them, and the one real page is lost in the noise.

Practices worth naming

  • Structured logging — emit JSON with consistent fields (trace_id, user_id, service, level) so logs are queryable rather than grep-able. Always include the trace id.
  • Correlation ids — generated at the edge and propagated through every hop, including into queue messages, so async work stays linked to its originating request.
  • Never log secrets or PII — passwords, tokens, card numbers. Redact at the logging library, not by remembering.
  • Cardinality discipline — putting a user id in a metric label creates millions of time series and will take down your metrics system. High-cardinality data belongs in logs and traces.
  • Runbooks — every alert links to a document saying what it means and what to check first.
  • Blameless postmortems — after an incident, document the timeline, the contributing causes and the action items, without attributing fault. People hide mistakes in blameful cultures, and hidden mistakes recur.
  • Synthetic monitoring — scripted probes hitting critical journeys from outside, so you learn about an outage before your users tweet about it.

Metrics for health and trends — the four golden signals per service: latency (split by success and failure, as percentiles not averages), traffic, error rate, and saturation of the most constrained resource. Logs, metrics and traces — what's the difference?

Metrics are numeric time series — request rate, error rate, p99 latency, queue depth. They're cheap to store, easy to aggregate and chart, and ideal for alerting and trends. What they can't tell you is why any individual request failed.

Logs are discrete events with detail — this request, this user, this exception, this stack trace. Maximum detail, but high volume and expensive to store and search, and useless for spotting trends by eye.

Traces show one request's path across services as a tree of timed spans. They're the only thing that answers "which of these twelve services consumed the 900 ms?" and they're usually sampled because tracing everything is costly.

The workflow ties them together: metrics tell you that something is wrong and page you, traces tell you where, logs tell you why. A system with only logs can't alert well; one with only metrics can't debug.

What should you alert on?

Symptoms users feel, tied to your SLOs — not internal causes. Good alerts: error rate above the SLO threshold, p99 latency above target, a critical user journey failing in a synthetic probe, consumer lag growing without bound, error budget burning fast, or a disk that will fill within the hour.

Bad alerts: "CPU above 80%" — that might be efficient use of hardware; "a pod restarted" — orchestrators do that routinely; anything that fires regularly and is routinely ignored.

The rules I'd state: every alert must be actionable — if there's nothing to do, it's a dashboard, not a page. Every alert links to a runbook. Page only for things needing attention now; everything else is a ticket. And treat noisy alerts as bugs, because alert fatigue is a real failure mode — an engineer paged forty times a night will miss the one that matters.

Multi-window burn-rate alerting on the error budget is the modern refinement: page fast when the budget is burning quickly, ticket when it's a slow leak.

How do you debug a request that's slow in production across many services?

Start with the trace. With a trace id propagated through every hop, I can open a slow request and see the span tree — which service, which database call, which external API consumed the time, and whether calls that could be parallel are running sequentially.

Common findings and what they mean: one span dominating points at a slow query or a missing index; many small repeated spans is an N+1 pattern; a long gap with no child spans is usually queueing, lock contention or garbage collection; a slow span in a dependency you don't own means adding a timeout and a fallback.

Then I'd cross-check with metrics — is this one request or the whole endpoint? p50 versus p99 tells me whether it's systemic or tail — and read the logs filtered by that trace id for errors and retries. If tracing isn't in place, correlation ids in structured logs are the fallback; and if neither exists, fixing that is the first task.

The hardest part: database migrations

Code rolls back in seconds; a dropped column does not. During any rolling or blue-green deploy, old and new code run at the same time against the same schema, so every migration must be backwards compatible. The standard technique is expand–contract:

  1. Expand Add the new column/table. Do not remove or rename anything. Make it nullable or give it a default.
  2. Backfill Populate it in batches so you don’t lock a large table.
  3. Dual write Deploy code that writes both old and new, and still reads old.
  4. Switch reads Deploy code that reads the new column. Verify.
  5. Contract Once no running code touches the old column — days later — drop it.

Never rename a column in one step; that is “add new, dual write, migrate, drop old”. Adding an index on a large table should be done concurrently/online so it doesn’t lock writes.

CI/CD, containers and infrastructure

  • Continuous integration — every push builds and runs the test suite, so integration problems surface in minutes rather than at a merge weeks later.
  • Continuous delivery/deployment — every passing build is automatically deployable (delivery) or actually deployed (deployment). The goal is small, frequent, boring releases: small changes are easier to review, easier to diagnose and much easier to roll back.
  • Containers — package the app with its dependencies so it runs identically everywhere, eliminating “works on my machine”. Far lighter than VMs since they share the host kernel.
  • Kubernetes — declare desired state (“5 replicas of this image”) and the control loop maintains it: scheduling, restarts on failure, rolling updates, service discovery, autoscaling. Worth knowing the vocabulary: pod, deployment, service, ingress, ConfigMap/Secret, HPA, readiness vs liveness probes.
  • Infrastructure as Code — Terraform/CloudFormation define infrastructure in version-controlled files, so environments are reproducible, reviewable and rebuildable after a disaster.
  • Immutable infrastructure — never patch a running server; build a new image and replace it. Eliminates configuration drift.
Disaster recovery — two numbers to know

RPO (Recovery Point Objective) — how much data you can afford to lose, which sets your backup/replication frequency. RTO (Recovery Time Objective) — how long you can afford to be down, which sets your failover architecture. RPO of 0 requires synchronous replication; RTO of minutes requires a warm standby, not a backup restore. And the rule that matters most: a backup you have never restored is not a backup — test restores on a schedule.

Interview questions · Section 19
Blue-green vs canary deployment?

Blue-green runs two complete identical environments. All traffic is on blue; you deploy to green, verify it, then switch everything at once at the load balancer. The huge advantage is instant rollback — flip back to blue. The costs are double the infrastructure and the fact that the switch is all-or-nothing, so a bug that only appears under real traffic hits 100% of users immediately.

Canary routes a small percentage — 1%, then 5%, 25%, 100% — to the new version, watching error rate and latency at each step and rolling back automatically on regression. The blast radius is tiny and you're testing against genuine production traffic and data. The costs are that it's slower, two versions run concurrently so the code and schema must be compatible, and it needs solid metrics and automation to be safe.

I'd prefer canary for a high-traffic user-facing service, because limiting exposure matters more than rollback speed. I'd use blue-green when a change can't be partially rolled out — a major version cutover — and I'd add feature flags to both, so I can disable a feature instantly without any deploy at all.

How do you deploy a database schema change with zero downtime?

The constraint is that during a rolling deploy, old and new code run simultaneously against one schema, so every migration must be backwards compatible with the version still running. The pattern is expand–contract, deployed over several releases:

  1. Expand: add the new column as nullable or with a default. Never rename or drop in this step. Old code ignores it and keeps working.
  2. Backfill existing rows in batches, so you never hold a long lock on a large table.
  3. Dual write: deploy code that writes both old and new columns while still reading the old one.
  4. Switch reads to the new column and verify with real traffic.
  5. Contract: after the old code is fully gone — days later, once you're confident there's no rollback — drop the old column.

Supporting details: build indexes concurrently/online so writes aren't blocked; keep each migration reversible; test on a production-sized copy because a migration that takes two seconds on dev can take an hour on a hundred million rows; and separate the migration deploy from the code deploy so each can be rolled back independently.

What are feature flags and why are they useful?

A feature flag is a runtime switch that enables or disables code without deploying. The core benefit is that it decouples deployment from release — code can ship to production dark, then be turned on for internal users, then 1% of customers, then everyone, all without touching the deployment pipeline.

What that buys: an instant kill switch when something goes wrong, in seconds rather than a rollback deploy; the ability to run A/B tests and gradual rollouts by user segment; safe trunk-based development, since incomplete features can be merged behind a disabled flag rather than living on a long branch; and per-customer enablement for beta programmes.

The cost is flag debt. Every flag is a branch in the code, and N flags mean 2ᴺ possible combinations, most of which are never tested. So flags need owners, expiry dates, and disciplined removal once a feature is fully rolled out. They should also be short-lived by default — a permanent flag is really a configuration setting and should be modelled as one.

What are RPO and RTO?

RPO — Recovery Point Objective — is the maximum acceptable data loss, expressed in time. An RPO of one hour means that after a disaster you can afford to lose up to an hour of writes, so hourly backups suffice. An RPO of zero means no data loss is acceptable, which requires synchronous replication and therefore adds latency to every write.

RTO — Recovery Time Objective — is the maximum acceptable downtime. An RTO of 24 hours allows restoring from backups; an RTO of five minutes requires a warm standby already running with replicated data and automated failover; an RTO near zero requires active-active across regions.

They're business decisions, not technical ones, because the cost rises steeply as both approach zero — so you ask what an hour of downtime or an hour of lost data actually costs, and buy accordingly. Different systems in one company legitimately get different targets: the payment ledger and the recommendation cache should not have the same RPO.

And the operational point I'd add: a backup that has never been restored is not a backup. Restore drills belong on a schedule.

Case 1 · URL Shortener (TinyURL / bit.ly)

Why it’s asked: the simplest problem that still touches hashing, storage, caching, redirects and collisions. Almost always the warm-up.

1 · Requirements

Functional: shorten a long URL to a short code; redirect a short code to the original; optional custom alias; optional expiry; basic click analytics.
Non-functional: redirects must be very low latency (<100 ms p99); extremely high availability — a dead shortener breaks every link ever shared; codes must be unique and non-guessable; read:write ≈ 100:1.

2 · Estimation & API

100 M new URLs/day → write QPS = 100 M ÷ 10⁵ = 1 000/s (peak ~3 000/s)
Reads at 100:1 → 100 000/s (peak ~300 000/s) → read-heavy, cache-first design
Storage: 500 B/record × 100 M/day × 365 × 5 ≈ 91 TB over 5 years
Code space: base62 (a–z A–Z 0–9). 62⁷ ≈ 3.5 trillion — 7 characters is plenty.

POST /api/v1/urls          {longUrl, customAlias?, expiresAt?} → {shortUrl}
GET  /{shortCode}          → 301/302 redirect
GET  /api/v1/urls/{code}/stats → {clicks, createdAt, ...}

3 · Generating the short code — the actual question

ApproachHowVerdict
Hash + truncateMD5/SHA of the URL, take first 7 charsDeterministic (same URL → same code, saves space), but collisions need a check-and-retry loop, and each write costs a read
Random + checkGenerate 7 random base62 chars, retry on conflictSimple; collision probability is negligible until the space fills, but still needs a uniqueness check
Counter → base62Take a globally unique increasing integer and encode it in base62Best. Zero collisions by construction, no read before write, shortest codes. Codes are guessable/sequential, so add an offset or bit-scramble if that matters

For the counter, use a Snowflake-style generator or a ticket server handing out ranges — each app instance claims a block of 10 000 ids and hands them out locally, so the central service is hit once per 10 000 URLs rather than once per URL.

4 · High-level design & deep dive

  • Database: the access pattern is pure key→value with no joins, so a distributed KV store (DynamoDB/Cassandra) or a sharded relational table keyed on the code. Shard by hash of the short code.
  • Cache: Redis with LRU in front. Links follow a strong power law — a tiny fraction of codes serve most redirects — so expect a hit rate above 90%, which is what makes 300 000 QPS affordable.
  • 301 vs 302: 301 (permanent) is cached by the browser, so subsequent clicks never reach you — great for load, terrible for analytics. 302 (temporary) means every click hits your server, so you can count it. Use 302 if analytics matter, 301 if load matters. This exact question is asked constantly.
  • Analytics: never write a click row synchronously — publish to Kafka and aggregate downstream.
  • Expiry: a TTL on the row plus a background cleanup job; expired codes can be recycled.

Case 2 · Twitter / News Feed

Why it’s asked: it is the canonical fan-out problem, and the celebrity edge case tests whether you can find your own bottleneck.

1 · Requirements & 2 · Estimation

Functional: post a tweet; follow users; view a home timeline of followed users’ tweets in reverse-chronological order.
Non-functional: timeline load <200 ms; eventual consistency acceptable (a few seconds’ delay is fine); very read-heavy; must survive celebrity accounts.

300 M DAU · 150 M tweets/day → 1 500 write QPS (peak 4 500)
6 B timeline views/day → 60 000 read QPS (peak 180 000) → read:write ≈ 40:1

3 · The central decision: fan-out on write vs on read

 Fan-out on write (push)Fan-out on read (pull)
On postingWrite the tweet id into every follower’s precomputed timeline listJust store the tweet
On readingRead one precomputed list — very fastQuery all N followees, merge and sort — slow
Write costO(followers) — a user with 50 M followers means 50 M writesO(1)
StorageHigh — the timeline is duplicated per followerLow
Good forNormal users; read-heavy workloadsCelebrities; inactive users
The answer: a hybrid

Use fan-out on write for normal users — the vast majority — so the common read is a single list lookup. For accounts above a follower threshold (say 100 000), do not fan out. Instead, at read time, take the user’s precomputed timeline and merge in the recent tweets of the few celebrities they follow. This bounds both the worst-case write (a celebrity post is O(1)) and the read (a small merge). Also skip fan-out to accounts inactive for months, and backfill their timeline lazily on next login.

4 · Deep dive

  • Timeline storage: Redis lists or sorted sets, capped at ~800 entries per user — nobody scrolls further, and unbounded lists are a memory disaster. Store tweet ids only, then hydrate the content from a cache in one batch call.
  • Fan-out execution: posting publishes to Kafka; a fleet of fan-out workers writes to follower timelines asynchronously, so the POST returns in milliseconds.
  • Data model: tweets sharded by tweet id (Snowflake ids give rough time-ordering for free); follows stored twice — by follower and by followee — so both “who do I follow” and “who follows me” are single-shard reads. That is deliberate denormalisation.
  • Media goes to object storage + CDN, never the database.
  • Ranked timelines: if the timeline is algorithmic rather than chronological, add a candidate-generation step then a ranking model — but say clearly that you are scoping to chronological unless asked.

Case 3 · WhatsApp / Chat System

Why it’s asked: it forces you to reason about long-lived connections, delivery guarantees and ordering.

Functional: 1:1 and group messaging; delivery and read receipts; online/last-seen presence; offline delivery when the recipient reconnects; media attachments.
Non-functional: low latency (<500 ms end to end); no message ever lost; messages ordered within a conversation; ~50 M concurrent connections.

Architecture

  • Transport: WebSocket for connected clients (full-duplex, low overhead), falling back to long polling. When the app is closed, push via APNs/FCM.
  • Connection tier: a separate, stateful fleet of “chat servers” holding the WebSocket connections, sized by memory and file descriptors rather than CPU. Keep them thin — they only move bytes.
  • Presence/registry: Redis maps user_id → chat_server_id with a heartbeat TTL. To send a message, look up the recipient’s server and forward to it.
  • Message flow: sender → their chat server → persist to the message store first (this is what guarantees no loss) → look up recipient’s server → push over its WebSocket → recipient acks → update status to delivered. If the recipient is offline, the message simply waits in storage and a push notification is sent.
  • Storage: Cassandra, partitioned by conversation_id and clustered by message id descending. That gives per-conversation ordering, cheap “fetch the last 50 messages” reads, and spreads writes evenly. Chat is extremely write-heavy, which is exactly what an LSM-tree store is built for.
  • Ordering: assign each message a monotonically increasing sequence number per conversation on the server. Client timestamps cannot be trusted — phone clocks are wrong.
  • Groups: for small groups, fan out to each member. For very large groups, store once and let members pull, exactly as with celebrities in Case 2.
  • Media: upload directly to object storage via a pre-signed URL; the message carries only the key.
  • Reconnection: the client stores the last message id it has and asks “what happened after N?” on reconnect — never assume the socket was reliable.
The follow-up you must be ready for

“How do you guarantee exactly-once delivery?” Answer: you don’t — you use at-least-once plus a client-generated message id, so a duplicate delivery is deduplicated on the client. And mention end-to-end encryption: with E2EE the server stores ciphertext only, which means server-side search and multi-device sync become genuinely hard problems.

Case 4 · YouTube / Video Streaming

Why it’s asked: the numbers are so large that they force correct instincts — object storage, CDN, async pipelines.

Functional: upload video; transcode to multiple resolutions; stream with adaptive quality; search; view counts.
Non-functional: playback starts in <2 s; must work on poor mobile connections; petabyte-scale storage; extremely read-heavy.

Upload & processing pipeline

  1. Direct upload Client gets a pre-signed URL and uploads straight to object storage — bytes never touch your app servers. Use chunked/resumable upload so a dropped mobile connection doesn’t restart a 2 GB file.
  2. Publish an event “VideoUploaded” goes onto a queue. The API returns immediately; the video shows as “processing”.
  3. Transcode in parallel Workers split the video into segments and transcode each into multiple resolutions and codecs (240p → 4K). Segment-level parallelism is what makes a 2-hour film process in minutes.
  4. Package for adaptive streaming Produce HLS/DASH: small segments (2–10 s) plus a manifest listing available bitrates. The player then measures bandwidth and switches quality per segment — this is why video doesn’t stall on a train.
  5. Distribute Push popular content to CDN edges; the rest is pulled on first request. This is the only viable way to serve tens of Tbps.
  6. Index Extract thumbnails, run moderation, update the search index — all asynchronous.
Why the CDN isn’t optional

10 M concurrent 1080p viewers × 5 Mbps = 50 Tbps. A 10 Gbps NIC is 0.01 Tbps, so that’s 5 000 saturated servers of pure network. A CDN typically offloads 95%+ of egress; without it the design is arithmetically impossible.

  • Metadata in a relational or wide-column store; view counts are write-hot, so buffer them in Redis and flush aggregates periodically rather than incrementing a row per view.
  • Storage tiering: most videos are watched almost never after their first week, so move cold content to cheaper archival storage.

Case 5 · Uber / Ride-Hailing (proximity search)

Why it’s asked: geospatial indexing plus a high-frequency write path plus a matching problem.

Functional: drivers publish location continuously; riders request a ride; match rider to a nearby driver; live trip tracking; fare and payment.
Non-functional: matching in a few seconds; location updates every ~4 s from millions of drivers; high write throughput; regionally partitioned.

The core problem: “find drivers near me”

A naïve WHERE distance(lat,lng) < 5km cannot use an index and scans every driver. The fix is to reduce 2D space to a 1D sortable key:

TechniqueIdeaNotes
GeohashRecursively split the world into a grid; encode the cell as a string. Shared prefix = nearby.Simple, indexable as a string prefix. Edge case: two points either side of a cell boundary have different prefixes, so you must also search the 8 neighbouring cells.
QuadtreeRecursively subdivide only dense regionsAdapts to population density — a city subdivides deeply, an ocean doesn’t
S2 / H3Google’s and Uber’s production libraries (H3 uses hexagons)Hexagons have uniform neighbour distance, which is better for coverage and matching
  • Location store: keep the live positions in Redis (which has native geospatial commands) rather than a durable database. Driver locations are ephemeral, updated every few seconds, and losing them briefly is survivable — writing millions of updates per second to a durable store is not.
  • Write path: millions of drivers × one update per 4 s ≈ hundreds of thousands of writes/second. Partition by region so each city is an independent workload — this system geo-shards naturally.
  • Matching: query the rider’s cell plus neighbours for candidate drivers, filter by availability and vehicle type, rank by ETA (from a routing service — road distance, not straight-line), then offer sequentially with a short timeout and a lock on the driver so two riders can’t be matched to one car. That last part is a distributed-concurrency problem, and mentioning it scores well.
  • Trip tracking: WebSocket or push for live position; write the trip’s durable record to a transactional store since it involves money.
  • Surge pricing: computed per cell from a stream job comparing demand and supply over a rolling window.

Case 6 · Web Crawler

Why it’s asked: pure distributed-systems reasoning — politeness, deduplication, and a frontier that must not explode.

Functional: start from seed URLs, fetch pages, extract links, repeat; store content for indexing; refresh pages periodically.
Non-functional: billions of pages; must be polite (never hammer one site); robust against traps; extensible.

  1. URL frontier The queue of URLs to crawl. It’s not one queue — it has two levels: a priority layer (important or frequently-changing pages first) and a politeness layer where each worker maps to a single host, so requests to any one domain are serialised with a delay. This is the heart of the design.
  2. Fetcher Respect robots.txt (cached per domain), set a real user-agent, apply timeouts, and cap page size.
  3. Duplicate detection Use a Bloom filter for “have I seen this URL?” — billions of URLs won’t fit in memory otherwise. For duplicate content (the same article on many sites), use SimHash to detect near-duplicates, not an exact hash.
  4. Parser & link extractor Extract links, normalise them (resolve relative URLs, strip fragments and tracking parameters, lowercase the host) and push new ones back to the frontier.
  5. Storage Raw pages to object storage; metadata and the link graph to a distributed store.
  6. Recrawl scheduling A news homepage changes hourly; an archived page doesn’t. Estimate change frequency and schedule accordingly rather than crawling everything uniformly.
Traps to name — this is what separates good answers

Spider traps: infinitely generated URLs like a calendar with a “next month” link forever — cap crawl depth and URLs per domain. Redirect loops. Huge or malformed files. Dynamic URLs that produce infinite variants from query parameters. And politeness is not optional: an impolite crawler is indistinguishable from a DDoS and will get you blocked.

Case 7 · Notification System

Why it’s asked: it is a pure fan-out-and-reliability problem with lots of third-party failure.

Functional: send push, SMS, email and in-app notifications; support templates and localisation; respect user preferences and quiet hours; deduplicate; support scheduling.
Non-functional: millions per minute; at-least-once delivery; no duplicates visible to the user; third-party providers will fail.

  • Ingestion: services call a Notification API (or emit events). Validate, resolve the user’s devices and preferences, render the template — then enqueue. Never send synchronously.
  • Per-channel queues: separate queues for push, SMS and email, because their throughput, cost and failure modes differ wildly. This is a bulkhead — an SMS provider outage must not block push.
  • Workers call the providers (APNs/FCM, an SMS gateway, an email provider) with retries, exponential backoff and jitter, circuit breakers, and a DLQ after the retry budget.
  • Deduplication: every notification carries an idempotency key, checked against a store before sending — because at-least-once delivery plus retries means duplicates will occur.
  • Preferences & rate limiting per user: honour opt-outs, quiet hours and per-user frequency caps. Notification fatigue causes uninstalls, so this is a product requirement, not a nicety.
  • Priority: a separate high-priority path so an OTP is never stuck behind a marketing blast.
  • Tracking: record sent/delivered/opened/failed events for analytics, and use provider webhooks to update status asynchronously.
  • Provider failover: integrate at least two SMS/email vendors and route around one that starts failing.

Case 8 · Ticket Booking (BookMyShow / IRCTC)

Why it’s asked: it is the concurrency problem. Overselling is unacceptable, and the load is extremely spiky.

Functional: browse events and shows; view a seat map; hold seats while paying; confirm booking on payment; cancel and refund.
Non-functional: never double-book a seat; handle enormous spikes when a popular show opens; payment must be consistent; browse traffic is read-heavy while booking is contention-heavy.

The core problem: two people, one seat

Reservation model — the answer

Don’t hold a database transaction open across the payment step; payments take minutes and involve a third party. Instead use a two-phase reservation:

  1. Hold: atomically mark the seats LOCKED for this user with a short expiry (typically 5–10 minutes). Atomicity comes from a conditional update — UPDATE seats SET status='LOCKED' WHERE seat_id IN (…) AND status='AVAILABLE' — and if the affected row count doesn’t match, someone else won and you tell the user.
  2. Confirm: on successful payment, transition LOCKED → BOOKED in a transaction.
  3. Expire: a background job (or a Redis key TTL) releases abandoned holds back to AVAILABLE.
  • Consistency choice: this is firmly CP. Refusing a booking during a partition is acceptable; selling the same seat twice is not.
  • Locking strategy: optimistic (version column) for normal shows where contention is low; for a blockbuster on-sale, the hot rows make optimistic locking thrash, so use a queue — admit users to the booking page in controlled batches (a virtual waiting room), which converts a stampede into a manageable stream.
  • Spiky load: pre-scale before a known on-sale; cache the seat map layout aggressively (it never changes) while keeping seat status uncached or very short-TTL; and put the whole browse path behind a CDN.
  • Payment: a saga — hold seats → charge → confirm; if the charge fails or times out, compensate by releasing the hold. Use an idempotency key on the payment call (Section 12) so a retry can’t double-charge.
  • Sharding: shard by show_id, so all seats for one show live on one shard and the hold can be a single-shard transaction. This is the shard key that makes the whole design work.
Cross-cutting case-study questions
In the URL shortener, would you return 301 or 302?

It depends on whether analytics or load matters more, and I'd say that explicitly. 301 Permanent is cached by the browser, so after the first click the user's browser redirects locally and never touches my servers again — enormous load reduction, but I lose all click tracking after the first visit, and I can never change where that code points because the cached redirect persists.

302 Found is not cached, so every click reaches my server. That means accurate analytics and the ability to update or expire a link, at the cost of serving every single redirect.

For a product like bit.ly where click analytics is the business, 302 is correct, and the load is manageable because a Redis cache serves those lookups in under a millisecond. If I were purely optimising for cost and the links were immutable, 301 would be right.

Twitter: a celebrity with 100 million followers posts. What happens?

With naive fan-out-on-write, that single post becomes 100 million writes into 100 million timeline lists. Even at high throughput that takes minutes, saturates the fan-out workers, delays everyone else's tweets behind it, and burns enormous storage — for a tweet most followers will never scroll to.

The fix is a hybrid fan-out. Accounts above a follower threshold are excluded from fan-out entirely; their posts are simply stored. When any user loads their timeline, the system reads their precomputed list (which covers everyone they follow who isn't a celebrity) and merges in the recent posts of the handful of celebrities they follow, sorted by time. Since a user follows very few such accounts, that merge is cheap.

Two refinements: cache each celebrity's recent posts aggressively, since millions of timeline reads will want the same few tweets; and skip fan-out to accounts that have been inactive for months, backfilling their timeline lazily when they next log in.

Chat: how do you handle a user who is offline?

The message is persisted to the message store before any delivery attempt, so being offline changes nothing about durability — the message is safe the moment it's accepted. The presence registry shows no active connection, so instead of pushing over a WebSocket, the system sends a push notification via APNs or FCM to wake the app.

When the user reconnects, the client doesn't rely on the server having remembered anything. It sends the last message id it holds per conversation and asks for everything after that — a sync-by-cursor. The server returns the missed messages in order, and the client updates delivery receipts.

Details worth adding: cap how much history is synced on reconnect and paginate the rest; use the per-conversation sequence number rather than timestamps for the cursor, since client clocks are unreliable; and deduplicate on the client using the sender-generated message id, because at-least-once delivery means the same message can arrive twice.

Uber: why not just store driver locations in PostgreSQL?

Two reasons — write volume and query shape.

Volume: a million active drivers updating every four seconds is 250 000 writes per second, continuously. Every one of those writes goes through a write-ahead log, updates indexes and generates replication traffic — for data that is obsolete four seconds later. It's the worst possible workload for a durable transactional database.

Query shape: "find drivers within 5 km" isn't a range scan on an indexed column. A naive latitude/longitude filter can't use a B-tree usefully and degenerates toward a scan. You need a spatial index — PostGIS can do this, but combining it with that write rate makes it worse.

So I'd keep live locations in Redis, which has native geospatial commands, is in-memory, and treats the data as what it is: ephemeral. Losing a few seconds of driver positions after a restart is completely acceptable — drivers re-report almost immediately. The durable database keeps trips, users and payments, where transactions genuinely matter. Matching the storage guarantee to the data's actual value is the point.

Ticket booking: how do you guarantee a seat isn't sold twice?

The mechanism is an atomic conditional update, not application-level checking. A read-then-write — "check if available, then mark booked" — has a race window between the two steps, and under load two users will both pass the check.

Instead: UPDATE seats SET status='LOCKED', held_by=?, expires_at=? WHERE seat_id IN (…) AND status='AVAILABLE'. The database evaluates the condition and applies the change as one atomic operation. If the number of rows affected is less than the number of seats requested, someone else got there first — roll back and tell the user. There is no window.

Then the seat is held, not sold, with a short expiry, so the user can complete payment without me holding a database transaction open across a third-party call. On payment success, a transaction moves LOCKED → BOOKED; on failure or timeout, a background job releases the hold.

Supporting decisions: shard by show id so all seats for one show live on one shard and the update stays a single-shard transaction; choose CP over AP, because refusing a booking during a partition is far cheaper than selling one seat twice; and for a blockbuster on-sale, put a virtual waiting room in front so the hot rows see a controlled stream rather than a stampede.

How to answer any LLD question — five steps

  1. Clarify requirements and scope Same discipline as HLD. “Should the parking lot support multiple floors? Multiple vehicle types? Is payment in scope?”
  2. Identify the entities (nouns) ParkingLot, Floor, ParkingSpot, Vehicle, Ticket, Payment, DisplayBoard. Nouns in the problem statement are usually your classes.
  3. Identify behaviours (verbs) and assign them park(), unpark(), calculateFee(), findSpot(). Ask “whose responsibility is this?” — that question is what produces a clean design instead of a God class.
  4. Define relationships and interfaces Inheritance for “is-a” (Car is a Vehicle), composition for “has-a” (a Floor has Spots). Prefer composition over inheritance. Program to interfaces.
  5. Apply patterns where they earn their place, and state extensibility “Fee calculation uses Strategy so adding weekend pricing is a new class, not an edit.” Then say how you’d handle concurrency — two cars arriving at the last spot needs a lock or an atomic reservation.

Interview questions · Section 21
Design a parking lot.

First I'd clarify: multiple floors? Multiple vehicle sizes — motorcycle, car, truck? Is payment in scope? Is there a display board showing free spots? I'll assume yes to all except a reservation system.

Entities: ParkingLot (holds floors, is the entry point), Floor (holds spots), ParkingSpot with a SpotType, an abstract Vehicle with Car, Motorcycle, Truck subclasses, Ticket (spot, vehicle, entry time), Payment, and EntryGate/ExitGate.

Behaviour: ParkingLot.park(vehicle) asks a SpotAllocationStrategy for a suitable free spot, marks it occupied and issues a ticket. unpark(ticket) computes the fee via a PricingStrategy, takes payment, frees the spot.

Patterns and why: Strategy for pricing, so adding weekend rates or EV surcharges is a new class rather than an edit to working code; Strategy again for spot allocation (nearest, cheapest); Factory for creating vehicles from a type; Singleton for the lot instance, though I'd note that dependency injection is usually better because a singleton is global state and hurts testing.

Concurrency: two cars can arrive at the last spot simultaneously, so spot assignment must be atomic — a lock on the spot, or a compare-and-set on its status, not a check-then-assign.

Extensibility: if we later add EV spots with charging, that's a new SpotType plus a new pricing strategy, and no existing class changes.

Explain SOLID with examples.

Single responsibility: a class has one reason to change. A Report class that both computes figures and renders PDF has two — a formatting change can break the calculation. Split into ReportCalculator and ReportRenderer.

Open/closed: open to extension, closed to modification. If adding a payment method means editing a growing switch statement, every new method risks breaking existing ones. Instead define a PaymentMethod interface and add a class per method.

Liskov substitution: a subclass must work anywhere the parent does. The textbook violation is Square extends Rectangle: code that sets width and height independently and asserts the area breaks, because a square silently couples them. The relationship isn't really "is-a" in behavioural terms.

Interface segregation: prefer several small interfaces to one large one, so implementers aren't forced to stub methods they don't need — a Robot shouldn't have to implement eat() because it shares a Worker interface with people.

Dependency inversion: depend on abstractions. OrderService should take a PaymentGateway interface, not construct a StripeClient itself — that's what lets me swap providers and inject a fake in tests.

The unifying goal is that a new requirement should mean adding code, not editing code that already works.

When would you use Strategy vs State?

They look identical structurally — both delegate to an interchangeable object — but the intent differs.

Strategy is about choosing how to do something, and the choice is made from outside. The object doesn't change strategies on its own; a caller picks one. Examples: a pricing algorithm, a compression algorithm, a sorting comparator, a route-matching policy. The strategies are peers and don't know about each other.

State is about behaviour that changes with the object's own lifecycle, and the transitions are internal. A vending machine in Idle behaves differently from one in HasMoney, and inserting a coin causes it to transition itself. Crucially, states do know about the transitions to other states.

Quick test: if the answer to "who decides which implementation is active?" is "the client", it's Strategy. If it's "the object itself, based on what has happened to it", it's State. And State's real benefit is replacing a sprawling conditional over a status field with explicit transitions, which makes illegal transitions impossible rather than merely unlikely.

Composition or inheritance?

Prefer composition. Inheritance couples a subclass to its parent's implementation permanently — a change in the base class can break every subclass, hierarchies get deep and rigid, and you can only inherit once in most languages. It also encourages modelling "has-a" relationships as "is-a", which is where Liskov violations come from.

Composition builds behaviour by holding other objects and delegating. It's flexible at runtime — you can swap a collaborator, which inheritance can't do — and it keeps classes small and independently testable. Strategy, Decorator and Adapter are all composition in action.

I'd use inheritance when there is a genuine "is-a" relationship with shared behaviour and the subclass is truly substitutable for the parent — Car and Truck as Vehicle, or extending an abstract base that defines a template method. Even then, I'd favour implementing an interface plus composing shared behaviour over inheriting a concrete class.

Sentence patterns that score

Use these
  • “Before I design, let me confirm the requirements…”
  • “I’m assuming X — please correct me if that’s wrong.”
  • “I’ll choose A over B because of C. The cost is D, and I’m accepting it because…”
  • “The bottleneck here will be X. Let me address that.”
  • “That’s a simplification — in production I’d also need to handle…”
  • “Let me trace one write and one read through this diagram.”
  • “I don’t know that in detail, but here’s how I’d reason about it…”
Never say these
  • “I’ll use Kafka” — with no reason attached.
  • “We’ll just add more servers.”
  • “It’ll scale.” (How? Where does it stop?)
  • “I’d use MongoDB because it’s web-scale.”
  • Silence for ninety seconds while thinking.
  • “That’s how Netflix does it” — as the whole justification.
  • Arguing when the interviewer pushes back — they’re usually hinting.

40 rapid-fire questions · answer each in under 30 seconds
1 · Horizontal vs vertical scaling in one line each?

Vertical = a bigger machine: simple, no code change, hard ceiling, still a single point of failure. Horizontal = more machines: unbounded and redundant, but needs statelessness, a load balancer and distributed data.

2 · What is a reverse proxy?

A server sitting in front of your backends that clients talk to as though it were the application. It does TLS termination, caching, compression, static file serving and request routing, and hides your topology. NGINX is the archetype; a load balancer is a reverse proxy specialised in distribution and health checking.

3 · Why is DNS not enough for load balancing?

Because resolvers cache records for the TTL and some ignore it, so changes take minutes to propagate — far too slow for failover. DNS also can't see server health or current load. Use it for coarse geographic routing, and a real load balancer for per-request decisions.

4 · What is a CDN in one sentence?

A globally distributed network of edge caches that serves your content from a location near the user, cutting latency and removing most bandwidth from your origin.

5 · When does caching make things worse?

When the hit rate is low — you've added a network hop and complexity for nothing; when staleness is unacceptable; when data changes as fast as it's read; and when caching personalised responses at a shared layer leaks one user's data to another.

6 · Redis vs Memcached in one line?

Redis: rich data types, persistence, replication, pub/sub, scripting — use it by default. Memcached: strings only, no persistence, multi-threaded — only for a pure, huge, simple string cache.

7 · What does ACID stand for?

Atomicity (all or nothing), Consistency (constraints preserved), Isolation (concurrent transactions don't corrupt each other), Durability (committed data survives a crash).

8 · What is BASE?

Basically Available, Soft state, Eventually consistent — the NoSQL counterpart to ACID, trading immediate consistency for availability and partition tolerance.

9 · Why not index every column?

Every index must be updated on every write, so write throughput falls roughly linearly with index count, and each index costs disk and memory. Index what your WHERE, JOIN and ORDER BY clauses actually use.

10 · What is the N+1 query problem?

Fetching a list with one query, then issuing one more query per item — 1 + N round trips. Fix with a join, an eager-loading option, or a single batched WHERE id IN (…).

11 · What is a materialised view?

A query result stored as a physical table and refreshed periodically or incrementally. It trades storage and staleness for very fast reads on expensive aggregations — denormalisation the database manages for you.

12 · Difference between a primary key and a clustered index?

A primary key is a uniqueness constraint. A clustered index determines the physical storage order of the rows — there can be only one, and range scans on it are very fast. In InnoDB the primary key is the clustered index; in Postgres they're separate concepts.

13 · What is connection pooling and why does it matter?

Reusing a fixed set of database connections instead of opening one per request. Connections are expensive to establish and each costs the database memory, so thousands of unpooled clients will exhaust it. The pool also acts as a natural concurrency limit.

14 · What breaks when you shard?

Cross-shard joins, cross-shard transactions, global secondary indexes, global auto-increment ids, and ORDER BY across the whole dataset. You also inherit rebalancing and hotspots. Which is why you shard last.

15 · Explain CAP in one sentence.

Since partitions are unavoidable in a distributed system, when one occurs you must choose between refusing requests to stay consistent, or serving possibly-stale data to stay available.

16 · Is Cassandra AP or CP?

AP by default — leaderless, every replica accepts writes, divergence reconciled afterwards. But it's tunable: quorum reads and writes give W + R > N and therefore strong consistency, at the cost of availability.

17 · What is read-your-writes consistency?

A guarantee that a user always sees their own writes, even if others' writes lag. Implemented by routing a user's reads to the leader briefly after they write, or by tracking their write position and requiring a replica at least that current.

18 · What is a vector clock for?

Determining whether two versions of data are causally ordered or genuinely concurrent, without relying on wall-clock time. It lets a system detect real conflicts instead of silently discarding one write as last-write-wins does.

19 · At-least-once vs exactly-once?

At-least-once means messages can be duplicated but never lost — the practical default. Exactly-once delivery is impossible over an unreliable network; exactly-once effect is achieved with at-least-once delivery plus idempotent consumers.

20 · Kafka vs RabbitMQ in one line?

Kafka: a retained, partitioned, replayable log for event streams. RabbitMQ: a broker with rich routing for tasks that should be done once and then deleted.

21 · What is consumer lag?

How far behind the latest message a consumer group is. It's the primary health metric for any queue — growing lag means consumers can't keep up and you'll eventually breach retention and lose data.

22 · What is the outbox pattern for?

Solving the dual-write problem: write the business row and the event row in one local database transaction, then have a relay publish unsent outbox rows. It guarantees the event is published if and only if the data was committed.

23 · Why avoid two-phase commit across services?

It holds locks across network round trips, blocks indefinitely if the coordinator dies after the prepare phase, requires every participant to be up, and most services don't support it. Use a saga instead.

24 · What is a compensating transaction?

A business operation that semantically undoes a completed step in a saga — a refund undoing a charge. It is not a rollback: the original action really happened and may be visible to the user.

25 · How many nodes for a consensus cluster?

An odd number — 3, 5 or 7. An N-node cluster tolerates ⌊(N−1)/2⌋ failures, so 4 nodes tolerate exactly as many failures as 3 while making every write wait for one more acknowledgement.

26 · What is a fencing token?

A monotonically increasing number issued with a lock. Storage rejects any write carrying a token older than the highest it has seen, so a process that resumed after a pause and lost its lock can't corrupt data.

27 · UUID or auto-increment for a primary key?

Auto-increment is compact and index-friendly but is a bottleneck and blocks sharding. UUID v4 needs no coordination but is 128 bits and its randomness fragments B-tree indexes. Prefer Snowflake or UUID v7 — uncoordinated and time-sortable.

28 · What is the thundering herd problem?

Many clients acting simultaneously and overwhelming a resource — most commonly a hot cache key expiring and every request stampeding the database at once. Fix with single-flight locking, stale-while-revalidate, early probabilistic refresh, and TTL jitter.

29 · Token bucket vs leaky bucket?

Token bucket allows bursts up to a capacity while enforcing an average rate — the friendlier default for user-facing APIs. Leaky bucket enforces a perfectly constant output rate — use it to protect a fragile downstream.

30 · What is a circuit breaker?

A wrapper that stops calling a failing dependency after the failure rate crosses a threshold, failing fast instead of blocking threads, then probes for recovery with a single trial request.

31 · Why does a retry need a budget?

Because retries multiply load on a system that is already failing. Capping retries to roughly 10% of traffic prevents a small blip from being amplified into a self-sustaining outage.

32 · What is graceful degradation?

Shipping a reduced experience instead of an error when a dependency fails — showing bestsellers when the recommendation service is down. The fallback should be decided per feature in advance, not improvised during an incident.

33 · What is a bulkhead?

Isolating resources per dependency — separate thread or connection pools — so a flood to one downstream can't consume everything and take down unrelated features. Named after a ship's watertight compartments.

34 · Monolith or microservices for a 5-person team?

Monolith, modularised by domain. Microservices solve an organisational problem you don't have yet, and cost you distributed transactions, tracing, discovery and N deployment pipelines.

35 · What is a distributed monolith?

Services split apart but still tightly coupled — sharing a database, deployed together, every feature touching five repos. You pay all the costs of distribution and keep all the constraints of a monolith. The worst of both.

36 · What are the four golden signals?

Latency, traffic, errors and saturation. Measure latency as percentiles and separate successful from failed requests, since fast errors flatter the average.

37 · What should you never alert on?

Causes rather than symptoms — CPU at 80%, a pod restart, a queue that's non-empty. Alert on things users feel and that are tied to your SLO, and make every alert actionable with a runbook.

38 · Blue-green vs canary in one line?

Blue-green: two full environments, switch all traffic at once, instant rollback. Canary: shift traffic gradually while watching metrics, so the blast radius stays tiny.

39 · How do you rename a database column with zero downtime?

You don't rename it. Expand–contract: add the new column, backfill in batches, dual-write, switch reads, verify, and drop the old column days later once no running code touches it.

40 · What is an error budget?

100% minus your SLO — roughly 43 minutes a month at 99.9%. While budget remains you ship aggressively; when it's exhausted you freeze features and spend the time on reliability. It converts a values argument into arithmetic.
